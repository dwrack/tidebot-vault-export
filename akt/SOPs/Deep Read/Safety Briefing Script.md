# Safety Briefing Script
## Austin Kayak Tours

### How to Use This

This isn't something you read word-for-word to guests. It's the content you need to internalize so you can deliver it naturally, in your own voice, before every single trip. Hit every point, but make it feel like a conversation, not a legal disclaimer.

The whole briefing should take 3-5 minutes. Longer than that and you lose them. Shorter than that and you're skipping something important.

### The Briefing

**PFDs stay on the entire time.** Not around your waist, not unclipped, not "I'll put it on if I need it." On, buckled, snug. If it rides up to your ears when you raise your arms, it's too loose and we need to adjust it. This is the one rule that's completely non-negotiable.

**These are sit-on-top kayaks. They don't sink.** If you fall off (and it happens to everyone at some point), the kayak will be right there floating next to you. Don't panic. Grab the side, pull yourself back on, or hold on and I'll come help you. The water in Lady Bird Lake is generally calm, but if you do go in, stay with your boat.

**Stay together as a group.** I'll be leading from the front and checking back regularly. Don't paddle ahead of me and don't fall too far behind. If you need to stop, signal me and we'll pause the whole group. Nobody paddles alone.

**Wildlife is part of the experience, but keep your distance.** We share the lake with turtles, herons, sometimes an egret or two. Look, don't touch. Don't feed them. If we're out for a bat tour, the bats will come out on their own schedule. Keep your paddles still when they're flying and enjoy the show.

**If you hear thunder or see lightning, we're getting off the water immediately.** I'll direct everyone to shore. Follow my instructions and paddle directly toward where I'm pointing. Lightning on open water is extremely dangerous and we take zero chances with it.

**Paddle technique in 30 seconds:** Hold the paddle with both hands, shoulder-width apart, knuckles facing up. Reach forward, plant the blade, pull back to your hip, lift out, repeat on the other side. Alternating sides keeps you going straight. To turn, paddle only on one side. If you need to stop, just stop paddling and lay the paddle across your lap.

**Signals:** If I blow my whistle once, look at me for direction. If I blow it three times, that's an emergency and I need everyone to stop paddling and listen. If you need help, wave your paddle overhead and I'll come to you.

**A few housekeeping items:** Your phone is welcome but put it in a dry bag or waterproof case. Lady Bird Lake has claimed more phones than I can count. Sunscreen and water are your friends, especially on afternoon trips. There are restrooms at the launch site, so use them before we go out.

**The combined weight limit for tandem kayaks is 450 lbs.** If we flagged this during check-in and moved anyone to singles or SUPs, that's why. It's about the kayak's performance and your comfort on the water, nothing else.

### Key Points Checklist

Guides should be able to hit all of these from memory:

- PFDs on, buckled, the entire time
- Sit-on-top kayaks float, you can't sink them
- Stay together, don't outpace the group
- Wildlife: look don't touch, don't feed
- Lightning: off the water immediately, no debate
- Basic paddle technique: grip, stroke, turning
- Whistle signals: one blast = attention, three = emergency
- Paddle overhead = "I need help"
- Phone in dry bag
- Tandem weight limit: 450 lbs
- Restrooms before launch
- Bat tour specifics (if applicable): stay still, quiet, enjoy

### Delivery Tips

Make eye contact. If someone's looking at their phone during the briefing, pause and wait. Everyone needs to hear this.

Use humor where it fits naturally. "The lake has a great phone collection" lands better than "please secure your electronic devices." But don't joke about the safety-critical stuff. PFDs and lightning are serious.

Read the group. A group of experienced paddlers needs the safety points but not the paddle technique tutorial. First-timers need everything, delivered patiently. Adjust your depth, not your coverage.

For bat tours, add context about timing ("The bats usually emerge about 20 minutes after sunset"), noise ("They're sensitive to sound, so we'll go quiet when they start coming out"), and light ("No flash photography, it disorients them").
