**Q1:**
```
Does sauna help with muscle recovery after a workout?
```
**A1:**
```
It can. Heat increases blood flow to sore muscles and triggers heat shock proteins that help repair exercise-damaged tissue. A 2023 study in Biology of Sport (Ahokas et al.) found post-exercise infrared sauna use improved next-day muscle soreness and neuromuscular performance after resistance training. Research is mixed on traditional high-heat saunas specifically, and heat exposure adds its own physical stress, so more isn't automatically better. Most people notice looser muscles and better sleep, not a soreness cure.
```

**Q2:**
```
How long should you cold plunge after a workout for muscle soreness?
```
**A2:**
```
Research on cold water immersion for delayed-onset muscle soreness (DOMS) points to 10-15 minutes at a moderately cold temperature, roughly 50-59°F, done within an hour of exercise, as the sweet spot for reducing next-day soreness. The Columbia runs colder than that in winter (around 42°F), so most guests do shorter rounds, a minute or two at a time, rather than one long continuous soak.
```

**Q3:**
```
Is it bad to cold plunge right after strength training?
```
**A3:**
```
If your main goal is building muscle size and strength, maybe. A well-known 2015 study in The Journal of Physiology (Roberts et al.) found that regular cold-water immersion immediately after resistance training blunted long-term strength and muscle gains compared to active recovery. If your goal is endurance recovery, feeling less beat up before your next run or ride, that concern matters much less. Know which goal you're training for before you time your plunge.
```

**Q4:**
```
What is contrast therapy and does it work for runners?
```
**A4:**
```
Contrast therapy means alternating heat and cold, sauna then cold plunge, to move blood in and out of muscle tissue. Runners and endurance athletes use it to feel less stiff and recover faster between hard training days. It won't repair an injury or replace rest, but as a recovery habit between long runs, it's well-suited to the kind of week-over-week fatigue marathon and ultra training creates.
```

Somebody in Willie McBride's 6:30 AM group is always limping a little. ## Does a sauna actually help after a workout?

Some, yes, with real limits worth knowing.

When your core temperature rises in the heat, your cells produce heat shock proteins, HSP70 in particular, which act like molecular repair crews for exercise-damaged muscle tissue. Regular heat exposure has also been linked to lower C-reactive protein, a marker of the inflammation that shows up as next-day stiffness. A 2023 study in [Biology of Sport](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10286597/) (Ahokas et al.) found a single post-exercise infrared sauna session improved neuromuscular performance and reduced soreness after resistance training, compared to a no-sauna control group.

Worth being straight about: most of that research used infrared saunas, not the higher-heat wood or propane sauna you'll find at Ebb & Ember (ours runs 180-190°F, wet or dry depending on the round). And not every study agrees. Some researchers argue post-exercise heat is itself a physical stressor, adding load onto a body that's already fatigued, which can work against recovery instead of for it. We're not going to tell you the sauna definitely speeds up your next race. What guests tell us, consistently, is that they sleep better and feel looser the next morning. That's a real outcome. It's just not the same claim as "cures soreness."

## What a cold plunge really does for sore muscles

The research here is more specific than the sauna side, and it comes with a dosage.

A 2025 network meta-analysis in [Frontiers in Physiology](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2025.1525726/full) looked at different cold water immersion protocols for exercise-induced muscle damage and found medium-duration, medium-temperature immersion, about 10-15 minutes at 50-59°F, worked best for reducing delayed-onset muscle soreness (DOMS) within the first 24 hours. Past 24 hours, the effect mostly disappears. So: it helps you feel less wrecked tomorrow morning. It doesn't change your recovery timeline a week out.

The Columbia doesn't hold at 50-59°F most of the year. Winter sits around 42°F, genuinely intense, cold enough that most guests, athletes included, do short rounds off the ladder rather than one long soak. Summer runs a mellower 60-65°F, closer to the research protocol and easier to hold for longer. Either way, the plunge is optional every single time. Some guests do three rounds. Some go waist-deep and call it good. Some skip the river and use the Portland Downpour bucket tower for a fast, controlled dose of cold instead. None of that is the wrong way to do it.

It's still a real river, not a tub, so we treat it like one: two ladders off the dock, throw rings on deck, life jackets available, and nobody goes in alone. Our one rule stays the same for a marathoner as it does for a first-timer: when in doubt, don't go out. Cardio fitness doesn't override that.

## The part cold-plunge marketing usually skips

Here's the thing most cold plunge content won't tell you, because it complicates the sales pitch: cold water immersion isn't free of tradeoffs.

A well-cited 2015 study in *The Journal of Physiology* (Roberts et al.) found that lifters who did cold water immersion immediately after strength training, session after session, ended up with smaller long-term gains in muscle size and strength compared to lifters who did active recovery instead. The likely mechanism: cold blunts some of the inflammatory signaling your body actually uses to build muscle back bigger.

If you're chasing hypertrophy, that's worth knowing. If you're a marathoner, triathlete, or ultra runner trying to survive a training block without your legs giving out, that tradeoff barely applies. Muscle growth isn't the goal. Showing up to Tuesday's run without your quads staging a revolt is. That's the actual population the Columbia works best for, and it's most of who shows up to the dock at 6:30 AM.

## A simple post-workout protocol to try

You don't need the full series to use heat and cold for recovery. Here's roughly what a session looks like if you're coming straight off a hard training day:

1. **Heat first, 15-20 minutes.** Long enough to sweat properly, not so long you feel drained walking out. Wet or dry, whichever round you land in.
2. **Cold second, dose to your comfort.** A minute or two on the ladder if it's your first time, longer once your body's used to it. Skip it entirely if that's the day you're having. Nobody tracks who went in and who didn't.
3. **Deck time after, don't skip this part.** Sit, hydrate, let your heart rate settle. This is where a lot of the actual benefit happens and where people rush the least effectively.
4. **Repeat once if your session allows.** A full Ebb & Ember session runs 1 hour 45 minutes, long enough for two full rounds if you want them.

Bring water. Bring a towel. Wear whatever you'd swim in. That's the whole gear list.
