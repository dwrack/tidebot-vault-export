# Safety & Compliance Quiz — Ebb and Ember Floating Saunas

*Pass: 80% (8 of 10 correct). Re-read the Safety & Compliance Overview if you score below 80%.*

### Q1. A guest mentions during health screening that they take blood pressure medication. What do you do?

A) Let them in, the medication is controlling their condition
B) Explain that some blood pressure medications combined with sauna heat can cause dangerous drops in blood pressure. Recommend they check with their doctor before doing a sauna session. If they insist, they don't enter without medical clearance.
C) Let them in for a shorter session
D) Lower the sauna temperature for them

**Correct: B**
BP meds + extreme heat = potential for a sudden, dangerous BP drop. You're not their doctor and you can't assess medication interactions. What you can do is inform them of the risk and require medical clearance. Lowering the temperature doesn't eliminate the risk. A shorter session doesn't eliminate the risk. Medical clearance does.

### Q2. What's the difference between heat exhaustion and heat stroke?

A) They're the same thing, just different severity
B) Heat exhaustion: heavy sweating, pale/clammy skin, weak pulse, nausea, dizziness. Treatable on-site with cooling, hydration, and rest. Heat stroke: body temperature 103F+, hot/red/dry skin (sweating may have stopped), strong pulse, confusion, potential loss of consciousness. Heat stroke is a 911 emergency.
C) Heat exhaustion means you're dehydrated, heat stroke means you're overheated
D) Heat stroke only happens outdoors in the sun

**Correct: B**
The critical difference: heat exhaustion is your body struggling to cool itself. Heat stroke is your body failing to cool itself. The biggest red flag for heat stroke is confusion or altered mental state. If someone in the sauna becomes confused, slurs their words, or becomes unresponsive, that's 911 immediately. Do not wait, do not try to handle it yourself.

### Q3. A programming partner's Certificate of Liability Insurance names Ebb & Ember as an Additional Insured but does NOT list Elevated Tides LLC. Can they run their session?

A) Yes, they have E&E covered which is enough
B) No. Both Ebb & Ember Floating Saunas AND Elevated Tides LLC must be named as Additional Insureds. The cert needs to be updated before any session takes place.
C) Yes, but only for this session while they update the cert
D) Ask management, it depends on the situation

**Correct: B**
Both entities. Every time. This isn't flexible. If only one is named and an incident occurs, the uncovered entity is exposed. The fix is usually simple: the partner's insurer adds the second entity. But it must be done before they're on-site with guests.

### Q4. Mid-session, a guest says they feel "a little dizzy" but wants to stay in the sauna. What do you do?

A) Let them stay, they know their limits
B) Get them out of the sauna. Dizziness during heat exposure is an early warning sign of heat exhaustion. Move them to the cooling area, give them water, monitor. If symptoms improve after 10-15 minutes, they can potentially do another round at a lower intensity. If symptoms don't improve, the session is over for them.
C) Open the sauna door to let cool air in
D) Give them water and let them stay

**Correct: B**
Dizziness is your body sending a clear signal. "I want to stay" doesn't override "my body is telling me something." The guest might be disappointed for 5 minutes, but you prevented a potential medical situation. Once they're cooled down and feeling better, they usually thank you. Getting them water while leaving them in the heat just slows the problem, it doesn't solve it.

### Q5. You're inspecting the entry/exit ladder before a session and find a bolt is loose. Session starts in 45 minutes. What do you do?

A) It's a little loose, it'll hold for today
B) Tighten or repair the bolt immediately. If you can't fix it securely in time, the session cannot proceed. A loose ladder with wet, heat-fatigued guests transitioning between sauna and water is an accident waiting to happen. Contact management if you need maintenance support.
C) Put a "use caution" sign on it
D) Have guests enter the water a different way

**Correct: B**
The ladder is the single most-used piece of safety equipment in a sauna session. Wet feet, elevated body temperature, reduced coordination from heat. A loose ladder becomes a broken ladder becomes a head injury. Fix it, replace it, or don't run the session.

### Q6. A contractor wants to bring their assistant to help with a wellness programming session. The assistant doesn't have their own insurance. The contractor says "they're covered under mine." What do you do?

A) Take their word for it
B) Require written confirmation from the contractor's insurer that the assistant is covered under their policy. "They're covered under mine" is not verification. If they can't produce written confirmation, the assistant doesn't participate.
C) Let the assistant help since the main contractor is insured
D) Have the assistant sign a waiver instead

**Correct: B**
Verbal assurances aren't insurance. You need written confirmation from the actual insurer. This isn't about trust. It's about legal coverage. If the assistant causes an incident and isn't actually covered, both E&E and Elevated Tides are exposed. A phone call or email to the insurer takes 10 minutes.

### Q7. The fire extinguisher's pressure gauge shows it's in the yellow zone (between green and red). What do you do?

A) Yellow is fine, only red is a problem
B) Flag it to management. Yellow means the extinguisher is losing pressure and may not perform properly in an emergency. Schedule a replacement or professional inspection. Continue the session only if you have a backup extinguisher available.
C) Shake it to redistribute the pressure
D) Replace it after the busy weekend

**Correct: B**
Yellow zone means the extinguisher is degrading. It might work, it might not, and you don't want to find out during an actual fire next to a heating element on a floating platform. Flag it, get it addressed. If you have no backup, you have a harder decision to make, but "it's probably fine" isn't a fire safety plan.

### Q8. A guest arrives smelling of alcohol. They're not falling-down drunk, but they've clearly been drinking. What do you do?

A) Let them in, they seem functional
B) Have a direct conversation. Alcohol impairs thermoregulation and judgment, both critical in a sauna. If they've had "a drink or two," use your judgment on whether they can safely participate with shortened sessions and increased monitoring. If they're noticeably impaired, they don't enter the sauna.
C) Refuse them entirely, zero tolerance
D) Let them do a shorter session

**Correct: B**
This is a judgment call, not a zero-tolerance situation. One glass of wine at lunch is different from three cocktails an hour ago. The key question is: can this person reliably tell you if they feel unwell, and can they safely use the ladder and transition between hot and cold? If yes, proceed with extra vigilance. If there's any doubt, they sit this one out. Err on the side of caution.

### Q9. During a session, a guest who disclosed no health issues during screening suddenly clutches their chest and says they feel pressure. What do you do?

A) Give them water and have them rest outside the sauna
B) Get them out of the sauna immediately. Call 911. This could be a cardiac event. Do not wait to see if it improves. Have them sit or lie in a comfortable position, keep them calm, monitor their breathing and consciousness. Begin CPR if they become unresponsive and stop breathing. Stay on the line with 911.
C) Ask them if they have a history of heart problems
D) Give them aspirin

**Correct: B**
Chest pressure in a heat-stressed environment is a potential cardiac emergency. You are not qualified to diagnose it, and the stakes of guessing wrong are fatal. 911, immediately. Everything else is secondary. The health screening exists to catch known conditions, but first-time cardiac events happen to people with no history. That's why you have CPR certification.

### Q10. How often should active contractor insurance certificates be audited?

A) Once, at onboarding
B) Quarterly. Insurance policies renew and lapse. A contractor who was covered 6 months ago may not be covered today. Quarterly audits catch gaps before they become liability exposure.
C) Annually, when the business renews its own insurance
D) Only when a contractor's scope changes

**Correct: B**
Quarterly. Policies expire, get cancelled, or lapse for non-payment. The contractor may not even know their coverage lapsed. A 5-minute check every quarter prevents a catastrophic discovery after an incident. Mark it on your compliance calendar and don't skip it.
