# Guest Experience Quiz — Ebb and Ember Floating Saunas

*Pass: 80% (8 of 10 correct). Re-read the Role Guide if you score below 80%.*

---

### Q1. A guest with an undisclosed heart condition starts sweating profusely, looks pale, and says they feel "weird" 12 minutes into the sauna. What do you do?

A) Give them water and open the sauna door
B) Get them out of the sauna immediately. Move them to a cool area, provide water, assess symptoms. Ask directly: "Do you have any heart conditions or take any medications you didn't mention earlier?" If symptoms don't improve quickly or worsen, call 911. Document everything.
C) Have them lie down inside the sauna
D) Wait 5 minutes to see if it passes

**Correct: B**
Get them out first, investigate second. Pale skin and profuse sweating in a sauna = heat exhaustion at minimum. The undisclosed condition question matters for 911 dispatch if it comes to that. Don't wait to see if it passes. Heat-related illness escalates, it doesn't resolve on its own in a hot environment.

### Q2. A guest is pushing through obvious discomfort, saying "I paid for this, I'm staying in." They're red-faced and breathing hard at 18 minutes. What do you do?

A) Respect their choice, they're an adult
B) Override them. "I hear you, and I want you to get the full experience. But right now your body needs a cool-down break. That's actually how the sauna works best, in rounds. Let's step out, cool down, and you'll feel amazing going back in."
C) Set a timer and let them stay 2 more minutes
D) Turn down the temperature

**Correct: B**
The guest's desire to stay doesn't override your safety judgment. Frame it as enhancing their experience, not cutting it short. "Rounds" are how saunas actually work: heat, cool, heat, cool. You're not taking something away. You're giving them the real experience. If they still refuse and their symptoms are concerning, you make the call for them.

### Q3. A couple books a session and one of them is visibly pregnant (third trimester). She wasn't the one who booked and wasn't asked during the booking process. What do you do at check-in?

A) Let her in, she knows her body
B) During the health screening, when you ask about pregnancy, address it directly and kindly. Explain that elevated core temperature during pregnancy carries real risks, especially to fetal development. She cannot do the sauna session without written medical clearance from her OB. Offer to reschedule for after delivery or have her relax dockside while her partner does the session.
C) Lower the temperature and let her do a short session
D) Ask her to sign an extra waiver

**Correct: B**
Pregnancy is a hard contraindication. No temperature adjustment or shortened session makes it safe. Elevated core temperature can cause neural tube defects and other developmental problems. Be kind but firm: "I completely understand, and I'm sorry for the surprise. The heat is genuinely risky during pregnancy and I wouldn't feel right letting you in without your OB signing off. Your partner can absolutely still do the session, and you're welcome to hang out dockside."

### Q4. A group of 4 friends books a session. One person is clearly the reluctant one, dragged along by the group. They're hanging back, arms crossed, not engaging. How do you handle them?

A) Focus on the enthusiastic ones, the reluctant one will come around
B) Give them space but include them. During the briefing, make eye contact and address them specifically: "If at any point you want to just hang out outside the sauna and enjoy the water, that's totally fine." Normalize opting out. Often the reluctant guest just needs to know they have an exit.
C) Pull them aside and ask what's wrong
D) Ignore them, they'll either warm up to it or not

**Correct: B**
Reluctant guests usually aren't resistant to the experience. They're resistant to feeling trapped in it. The moment they know they can step out without judgment, they relax. Some end up loving it. Some sit dockside and have a great time anyway. Either way, they leave happy because they felt respected, not pressured.

### Q5. A programming partner's yoga class gets a complaint from a guest afterward: "The instructor kept pushing us to hold poses longer than was comfortable." What do you do?

A) Pass the complaint to the instructor and move on
B) Report it to management with the specific guest feedback. This touches on guest safety (forcing physical limits in a heat environment) and brand alignment. Management needs to address it with the instructor before the next session. The programming activation walkthrough should have caught this, so it's also a process question.
C) Tell the guest that's just how yoga works
D) Drop the instructor immediately

**Correct: B**
This is a safety and brand issue, not just a style preference. Pushing physical limits in a heated environment increases injury and heat-related illness risk. Management (David and Jonah) need to know. The fix might be coaching the instructor on adapting their approach for the sauna environment, not necessarily dropping them. But it needs to be addressed before the next session.

### Q6. A guest complains about the price after the session, saying "it wasn't worth what I paid." How do you respond?

A) Offer a partial refund on the spot
B) Listen without getting defensive. "I appreciate you telling me. Can you share what fell short of your expectations? I want to make sure we're delivering something worth your time." Then pass the feedback to management. You don't have authority to issue refunds, but showing you care goes a long way.
C) Explain all the costs that go into running the operation
D) Tell them to leave a review with their feedback

**Correct: B**
Defensive responses confirm the guest's feeling that the business doesn't care. Listening and asking what specifically fell short gives you actionable information and makes the guest feel heard. Often the complaint isn't really about price. It's about unmet expectations. Understanding what they expected helps the business adjust messaging, not pricing.

### Q7. Two guests in the same session are making other guests uncomfortable with loud, disruptive behavior. What do you do?

A) Let it go, different people enjoy things differently
B) Address it calmly and privately. "Hey, I love the energy, but a few folks are here for a quieter experience. Mind if we keep it a little more mellow?" If they continue, be more direct: "I need the volume to come down for the group." The sauna is a shared space and everyone's experience matters.
C) Separate them from the group
D) End the session early for everyone

**Correct: B**
One conversation usually fixes it. People often don't realize they're being disruptive. Private, calm, direct. If they escalate or refuse, you have the authority to ask them to leave. But most of the time, "hey, mind keeping it down?" does the job.

### Q8. A guest asks if they can bring their own essential oils to add to the sauna. What do you say?

A) Sure, the more the merrier
B) "I appreciate the thought, but we keep the sauna scent-free for a few reasons: other guests may have allergies or sensitivities, some oils can damage the heating equipment, and we want to control what goes into the space for safety. If you'd like, I can look into options we can offer officially."
C) Only if they're natural oils
D) Let them add a few drops, it won't hurt

**Correct: B**
Scent-free is the default because you can't predict allergies, sensitivities, or reactions in a heated environment. Some essential oils are also flammable or can damage surfaces and equipment. Guest-supplied products are uncontrolled variables. If there's demand, management can evaluate approved options for future sessions.

### Q9. A guest has a wonderful session and asks if they can book a private after-hours session for a small group next month. How do you handle it?

A) Book it yourself, you know the schedule
B) "That sounds great, I'd love to help make that happen. Let me connect you with management so they can set up the details, timing, and pricing for a private booking." You don't book custom sessions yourself since pricing, availability, and logistics need management input.
C) Tell them private sessions aren't available
D) Give them your personal number to arrange it

**Correct: B**
Custom bookings go through management. You're the face of the experience, but pricing, scheduling, and private event logistics are above your pay grade. Connect them warmly, don't just redirect them. "Let me get you in touch with the right person" is better than "that's not my department."

### Q10. At the end of a session, what's the best way to close the experience?

A) "Thanks, have a good night"
B) Thank them specifically for something from the session ("the way you handled that cold plunge was impressive"). Remind them to keep hydrating for the rest of the evening. Mention that a Google review means a lot to a small operation. Walk them out rather than waving from the dock.
C) Hand them a review card and start cleaning
D) Offer them a discount on their next booking

**Correct: B**
The ending is what people remember. A specific, genuine thank-you. A practical reminder (hydration). A low-pressure review ask. Walking them out rather than immediately pivoting to cleanup. These small things are the difference between "nice sauna" and "incredible experience, you have to try this place."
