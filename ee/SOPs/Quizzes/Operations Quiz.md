### Q1. You arrive to set up for a session and the sauna heater won't ignite. Session starts in 40 minutes. What do you do?

A) Keep trying, it usually works after a few attempts
B) Try the standard troubleshooting steps (check fuel/power, pilot light, ventilation). If it doesn't resolve in 10 minutes, contact management immediately. The session may need to be cancelled or rescheduled. Communicate with booked guests as early as possible so they're not driving out for nothing.
C) Run the session without the sauna, just do a cold plunge experience
D) Borrow a space heater from somewhere

**Correct: B**
10 minutes of troubleshooting is reasonable. After that, you need management involved because the clock is ticking on guest communication. A 40-minute window means you have maybe 20 minutes to resolve it before you need to start contacting guests. Don't wait until they show up to tell them the sauna isn't working.

### Q2. A programming partner's insurance certificate expired 2 weeks ago. They have a session scheduled for tomorrow and say the renewal is "in process." What do you do?

A) Let them run the session, it's just paperwork
B) They cannot run the session without a current certificate. No exceptions. Contact management. The partner needs to produce proof of renewed coverage before they're back on-site with guests. "In process" is not the same as "active."
C) Have them sign a personal liability waiver
D) Let them do one more session while it gets sorted

**Correct: B**
Expired insurance = no insurance. "In process" means the coverage has lapsed and may or may not be retroactively effective depending on the policy. You can't take that risk with guests on-site. It's not personal. It's a hard requirement that protects everyone, including the partner.

### Q3. During the Programming Activation walkthrough (private evaluation session), David and Jonah disagree on whether to approve a new meditation instructor. David wants to go ahead, Jonah has concerns about quality. What happens?

A) David's the owner, his vote counts more
B) The Go/No-Go decision requires alignment. If there's disagreement, the decision is documented along with both perspectives, and the concerns are addressed before launch. This might mean another walkthrough, specific adjustments, or a trial period with built-in review points.
C) Approve it with conditions
D) Reject it and move on

**Correct: B**
The walkthrough process exists so that these decisions are made before guests are involved. Disagreement doesn't mean rejection. It means the concerns need resolution. Maybe Jonah's quality concerns can be addressed with coaching. Maybe another walkthrough after adjustments resolves it. The point is: don't launch with unresolved concerns between decision-makers.

### Q4. You're between sessions and discover you're down to 3 clean towels. Next session has 6 guests. What do you do?

A) Reuse some from the previous session
B) Contact management immediately. You need more towels before the next session. Check if there's backup stock, laundry that can be turned around fast, or towels that can be sourced locally. If you can't get enough, management may need to adjust the session. Never reuse towels between guests.
C) Give towels to the first 3 guests and tell the others you ran out
D) Use smaller hand towels as substitutes

**Correct: B**
Towel shortage is a setup problem, not a session problem. Solve it before guests arrive. Reusing towels is a hygiene and brand issue. Running out in front of guests damages the premium experience. Flag it early, solve it, and figure out why the restocking process failed so it doesn't happen again.

### Q5. The dock surface is wet and slightly slippery from morning dew. First session is in 30 minutes. What do you do?

A) Guests will be careful, it's fine
B) Dry the dock surface or lay down non-slip mats. Check the ladder for grip. Wet dock + guests transitioning from hot sauna to cold water = fall risk. This takes 10 minutes to address and prevents the most common injury scenario.
C) Put up a "wet floor" sign
D) Have guests take their shoes off for better grip

**Correct: B**
Wet surfaces are the number one slip hazard in this operation. Guests coming out of a sauna are heat-flushed with reduced coordination. A "wet floor" sign doesn't prevent a fall. Drying the surface or adding non-slip material does. This is a pre-session checklist item for exactly this reason.

### Q6. A new Programming Activation proposal comes in: an instructor wants to run hot yoga sessions in the sauna space. What's your initial assessment before passing it to David and Jonah?

A) Sounds great, yoga and saunas are a natural fit
B) Flag potential concerns: hot yoga in a sauna pushes heat exposure significantly beyond normal sauna levels. Guests doing physical exertion in extreme heat have elevated risk of heat-related illness, dehydration, and cardiovascular stress. The insurance requirements may need to be more specific for this activity. Worth evaluating, but the safety bar is higher than for a meditation or stretching class.
C) Reject it, too risky
D) Let the instructor decide if it's safe, they know yoga

**Correct: B**
Your job is to flag risks, not make final decisions. Hot yoga in a sauna combines two heat sources (ambient sauna heat + exercise-generated body heat) in a way that dramatically increases risk. That doesn't mean automatic rejection, but it means the evaluation needs to be thorough: modified temperatures, shorter sessions, stricter hydration protocols, higher insurance coverage. The instructor's yoga expertise doesn't equal sauna safety expertise.

### Q7. Post-Activation review for a fitness series shows: 4 sessions completed, average 3 guests per session, 2 minor complaints about intensity, revenue barely covers costs. What's your recommendation?

A) Keep it going, it'll grow
B) Present the data honestly to management. Low attendance plus complaints about intensity suggests the product-market fit isn't there in its current form. Options: adjust the programming (lower intensity, different time slots, better marketing), give it 2 more sessions with changes, or sunset it and try something different. The walkthrough should have caught the intensity issue.
C) Cut it immediately
D) Double the marketing budget

**Correct: B**
Data drives the decision. 3 guests per session with complaints isn't a marketing problem. It's a product problem. Maybe the intensity needs adjustment for the sauna environment. Maybe the time slots are wrong. Maybe this type of programming doesn't fit the brand. Present options, let management decide, but don't throw good money after bad without changing something.

### Q8. You finish a session and realize you forgot to complete the health screening for one of the guests. The session went fine, no incidents. What do you do?

A) No harm done, skip the paperwork
B) Document the miss in your session notes and report it to management. This is a process failure even though nothing went wrong this time. If that guest had an undisclosed heart condition and had a cardiac event, you'd have zero documentation that you screened them. Identify why you missed it (rushed? Group dynamics? Forgot?) and fix the process.
C) Fill in the screening form after the fact
D) Call the guest and do it over the phone

**Correct: B**
A near-miss is still a process failure. The outcome was fine, but the system broke. Documenting it and understanding why prevents the next time from being the time something actually goes wrong. Filling in forms after the fact is falsification. Calling the guest after is awkward and pointless. Own it, fix the process.

### Q9. The weather forecast shows thunderstorms clearing by 2pm. Your session is at 3pm. At 2:30pm, skies are clear but the dock is still wet from rain. What do you do?

A) Run the session, storms are over
B) Storms cleared, so the lightning cancel condition is no longer active. But: check the dock for safety (dry it or add non-slip material since it's wet from rain), verify the sauna equipment wasn't affected by the storm, and confirm with guests that the session is on. The 30-minute post-lightning rule applies: if the last lightning was at 1:45pm, you're clear by 2:15pm.
C) Cancel out of caution
D) Wait until 3pm exactly to make the call

**Correct: B**
Post-storm operations require a site assessment. The weather danger has passed (30-minute rule), but the aftermath needs attention. Wet dock, potential equipment issues, guest uncertainty about whether the session is happening. A quick assessment and confirmation call to guests shows professionalism and keeps the booking.

### Q10. End of season: management asks you to document what worked and what didn't about the sauna operations this year. What's most valuable to include?

A) Revenue numbers and session counts
B) Specific operational lessons: what guest complaints you heard repeatedly, which programming activations worked vs. didn't, equipment issues that kept coming up, health screening edge cases you encountered, weather patterns that affected scheduling, and suggestions for next season based on what you actually experienced. Revenue is management's job. Your perspective from the ground is what they can't get from a spreadsheet.
C) A list of things you'd like changed
D) Guest testimonials

**Correct: B**
Your ground-level perspective is irreplaceable. Management has the numbers. What they don't have is the pattern recognition from being there every session: the guest who almost had a problem because of a gap in the screening questions, the programming partner whose sessions always ran over time, the dock section that got dangerously slippery every time it rained. That's the stuff that makes next season better.
