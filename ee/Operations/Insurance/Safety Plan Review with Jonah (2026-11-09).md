## 1. The rule we are writing to

The plan is a two-sided document. To Capitol it is a representation: a line that is false when they rely on it is a misrepresentation they can use against a claim. To a plaintiff's lawyer it is our own standard of care: every "we do X weekly" becomes the yardstick, and a missed week is negligence by our own definition.

So the test for every line is:

1. **Rules for guests**: keep. They cost nothing and they feed comparative fault (guest broke a posted rule).
2. **Claims about what exists on site**: keep only if a photo proves it today.
3. **Promises about what we do and how often**: keep only if there is a log that proves it, and write the lowest frequency we will actually hit ("at least").
4. **Anything that reads as a guarantee** ("ensures," "monitored," "always," "every guest signs"): cut or soften.

Oregon points that change the strategy (Todd Key to confirm):
- **Releases are weaker here.** Bagley v. Mt. Bachelor (Or. 2014) threw out a recreational release as unconscionable. Ours may hold, but it cannot be the only shield. Offering a real choice (a price with and without release, or a plain "sign or don't enter") helps enforceability, but see decision 7 below, it collides with the insurance condition.
- **Implied assumption of risk is gone** (ORS 31.620). The guest "knew the river was cold" is not a defense by itself. What works is **comparative fault** (ORS 31.600): posted rule, guest broke it, damages cut. That is the legal job of the signs, and why the warnings matter more than the pretty signs.
- **Unstaffed site**: we cannot claim supervision, so the whole structure is warn, equip, inspect, log. Never write "we monitor."
