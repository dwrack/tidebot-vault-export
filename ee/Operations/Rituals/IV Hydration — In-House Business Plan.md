# IV Hydration: In-House Business Plan

*Research draft, Oct 7 2026. Not legal advice. Anything we'd actually do here gets a one-hour call with an Oregon health care attorney first.*

## Bottom line

**Partner. Don't build it in-house.**

- E&E legally **can't own or control** an IV business in Oregon. IV hydration is the practice of medicine here, and Oregon's corporate-practice rules (tightened by SB 951, in force Jan 1 2026 for new arrangements) bar a lay company like Elevated Tides LLC from owning a medical practice or controlling its staffing, schedules, or clinical calls.
- The only legal "in-house" version is a separate professional corporation owned by an Oregon physician or NP, renting space from us at arm's length. With E&E having no control, that's just a partner with more paperwork and more of our money at risk.
- The math doesn't work at our volume anyway. We'd need roughly **30+ drips a month** to clear fixed costs. Realistic demand off 8-9 weekend mornings is more like 8-16.

Partner model: $0 upfront, partner carries the license, malpractice and medical waste, and we still sell the $79 ritual ticket. Revisit only if a partner is consistently doing 30+ drips a month on our float.

## 1. Legal structure

### IV hydration is medicine
The Oregon Medical Board says flatly that IV hydration therapy is the practice of medicine under ORS 677.085 and must meet the same standard of care as any other treatment. Medical directors can't hand off diagnosing, consent, prescribing, treating or documenting to anyone not licensed and trained for it, and can't issue standing orders that let unqualified staff practice. ([OMB statement, adopted Oct 3 2024](https://www.oregon.gov/omb/board/Philosophy/Pages/IV-Hydration-Therapy.aspx))

### Corporate practice of medicine (the main hurdle)
- **ORS 58.375:** a professional corporation practicing medicine must have Oregon-licensed physicians holding the majority of voting shares and a majority of the board, with officers (other than secretary/treasurer) also physicians. Later amendments let NPs share in some structures. ([ORS 58.376](https://oregon.public.law/statutes/ors_58.376), [HB 2465 history](https://apps.oregonlegislature.gov/liz/2017R1/Downloads/MeasureDocument/HB2465))
- **SB 951 (signed June 9 2025):** the strictest CPOM law in the country. A management company (that'd be us) can't own or control a majority of a medical entity, can't make final hiring/firing calls on clinicians, can't set their pay, schedules or staffing levels, can't control clinical standards or billing, and can't decide how long providers spend with patients. Covers MDs/DOs, NPs and PAs. Applies to new arrangements from **Jan 1 2026**. Violating contracts are void, and the other side can sue for damages, punitive damages and attorney fees. ([Davis Wright Tremaine](https://www.dwt.com/insights/2025/06/oregon-sb-951-restricts-corporate-medical-practice), [Reed Smith](https://www.reedsmith.com/articles/oregon-enacts-strict-new-corporate/), [McDermott](https://www.mwe.com/?p=321054), [American Med Spa Assn](https://americanmedspa.org/news/oregon-bill-restricts-msos), [Epstein Becker Green on SB 951 + HB 3410](https://natlawreview.com/article/sb-951-and-hb-3410-road-map-medical-professionals-oregon))

What that means for us: E&E can't hire an RN and run "E&E IV Bar." We'd need a doctor- or NP-owned PC, and we'd have no say over who works, when, or how. That's a partner.

### Fee-splitting / revenue share
Taking a percentage of each drip is the textbook problem. Oregon treats fee-splitting as unprofessional conduct for licensees, and SB 951 voids arrangements that give us control through money. Keep it to a **flat, fair-market venue fee in writing**, or nothing. Confirm with counsel.

### Who can start IVs

| Role | Can they? | Conditions |
|---|---|---|
| MD / DO / PA | Yes | Assess, prescribe, administer |
| Nurse Practitioner | Yes | Oregon NPs have independent prescribing; can assess and administer |
| RN | Yes, with a per-guest order | **Needs a prescriber order for each individual client** after an individual assessment (in person or telehealth). No blanket standing orders for IV hydration. ([OSBN Interpretive Statement, Oct 2024, rev. Mar 2026](https://www.oregon.gov/osbn/Documents/IS_IVHydrationTherapy.pdf)) |
| LPN | No | Not authorized for IV hydration under the OSBN statement |
| Paramedic / EMT | Not here | Oregon EMS providers work under a Board-approved supervising physician's standing orders, and non-emergency IV hydration is tied to their official EMS duties. A paramedic running drips at a sauna is a gray area at best. ([OAR 847-035-0030](https://oregon.public.law/rules/oar_847-035-0030), [Cohen Healthcare Law](https://cohenhealthcarelaw.com/can-a-paramedic-own-and-operate-an-iv-hydration-business/)) |

Also: Oregon isn't in the Nurse Licensure Compact, so any RN needs an Oregon license specifically.

### Medical director + standing orders
- A medical director (MD/DO, sometimes NP) signs protocols, audits charts, and is on call. They can't use standing orders to let the RN skip the individual prescriber assessment.
- Retainers typically run **$1,000-2,500/month** for a single site, and must be a flat fee for defined duties, not a cut of revenue. ([beancount.io guide](https://beancount.io/blog/2026/08/04/mobile-iv-therapy-wellness-injection-bar-bookkeeping-medical-director-corporate-practice-guide), [Modern Aesthetics](https://modernaesthetics.com/articles/2022-nov-dec/does-an-iv-hydration-therapy-practice-need-a-medical-director))

### Drugs, pharmacy, waste
- Any compounded product has to come from an Oregon Board of Pharmacy-registered source meeting USP <795>/<797> (per the OMB statement).
- The PC would need compliant drug storage and dispensing. Anti-nausea and Toradol add-ons are prescription drugs.
- Sharps and IV waste fall under Oregon's infectious-waste rules. Mail-back or contracted pickup. Not our dumpster.

## 2. Licensing + setup checklist (if we ignored the advice and built it)

1. Oregon health care attorney: structure the PC + space lease ($5,000-15,000)
2. Find an Oregon MD/DO or NP willing to own the PC and be medical director
3. Form the PC (Oregon SOS), EIN, bank account. Separate from Elevated Tides LLC
4. Medical director agreement (flat fee), written protocols
5. Telehealth or on-site prescriber for the per-guest screen
6. Hire Oregon-licensed RNs (per diem), verify licenses
7. Drug sourcing account, storage, logs
8. EMR/charting system, informed consent forms, HIPAA policies
9. Infectious waste contract
10. Malpractice + GL for the PC; notify E&E's carrier
11. Emergency kit: AED (we should have one anyway), oxygen, anaphylaxis kit (epinephrine), BP cuff, pulse ox

Realistic timeline: 3-5 months.

## 3. Insurance + liability

- **Malpractice / professional liability** for the PC and each clinician. Individual policies can start around $40/month; a clinic policy covering multiple RNs and an MD runs much higher. Get real quotes. ([Insureon IV therapy](https://www.insureon.com/healthcare-professionals-business-insurance/iv-therapy), [CM&F for AIVA members](https://cmfgroup.com/about-cm-f/cmf-partners/professional-liability-insurance-for-aiva-members))
- **E&E's own general liability almost certainly excludes medical services.** Even with a partner, tell our carrier and get the partner's COI naming us additional insured (goes in `Operations/Vendor Insurance Certs/`).

### Heat + cold + IV: why this is riskier than an IV lounge
- **Fainting.** Sauna drops blood pressure, hangovers drop it more, and a needle stick triggers vasovagal faints in some people. Stack all three and you get someone going down on a wet wooden deck next to a 40-ft-deep river.
- **Line + river water.** An open cannula site in river water is an infection risk. No plunge with a line in, and no plunge right after.
- **Toradol (ketorolac) on a dehydrated guest.** It's a common hangover-drip add-on. NSAIDs are harder on the kidneys when someone's dehydrated, which is exactly the state heat + hangover produces. That's a prescriber call, but it's a real reason the clinical decisions should stay with a partner's prescriber, not with us.
- **Allergic reactions** to vitamin additives. Needs epinephrine on hand and someone trained to use it.
- **Marketing claims.** "Hangover cure" language invites regulator attention. Our copy doesn't promise outcomes, and that matters more once an IV is attached.

Partner model puts all of the clinical risk on the party licensed and insured for it. We keep venue safety: water rules, heat rules, the AED, staff trained on what to do if someone faints.

### Monthly (8-9 weekend mornings)

Fixed: medical director ~$1,500, malpractice/GL ~$300, EMR ~$100, waste ~$100 = **~$2,000/month**.

| Scenario | Drips/mo | Revenue | Supplies + screen + fees | RN shifts | Fixed | **Monthly result** |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Low (1-2 per morning, 6 mornings) | 8 | $1,800 | -$616 | -$1,296 | -$2,000 | **-$2,112** |

## 6. Recommendation

1. **Partner with Good IV** (first pick, physician-led, group mobile only) or Boost IV (NP-staffed). Details in [[IV Partners — Portland]].
2. Launch The Morning After **without IV first** for 4-6 weekends. See if the ritual sells on its own.
3. Add the IV partner after that, with partner-direct booking and billing, a flat venue fee or no fee, and their COI on file.
4. **Skip in-house.** Revisit only if a partner is doing 30+ drips/month on the float and Oregon's rules have loosened, which isn't the direction they're heading.
