_2026-10-15. One plan for paying for The Rose (Holman Dock) and The Rosebud (the small teaser at the Elevated Tides marina). Built from the Community Funding Plan and Parts Catalog (Sept 2026), the Waitlist Playbook (Oct 2026), the Master Plan and Design Brief budget, the Oslo offsite plan, and the 10/15 team call. Supersedes the raise stack in the Parts Catalog section 4; the catalog itself and the founding-product rules still stand._

## The short version

- **What we're raising for:** The Rose all-in is **~$2.0-4.3M** (Design Brief s.11, pre-engineering). Plan against **~$3.0M** until a naval architect gives us a real number. The Rosebud and the land-side build-out are **not priced yet** `[TBD]`.
- **How:** six sources, stacked in order of how early we can use them. **Community first, debt last.** Community money buys belonging (a name, a seat, a founding membership), never a share of anything.
- **Realistic stack (assumption):** ~$3.0M, of which roughly half is community, presale and partner money, a quarter is site-level investment, and debt closes the gap. **Best case:** $4.5M+, no debt needed.
- **Named parts are simple:** give $X, your name goes on a plaque at that seat, stone or fire pit. Pioneer Courthouse Square bricks. No ownership, no return.
- **Two things nobody pitches around until they're answered** (see "Flags"): how gift money is booked (and whether any of it is tax-deductible); the 49-person code threshold.
- **Lead with community.** The Rose is a public third space tied to the identity of the City of Roses. Wellness is supporting, never the headline, and no copy makes health claims.

## 2. The capital stack, in order

Order matters. Each layer unlocks the next: community proof makes partners say yes, partners and presales make investors comfortable, and all three make a lender comfortable.

### Layer 1: Community (named parts, the Pioneer Courthouse Square play)

Portland already knows this move: the named bricks in Pioneer Courthouse Square. People pay to have their name in the city's living room forever. The Rose does the same with seats, stones, planks, petals and fire pits.

| | |
|---|---|
| **What it funds** | The intimate parts (hot-room seats, altar stones, ceiling ribs, boom planks, leaflets, thorn cleats, terrace seats), patron-funded art commissions (fire roses, petal skins, drapes, doors), the five amphitheater fire pits, the climbing wall |
| **Range (assumption)** | Realistic **$400-650K**. Best case **$900K-1.0M**. (Parts Catalog: intimate parts $250-430K, commissions $125-400K, climbing wall $40-80K + in-kind, fire pits $10-20K each.) Davey's "$200K a leaf" is a placeholder only, not in these numbers. |
| **Timing** | Opens at the public reveal, after the LOI is signed. Intimate parts and commissions need only the story and the renders, not engineering. Climbing wall bid runs after the insurer and engineer weigh in. |
| **Requires** | Holman public; selling entity + separate bank account; the Parts Board page; the contingency language (refund before the Groundbreaking Gate; refund / credit / roll after); recounted part quantities once the design locks; insurer OK on Build Days |
| **Risks** | Selling parts of something not yet engineered (counts may change); a named part that gets cut in value engineering; whether a gift is tax-deductible (only if it runs through a nonprofit; see Flag 1) |

**Build Days:** members do finish work only (oiling, sanding, installing holds, painting skins with the muralist). Not a funding source on its own; it's the loyalty and content engine that sells the other parts. Waivers, a site lead, insurer sign-off.

### Layer 6: Debt (closes the gap)

| | |
|---|---|
| **What it funds** | Whatever is left after layers 1-5, plus equipment (heaters, electrical gear, trailers) |
| **Range (assumption)** | Realistic **$400K-1.0M**. Best case **$0** (not needed). |
| **Timing** | Closes at the Groundbreaking Gate, after permits and the rest of the stack are committed. Lenders want to see the other money first. |
| **Requires** | Operating history (Ebb & Ember's books help), a lease or permit term at least as long as the loan, collateral, and almost certainly personal guarantees |
| **Risks** | Personal guarantees on top of the marina's existing debt and CapEx, which compete for the same cash and the same guarantors. A floating structure on a City permit may be hard collateral. Rate risk. |

**Options (all to verify with a lender):**
- **SBA 7(a):** flexible use; personal guarantees from larger owners are standard.
- **SBA 504:** built for long-lived fixed assets; check whether a floating structure on leased or permitted water qualifies.
- **Equipment financing:** heaters, electrical, trailers, sound. Easiest to get, smallest dollars.
- **Local bank / CDFI:** Oregon CDFIs such as Craft3 and MESO are worth a call; community-impact framing may help.
- **Revenue-based financing:** fast, expensive, fits short-term gaps (e.g., Rosebud equipment), not a $1M build.

## 5. Flags (raised, not resolved)

### How named gifts work (decided: plaque, not ownership)
Davey's call 2026-10-16: a named part is a gift with recognition. "I gave $500 (or $5,000) and my name is on the plaque by the seat." That's it. No ownership, no share, no return. Same as the Pioneer Courthouse Square bricks or a theater seat plaque.
- The plaque sits on or next to the item (brass under the seat, etched under the stone, a small plate at the fire pit, the plank itself).
- Every gift also goes on the Founders' Wall and the Parts Board.
- Copy says **give, name, dedicate**. Skip "own a leaf" as a headline so nobody reads it as a stake.

### Flag 1: Where the gift money lands
Not a blocker, a bookkeeping call for Jonah. A gift to Elevated Tides or Ebb & Ember is ordinary income to us and **not tax-deductible** for the giver. A gift through a nonprofit fiscal sponsor (HAP, WaterStrong) can be deductible, but only for the public-benefit pieces. So: never say "tax-deductible" on a plaque gift unless it runs through a nonprofit. Site-level investors (Layer 5) are a separate pile with their own paperwork.

**Owner:** Jonah (CPA), attorney for the fiscal-sponsor piece. **Before:** the Parts Board goes live.

### Flag 2: The 49-person threshold
The Newport review flagged 49 people. Above 49 triggers a different code category (likely assembly occupancy: two or more exits, more fire and life-safety requirements). Our own capacity notes say the same (IBC: over 49 needs two exits; the crown's single stair only works under 50).
Why it matters for funding: it sets people per round, which sets revenue per round, which sets what investors can be repaid and what a lender will lend. It also changes cost (exits, stairs, possibly sprinklers or more egress on a floating structure).
- Option A: design every room to 49 max (cheaper code path, lower revenue ceiling).
- Option B: accept assembly code at 50+ (more cost and review, higher ceiling, closer to Jonah's 50+ circular Aufguss).

**Owner:** architect + engineer (+ code consultant). **Before:** the pro forma goes to any investor or lender. The pro forma should show both cases until then.

### Smaller flags
- **"The Bud" naming collision:** the Sept plan used "The Bud" for a second site; Jess's "The Rosebud" is now the marina teaser. Pick one meaning.
- **Elevated Tides vs Ebb & Ember on the masthead:** the Sept plan says Elevated Tides leads; the call says the January activation is "Ebb & Ember brought this" and the site brand launches later. Both can be true; write it down.
- **Asking partners for letters and money at once.** Letters first.
- **Gas use and noise** are operating-cost and experience risks investors will ask about (Hannah and Jess both raised them).
