# Safety & Compliance Overview

## Regulatory Framework

Texas Parks & Wildlife Department (TPWD) oversees commercial watercraft operations in Texas. Lone Star Kayak Tours operates under TPWD regulations, and every guide needs a working understanding of what that means on the water.

**PFD Requirements**
Every person on the water wears a U.S. Coast Guard-approved Type III PFD for the duration of the tour. Buckled, fitted, the entire time. Texas law requires PFDs for commercial paddle operations, and our policy matches. No exceptions for experienced paddlers, strong swimmers, or guests who say it's too hot. The "it's too hot" objection comes up a lot in Houston. The answer is always the same: PFD stays on.

Type III is the standard for paddle sports because it allows arm mobility while providing buoyancy. We size and fit during check-in. A loose PFD rides up during immersion and becomes a face-dunking device instead of a flotation device. Fit matters.

**Throw Bags**
Lead guides carry a throw bag on every tour. You should be able to deploy it accurately to 50 feet. Practice if you haven't. This is not a decorative piece of kit.

**State Registration**
All kayaks in our fleet carry current Texas registration as required for commercial watercraft. You don't handle registration paperwork, but you should know it exists and is current. If TPWD shows up for an inspection, cooperate fully, be polite, and call management immediately.

**Guide-to-Guest Ratios**
We follow TPWD guidelines on group sizes. Standard is one guide per 8-10 guests depending on conditions and route. Management adjusts ratios based on conditions, route difficulty, and group composition. You don't set the ratio, but if you're solo-guiding and the headcount feels too high for conditions, say something.

## Heat: The Thing That Makes Houston Different

Most kayak operations worry about cold water. Houston worries about heat. The Houston area regularly hits 95-100°F from June through September, with humidity that makes it feel worse. On the water, there's limited shade. Guests who seem fine at check-in can be struggling 45 minutes into a paddle.

**What heat does to people:**
Heat exhaustion starts with heavy sweating, nausea, dizziness, and weakness. If it progresses to heat stroke (confusion, hot dry skin, loss of consciousness), it's a medical emergency. The progression can happen fast, especially in people who didn't hydrate before the tour, which is most of them.

**Your job:**
- Remind guests to bring water during booking confirmations and at check-in
- Carry extra water in the guide kit
- Watch for guests who stop paddling, look flushed, or seem disoriented
- Build in rest stops on longer tours where guests can hydrate and cool down
- Morning tours are preferred in peak summer for a reason. Afternoon tours in July and August are significantly hotter and more dangerous

**Cold water exception:**
Houston doesn't have cold water concerns most of the year, but after a strong cold front in winter months (December through February), water temps can drop below 50°F. When that happens, the same cold water protocols apply: wetsuits required or cancel. It's rare but it's real.

## Water Quality and Environmental Hazards

Houston-area waterways have their own set of considerations:

- **After heavy rain:** Water levels rise, current increases, and debris flows through. Flooding risk is real in Houston. If water is visibly high or fast-moving after storms, hold or cancel.
- **Water quality advisories:** Pay attention to any posted advisories. After major rain events, runoff can make conditions unsuitable. If there's a public health advisory on the waterway you're touring, don't go.
- **Wildlife:** Alligators are present in Houston-area waterways. They're generally not aggressive but guests need to know they might see one. Don't feed them, don't approach them, don't panic if one surfaces nearby. Snakes are also present, particularly water moccasins. Stay away from overhanging vegetation at the water's edge.

## Incident Reporting

Same principle everywhere: stabilize, communicate, document.

**On-water incidents** (capsizes, medical events, weather, heat emergencies): Handle the immediate safety issue. Call management. Document everything within two hours.

**What gets reported to TPWD:** Any serious injury requiring hospitalization, any water rescue involving emergency services, any fatality. Management handles formal reporting, but your written account is the foundation. Be factual. Write what happened, not your theory about why.

**Near-misses:** A guest who almost went over, a heat situation you caught early, a weather cell that barely missed you. Log these. They're the early warning system that prevents the actual incident next time.

## Waiver Process

Every guest signs a booking-specific waiver through FareHarbor before they paddle. Check during check-in. If someone hasn't signed, they sign on-site using the booking link. No generic QR codes, no "they signed last time," no exceptions. Unsigned guests don't paddle.

## Emergency Equipment

Every tour goes out with:
- Guide-carried throw bag
- Whistle (attached to PFD)
- First aid kit (checked and stocked before every tour)
- Phone in waterproof case or dry bag
- Extra water (minimum 2 bottles beyond your own)
- Sunscreen

Check it all before every tour. In Houston's heat, the extra water is arguably the most critical piece after the PFD.
