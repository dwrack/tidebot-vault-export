# Weather Hold Protocol

## The Basic Idea

A weather hold means we pause or cancel a tour because conditions are unsafe or heading that direction. Houston weather is its own animal. You can have a clear morning, a brutal thunderstorm by 2 PM, and sunshine again by 4. That's a normal summer day. The decision to hold isn't about whether conditions will improve. It's about whether conditions are safe right now and whether they'll stay safe for the duration of the tour.

## Thresholds

These are hard lines, not suggestions.

| Condition | Threshold | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Wind speed | >15 mph sustained | Hold: do not launch. Reassess in 30 minutes. |
| Lightning | Within 10 miles | Cancel immediately. If on water, get to shore now. |
| Visibility | <0.25 miles | Hold: fog, heavy rain, or smoke. Do not launch. |
| Water temperature | <50°F | Wetsuits required. If no wetsuits available, cancel. |

### Wind

15 mph sustained is where novice paddlers in sit-on-top kayaks start having trouble. In protected bayous and channels, 15 mph is less of an issue than on open water, but we hold the same threshold across all locations. If you're on a route with any open-water exposure, 12-13 mph with a building trend should be treated as a borderline situation.

Houston gets strong southerly winds off the Gulf, especially in spring and summer. They can build through the day. A morning tour that launches at 10 mph can be dealing with 18 mph by the time you're heading back. Check the trend, not just the current reading.

### Lightning

The 10-mile rule is standard across outdoor recreation. If you see lightning or hear thunder, it's within range. Get off the water. Kayaks on open water are the worst possible place during an electrical storm.

Houston thunderstorms are a near-daily occurrence from June through September, typically in the afternoon. Morning tours are less likely to hit lightning, but it can happen any time. If you're on the water and a storm builds, head to the nearest shore. Not the planned takeout if it's farther. The nearest shore. Get people into vehicles or solid structures. Not under trees.

Wait 30 minutes after the last lightning or thunder before considering a return. Most afternoon storms in Houston mean the tour is done. Reschedule and move on.

### Visibility

Fog is less common in Houston than in coastal operations, but heavy rain can drop visibility below the threshold. If it's raining hard enough that you can't see the landmarks you'd normally navigate by, hold.

### Water Temperature

This is rarely an issue in Houston. But after strong winter cold fronts, bayou and lake temps can dip below 50°F. Check the actual reading, not your assumption that "it's Houston, the water's warm." January and February after a norther can surprise you.

## Flooding and High Water

This is Houston's unique weather hazard. The city floods. Bayous and waterways that are perfectly calm on Tuesday can be raging brown water on Wednesday after a heavy rain. High water means:

- Stronger, unpredictable currents
- Debris in the water (logs, trash, construction materials)
- Submerged hazards that weren't there yesterday
- Water quality issues from runoff

If there's been significant rain (2+ inches in 24 hours), check water levels and conditions before launching. If the waterway looks visibly higher, muddier, or faster than normal, hold. This isn't a judgment call for borderline situations. If it looks different than normal after rain, it is different and it's not safe for guest tours.

## Houston's Afternoon Storm Pattern

From June through September, the pattern is reliable enough to plan around:

- Morning: typically clear or partly cloudy
- Midday: cumulus clouds build
- 1-4 PM: highest likelihood of thunderstorms
- Late afternoon/evening: storms typically pass

This doesn't mean afternoon tours are automatically cancelled. It means you need to be watching the sky and radar actively during afternoon tours. Have an exit plan before you launch. Know where the nearest shore pull-out is at every point along your route.

## Communication Protocol

When you call a weather hold or cancel:

1. **Tell management first.** Call or text. Don't just decide solo if conditions are borderline. Management may have information you don't (other guides' reports, updated forecasts).
2. **Notify guests.** Be direct and warm. "Conditions aren't safe for today's tour. Let's get you rescheduled." Don't over-apologize or hedge. You're keeping them safe.
3. **Offer alternatives.** Different time, different day. Work through FareHarbor for rebooking.
4. **Document it.** Log the hold/cancel, conditions, and time. Helps with refunds and pattern tracking.

The right call is always the safe call. A cancelled tour is a mild inconvenience. A lightning strike or a capsized guest in floodwater is a catastrophe. Lean conservative every time.
