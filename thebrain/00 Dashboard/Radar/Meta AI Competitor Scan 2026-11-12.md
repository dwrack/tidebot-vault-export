## What are the best-performing paid Meta ad angles for bachelorette and group-event experiences (party boats, bike bars, pedal barges)?

Show thinking

Important caveat up front: the Ad Library shows what's running, not ROAS, CPA, or CTR. No one outside the advertiser can see true "best-performing" numbers.

What you can infer is strong: advertisers keep spending on angles they duplicate across multiple ad variants, keep live for weeks, and reuse across locations. Those patterns are very clear right now for party boats, bike bars, and pedal barges.

Here are the angles that keep showing up:

1. The "#1 in [City]" Authority Angle

Example in the wild: Charleston Party Cat — "#1 Bachelorette Party Boat in Charleston, SC"

This works because the bachelorette planner is usually searching by destination first, not by boat type. Lead with city + bachelorette + superlative, then prove it with reviews, capacity, and photos of real groups.

Use it for: Party boats, pedal barges in a tourist-heavy city.

2. The "Don't Split the Party" / Private Boat Angle

Example in the wild: The Bach Boat Destin — "Don't Split the Party"

This is the strongest objection-killer in this niche. Groups of 8-14 hate the idea of being split across two boats, two bike bars, or mixed with strangers. The winning promise is: your whole group, one private vessel, one captain.

Use it for: Party boats and pedal barges where you can actually guarantee private.

3. The "Bachelorette, Elevated" Premium Angle

Example in the wild: Breezy Tiki Party Boat — "Bachelorette, Elevated"

Instead of selling "party," sell aesthetic + ease: curated music, decor, champagne, sunset light, no planning stress for the maid of honor. This angle pulls higher-ticket bookings and gets shared in group chats because it looks expensive.

Use it for: Tiki boats, sunset cruises, upscale pedal bars.

4. The "Best Bachelorette You'll Ever Book" Experience Angle

Example in the wild: Siesta Adventures — "Private Bachelorette Party On The Water | The Best Bachelorette Party You'll Ever Book"

This is pure social proof + FOMO. It works best paired with UGC-style video: real bride, real friends screaming, jumping in, dancing on deck. The copy is vague on purpose — the video does the selling.

Use it for: Any experience where the vibe is the product.

5. The "Book & Save" / Scarcity Angle

Example in the wild: St Pete Coastal Cruises — "Book & Save 30%" and Island Party Boat — "Now Booking Summer 2027!"

This is the retargeting / bottom-funnel angle. It doesn't create demand, it closes planners who already looked. Operators are booking far ahead right now — Summer 2027 messaging is already live — so scarcity is real, not manufactured.

Use it for: Retargeting website visitors, Instagram engagers, and past inquiry leads.

6. The Niche Identity Angle for Bike Bars

Examples in the wild:

Trolley Pub Cape Coral — "Pedal, Pint, Repeat"
Trolley Pub San Antonio — "Good Times Don't Wait" / "Your Night Out, Upgraded!"
Trill On Wheels — "Houston's Hip-Hop Party Bike - 100% Motorized!"

Bike bars can't win on "bachelorette" alone — they win by owning a vibe. Trolley Pub leans generic fun + bar crawl. Trill On Wheels leans hard into a specific identity (hip-hop, motorized, no pedaling required) and repeats that exact headline across many variants, which is a classic sign it's working.

Use it for: Pedal bars / bike bars where differentiation is music, route, or "no pedaling" vs. traditional pedal.

How to test these for your market

Don't run one ad. Run this structure:

Prospecting (broad / Advantage+ Audience): Angles 1, 3, and 4 — video-first, 15-30 sec, first 2 seconds = group laughing on boat/bike, text overlay with city + bachelorette
Retargeting: Angle 5 — static or carousel with date availability + discount / deposit info
Creative test: Same video, 3 hooks in the first 3 seconds: "#1 in [City]" vs. "Don't split your group" vs. "Bachelorette, elevated"

For bachelorette specifically, the planner is almost always the maid of honor / best friend, not the bride. Copy that speaks to her pain — "planning is stressful, this part is easy" — outperforms copy that speaks to the bride.

## How are outdoor sauna and wellness businesses in Portland, Oregon marketing on Instagram and Facebook, and what content gets the most saves and shares?

Show thinking

Portland's sauna scene isn't marketing like a spa — it's marketing like a social, outdoor experience. On both Instagram and Facebook, the winning accounts fall into three buckets: urban bathhouses, floating/riverfront saunas, and private backyard rentals. And the content that travels furthest isn't polished brand ads, it's creator-led tours, price-transparent posts, and giveaways built around tagging.

1. The 5 playbooks that keep showing up

1. The creator tour / "places to soak" listicle
Travel Portland, Travel Oregon, and local creators like Michele Venlee and Em Snieska do the heavy lifting for discovery. A single Reel tours 2-3 spots in one go — Cascada, Backyard Bliss, Edgefield, Enso Float — with price, vibe, and booking method on screen.

Those posts get shared because they answer the exact question in comments: "How much is it?" Travel Portland's Cascada / Backyard Bliss Reel drew 2K likes and 110 comments, mostly people tagging friends and asking for pricing, with the brand replying "$100 per person for 3 hours" for Cascada. 
 A similar Travel Oregon Reel for Guss Sauna and Ebb & Ember sparked the same pattern: tags + "how do you rent this?" 

2. Contrast therapy as the hook
Almost every high-engagement Reel is structured the same way: sauna → cold plunge / river jump → sauna again. It's visual, it's repeatable, and it gives viewers a reason to watch to the end.

Ebb & Ember's Friday morning "Ebb & Ember" session is filmed at dawn on a floating dock: sauna, jump in the river, repeat. 
A Facebook Reel from the Columbia River marina shows the same loop, plus a backflip dive, and comments turn into booking questions and local "I was watching from the cafe" validation. 
Guss Sauna's launch video leans into it explicitly: founder in a poncho, jumps in the Willamette, then back to the barrel sauna. The brand then uses comments to post pricing — $30 drop-in, $24 punch pass, $20 first session. 

3. Price-transparent, saveable offers
The posts that look most "saveable" are not aesthetic — they're practical. They put the deal in the graphic itself.

Löyly's "Summer Stillness Sauna" graphic lists exactly what's included — 2 hours sauna, lemon + spruce foot soak, face mask, rosemary mint salt scrub, cleansing tea — plus $55 Mon-Thu / $70 Fri-Sun. 
Löyly also ran an August promo on Facebook: 2-for-1 sauna for $40, or $67 with foot soak, face mask, body salts, and a beverage. 
Bawbs Acres in Corbett (about 40 min from Portland) led with "$20 per person for a 2.5-hour session" over valley / Mt Hood views, and comments turned into immediate booking intent and testimonials. 
Common Ground Wellness Co-op got traction by leading with "$18" — "$18 for 30 minutes, $30 for an hour" — plus all-genders / clothing-optional and affinity hours. Price verification dominated comments. 
SaunaOlo's walkthrough leads with "$19 day pass" for sauna + ice bath + cold shower. 

4. Giveaways engineered for shares and tags
This is the clearest share driver. The formula is consistent: follow + like + save + tag 2+ friends + share to Story for bonus entry.

The Gorge House giveaway Reel (2-night stay, wood-fired sauna + cedar hot tub) got 3.7K likes but 8.6K comments — almost all tagging chains. 
Ebb & Ember's own giveaway for a private session for up to 10 got 370 comments on Instagram, mostly "We need this!!" + tags. 
Good Day Portland x SolVita explicitly required "liking and saving the post, and tagging two friends" to win 2 free services.

Note: giveaways inflate comments/tags, but they don't always inflate saves in a lasting way — the value is reach to friends-of-followers, not necessarily loyal followers.

5. Build / design portfolios for homeowners
A second lane is B2C for buying a sauna, not booking one. Cascade Fence & Deck runs 10-12 slide carousels of finished backyard saunas — exterior, interior, heater, cold plunge, shower, controller close-up — framed as a winter investment. 2. What actually gets saved vs. shared

Instagram and Facebook don't publish save counts publicly, so you have to infer it from format + comment behavior. Here's the split emerging in Portland:

Most likely saved:

Listicles / itineraries: "3 places to soak," "24 Hours in Portland," "5 things worth the money" — people save these to plan later. One "24 Hours" Reel even ends with "save the list for your next visit."

Knot Springs has historically said Instagram is its preferred marketing channel, posting 4-7 times a week. 
Price + package posts: The Löyly, Bawbs Acres, and Common Ground examples above. People save to compare prices later, and comment "send me this post" — exactly what happened on a Backyard Bliss walkthrough. 

Most likely shared / tagged:

Giveaways (above) — tagging is the entry mechanic.
Novelty experiences: floating sauna, riverfront barrel sauna, private backyard rental. The novelty + "you have to see this" factor drives shares to Stories and DMs.
Community events: The Willamette Sauna Festivaali (Feb 14-15, 2026 at Milwaukie Bay Park) — 12+ mobile saunas, live music, Brew Dr. Kombucha, Nordic food, vendor village — was promoted via Portland Mercury with a contest for free passes. Instagram: Reels + carousels dominate. Brands use Reels for the sauna → plunge loop, carousels for builds and packages, Stories for giveaway bonus entries. Creator partnerships are essential — most discovery happens through Travel Portland / local creators, not the brand's own feed.

Giveaways will spike shares fast, but the posts that keep working after the contest ends are the ones people save to actually book: clear packages, clear pricing, and a creator showing the full experience start to finish.

## What short-form video hooks are going viral right now for local experience and tour businesses in the US?

Show thinking

Right now, the hooks winning for tour / local experience businesses aren't generic "Hey guys, welcome to..." intros. They're 1-2 second pattern-interrupts that put the viewer in the experience, then open a curiosity loop they have to watch to close.

Here's what's actually circulating on Reels/TikTok for this niche, translated into plug-and-play scripts:

1. The POV + Secret Reveal

This is the #1 format for kayak, boat, food, and walking tours right now.

Hook: Text on screen + voiceover while you're already moving

"POV: no one told you there would be gators on the tour" / "POV: you booked the cheapest tour in [City] and got this"

It works because it starts mid-scene, not with an intro. One viral Everglades kayak clip opens on a massive alligator gliding under the kayak, then cuts to a guest's shocked reaction — the mismatch between calm guide and panicked guest is the joke and the retention driver. 

Use it for: "POV: you thought [City] was boring until..." / "POV: your guide takes you to the spot locals don't post about"

2. The "Places No One Tells You About" List

Clear kayak operators are crushing this with aerial drone + bold text.

Hook: "PLACES IN [STATE] no one tells you about!" then immediate cut to the payoff shot. 

Another version from a Florida springs tour: Opens on turquoise water with just "Meet me at..." then pans to reveal the location tag. No talking, just beauty + mystery. 

Formula: Secret-sounding text + stunning 2-second B-roll + location reveal at second 4-5. Viewers stay to find out where it is.

3. The Contrast Hook

Creators are explicitly teaching this as the highest-performing hook type right now: a stark disparity in the first 5 seconds. 

For tours, it sounds like:

"I charge $75 for this tour. Hotels charge $400 for the view from your room."
"Everyone goes to [Famous Landmark]. We go here instead."
"Most people vacation here once, then start asking about residency. Guess which city I'm in?" — that exact line was used in a viral hook-building tutorial because it forces a guess. 
4. The "Skip The Crowds" Anti-Tourist Hook

Especially strong for US domestic travel and day-trip operators.

Hook: "Skip the big cities" / "Skip [Famous Spot] — do this instead"

The Irish Experience used "Skip the big cities" to frame an entire 1-day itinerary — Tintern Abbey, sea-cave kayak, lighthouse, sunset beach — as the hidden-gem alternative. It positions your tour as insider knowledge, not a tourist trap. 

Use it for: "Don't book [Popular Tour]. Book this." / "Stop going to [Crowded Beach]. Locals go here."

5. The Price / Value Hook On Screen From Frame 1

Property-tour creators proved this — price on screen from frame one massively outperforms a slow reveal. A recent breakdown noted a condo tour did 140.9K views at 116.5x follower count with price on screen from frame one. 

For tours:

"$65. 2 hours. This is what you actually get."
"Is a $99 clear kayak tour worth it? Watch this."

Put the price + duration in huge text over the best shot. It filters for buyers and creates a "is it worth it?" loop.

6. The Expertise / "3 Years In 60 Seconds" Hook

One of the 7 hooks that creators say "always work" right now is the Expertise Hook: "3 years of [niche] in 60 seconds." 

For tours:

"10 years guiding in [Location] in 60 seconds — 3 mistakes tourists always make"
"I've taken 5,000 people kayaking here. Here's what no one warns you about."

Pair it with B-roll, not talking-head. The current best practice is B-roll + storytelling voiceover — film the tour, then narrate a lesson / story / challenge over it, don't describe what you're doing on camera. 

7. The Visual Hook (No Words Needed)

The algorithm in 2026 is rewarding visual hooks over spoken hooks — one breakdown ranks them: Spoken (1K) < Captions (10K) < Text Hook (100K) < Visual Hook (1M). 

Winning visual hooks for tours right now:

Walk toward camera / jump into frame while already on the water/trail
Catch / zoom: toss something to camera, quick zoom into your face, then hard cut to the view
Mirror / POV paddle: Start with paddle hitting water in POV, then reveal the landscape

Creators are teaching these as "steal these 5 visual hooks" — mirror, catch, movement, jump-in, zoom-in — because they stop the scroll before a single word is spoken. 

What to avoid right now
"Hey guys, it's [Name] from [Business]" — creators are calling this the worst way to start a video because viewers decide in the first 3 seconds and no one is waiting for an intro. 
Bait-and-switch shock clips with no payoff — the "Snapchat hook" style is getting backlash for feeling deceptive, even when it gets views. For a real business, trust matters more than one viral spike. 
Quick test batch for you

Film the same tour 3 ways this week, same B-roll, different first 2 seconds:

POV: "POV: you booked the sunset tour and this happens"
Secret: "Places in [Your Area] no one tells you about"
Value: "$XX. 2 hours. Would you do it?"

Post all three, watch 3-second retention, not just views. The winner tells you which hook your audience actually responds to.

## What are tour companies doing to show up in AI answers like Meta AI, ChatGPT and Gemini for queries like best things to do in New Orleans?

Show thinking

Tour companies aren't just doing SEO anymore — they're doing GEO (Generative Engine Optimization) and AEO (Answer Engine Optimization). The goal isn't ranking #1 on Google, it's getting cited and recommended inside the answer itself.

For a query like "best things to do in New Orleans," AI doesn't pull from one website. It synthesizes from dozens of sources it trusts, then names specific tours. Here's what operators who are winning that are actually doing:

1. Answer the exact questions travelers ask AI

AI answers are built from Q&A-style content, not brochure copy.

Operators are publishing:

"Best [X] in [City]" pages — e.g. "Best ghost tours in New Orleans for first-timers," "Best swamp tour if you only have half a day"
Comparison pages — French Quarter walking tour vs. cemetery tour vs. food tour, airboat vs. swamp boat, small-group vs. large-group
FAQ blocks at the top of every tour page — How long? Where does it start? Is it kid-friendly? What if it rains? What's included? How much walking?

The trick is to write in complete, quotable sentences that an AI can lift verbatim. A bullet that says "2 hours, departs Jackson Square, max 12 people, rain or shine" is far more citable than a paragraph of marketing fluff. 

This is why you see so many social posts now structured as listicles — "11 things to do in New Orleans," "5 only-in-NOLA experiences worth booking" — with a named tour for each slot. That format maps perfectly to how AI builds an itinerary. 

2. Make every tour machine-readable

AI can't recommend what it can't parse.

Winning operators add:

Schema markup for TouristTrip, Product, FAQPage, Review, LocalBusiness, and Event — price, duration, departure point, meeting location, languages, age limits, cancellation policy
Consistent NAP + facts everywhere — same tour name, same start address, same duration on website, Google Business Profile, Tripadvisor, Viator, GetYourGuide, Yelp, Bing Places
A dedicated page per tour, not one mega "Our Tours" page. AI needs a single URL to cite for a single experience.

One guide puts it bluntly: put the same clear facts about each tour on your tour pages, in schema code, and on Tripadvisor and OTAs: what it is, who it suits, where it starts, and what it costs. 

3. Win the review / listing layer — because each AI leans on different ones

This is the part most operators miss. The engines don't all use the same sources:

Gemini: Strong on local — Google Maps + Travel data feeds answers; LocalBusiness schema compounds. 
ChatGPT: Leans on Yelp and Bing, not mainly on your Google Business Profile. 
Meta AI: Leans heavily on what's happening on Instagram/ Facebook — Reels, creator posts, Visit New Orleans-type accounts, and tagged tour operators showing up in real itineraries.

So operators are maintaining all of them: their own site, Google Business Profile, Tripadvisor, OTAs, Yelp, Bing Places, Apple Business Connect, and the local tourism board directory. 

And they're actively farming specific reviews — not just "great tour!" but reviews that mention the exact query terms: "best food tour," "great for families," "perfect first day in New Orleans," "our guide knew so much about voodoo history." AI picks up those phrases as evidence.

4. Publish original, citable expertise

AI trusts sources that originate information, not just repeat it.

Top operators are creating:

Proprietary data/reports — "We ran 1,200 cemetery tours last year, here are the 5 questions everyone asks"
In-depth destination guides — not just their tour, but "How to spend 1 day / 2 days / a rainy day in New Orleans," with their tour naturally included
Local expert bylines — guides named, with bios, local credentials, and quotes. AI prefers a named human expert over anonymous copy.

The shift is described as moving from basic keyword stuffing to building authoritative, structured content that AI models trust and reference. 

5. Get mentioned by third parties AI already trusts

AI rarely cites a tour company alone. It cites a consensus.

Operators are pitching to get included in:

Destination guides from Visit New Orleans, local magazines, and creator roundups

## What review and UGC tactics are top tour operators using on Facebook and Instagram to drive bookings?

Show thinking

Top operators aren't just collecting reviews anymore — they're running a full loop: capture on-tour → package as proof → push it back into ads, carousels, and booking pages.

Here are the tactics showing up again and again on Facebook and Instagram:

1. Screenshot-and-brand the review

The lowest-effort, highest-volume play is turning a Google / Facebook review into a branded graphic.

Corazon Travel & Tours posts 10-slide carousels where every slide is a 5-star review over a destination photo, naming the specific guide/driver and tour, with booking email + phone on every slide 
Skylife Travel and Tours uses the same template: "CLIENT'S TESTIMONIAL" + phone mockup + client trip photo + quote, framed in the caption as "reviews and receipts" to answer doubts 
Chan Brothers Travel simply screenshots a Google review — 4.6 stars from 3,806 reviews visible — and lets the detail do the work: tour name, dates, tour leader name, and "best experience despite being most expensive" 

Why it works: Specificity beats stars. Guide names, tour names, group size, and dates make it feel verifiable, not generic.

2. On-tour video testimonials

Instead of asking for a review later, operators film 30-60 seconds during or right after the trip.

Kesari Tours films couples/guests on location — often holding a branded flag — praising planning and the tour manager by name, then adds B-roll of the destination and a logo end-card 
Newmarket Holidays films a couple at a table post-trip, intercut with their actual trip footage, glacier, train, and group photos — it reads like a mini-documentary, not an ad 
Falcon Adventure Excursions does rapid-fire boat-deck interviews: multiple families/couples in one Reel all saying "fantastic / safe / recommend," which creates volume proof in under a minute 

Why it works: Face + place + emotion. Facebook Reels and Instagram Reels favor this format, and commenters then pile on with their own experiences.

3. Pair UGC photo + quote + direct booking CTA

The classic conversion format: real guest photo on one side, quote on the other, bookable tour named at the bottom.

Adventure Tours Australia overlays a detailed testimonial for its "3 DAY ULURU ADVENTURE" on a landscape shot, credits guide Hunter, and ends with "ready to experience... book at [site]" 
TourRadar builds a carousel around "in travelers' own words" — 4 real travelers, 4 real tours — then closes with "Every trip in this post is bookable... 50,000+ tours · real traveler reviews" and link in bio 
Much Better Adventures pairs guest photos (kayak, paddleboard, group river shot) with a 5-star review naming guides Sefa and Ahmet, then adds scarcity: "grab a spot in November, last departure of the season" 
4. Branded hashtag + repost engine

Larger operators don't just post their own content — they curate guests'.

G Adventures runs a photo-dump carousel entirely from tagged customers across Kyrgyzstan, Peru, Italy, Tanzania, Uganda, etc., tied to #gadventures / #travelyourheartout and a sale CTA 
Trafalgar built its funnel around #trafalgarunlockedawards plus a direct uploader for guests less comfortable with social, then distributed that UGC across social, site, and ads 
The playbook from operators generally: put the hashtag on confirmations, signage, and post-tour emails, then actively collect rights to the best photos 
5. Turn UGC into paid ads

This is where reviews stop being organic content and start driving bookings directly.

UGC in Facebook ads is reported to drive 4x higher click-through rate than traditional ads 
Operators who embed authentic guest photos on trip pages report booking conversion lifts around 30%, per Arival research cited in UGC guides 
63% of consumers trust user-generated images/reviews over professional photos, which is why operators push UGC into ads, not just feeds 

Tactical pattern: Best organic testimonial → test as Reels ad → retarget video viewers + past guests on Facebook with the same proof + a date-specific offer.

6. Ambassador / creator loop

A step beyond one-off UGC: formalize your best guests/creators.

Agoda's Ambassador program is the scaled version: creators share, audiences book via exclusive codes like AMBASSADOR10, creators earn commission — "Creators Share, Audiences Book, [Brand] Grows" 
Smaller operators do the same informally: invite top UGC posters into long-term partnerships, give them a code, and repurpose their content first — a model also used by travel brands moving from one-off sponsored posts to tiered ambassador programs 
7. The collection system behind it all

The operators winning at this don't hope for reviews. They build it into the journey:

At booking: "We'd love to feature your adventure! Share + tag us @handle #hashtag" in confirmation 
On tour: Guides prompt a photo moment, ask for a 20-second video testimonial, and name-check guests in posts
Post-tour: Survey/email within 24-48 hours asking for review + photo upload, with permission asked at upload — critical because a hashtag/tag alone does not give you rights to use someone's photo in ads 
Incentive: Small, authentic perk — feature spot, discount on next trip, entry to win a stay — rather than paying for fake reviews

One hashtag printed everywhere, plus a post-trip email that asks for permission, not just a review

## Which seasonal or holiday campaign ideas are working for tour businesses in November and December?

Show thinking

For tour businesses, November and December aren't really about selling November and December tours — they're about selling next season as a gift. The campaigns getting traction right now fall into 5 clear patterns:

1. Gift cards with a bonus, not a discount

This is the #1 play across the board.

The winning formula operators are using:

Fixed-value + tour-specific: Sell both a "$100 gift card" and a "Sunset Kayak Tour for 2" card, so shoppers who don't want to pick a date still have something concrete to give 
Bonus value: "Pay $500, get $600 in travel credit", "Buy $250, get $30 bonus credit," or tiered bonuses like Spend $150 → get $75 added, Spend $500 → get $200 added 
20% bonus for 3 days only is working for smaller operators as a pre-holiday flash sale 

Practical tip from operator guides: put the gift card on your homepage, every tour page, and in your booking confirmation email from mid-November through Dec 24, and email past guests twice — once before Black Friday, once the last week before Christmas. Federal rules keep most purchased gift card funds usable for at least 5 years, so write your terms before you sell. 

2. Black Friday / Cyber Monday as "Book Next Year Now"

In 2026, Black Friday is Nov 27 and Cyber Monday is Nov 30. 

Operators aren't discounting heavily — they're framing it as:

"Biggest sale of the year, up to $800 off" on 2026 tours, with a "comment SAVE for deals" CTA to drive DMs 
"Black November Deals" to book December holidays in November, with a hard Nov 30 deadline 
Flash sales that push people from comments → DM for details, which is becoming the standard lead-gen funnel for tour operators 

For a seasonal business like kayaking, this is ideal: you're selling summer inventory while people are in gift-buying mode, with no need to actually run tours in winter.

3. "Experiences > Things" gift messaging

This is the emotional angle that's outperforming straight discounts on social.

GetYourGuide-partnered creators are running "no slippers or socks this year" videos positioning a tour voucher as the Christmas hack 
A Portland couple's "experiences instead of material gifts" reel — chocolate tasting, holiday market, lights at Pioneer Courthouse Square — got huge agreement in comments: "Experiences > things" 
Munich Tourismus is selling it literally as "shared time is the most precious gift" with experience vouchers for Old Town tours, tower climbs, and neighborhood tours 

What to steal: Don't just post a gift card graphic. Post a 30-second "what they'd actually get" video — the launch, the water, the guide talking — with text like "Give them this for Christmas."

4. Giveaways + 12 Days / Advent-style campaigns

Giveaways are still the highest-engagement format in November/December, especially when they force tagging:

City Sightseeing London ran a Christmas Giveaway for flights + Christmas Lights Tour, driving 7.7K comments mostly from people tagging friends 
Golden Tours ran "Day 9 of our 12 Days of Giveaways" — 2 tickets to a Singalong Christmas Lights Tour — with follow / like / tag / share-to-story entry 
Show Me Seattle bundled a $275 tour gift card into a local-business holiday basket giveaway, and gave extra entries for buying a $75+ gift card

The local-business bundle is the smart small-operator version: you get exposure to 5-10 other local audiences without paying for ads, and the prize feels bigger than a single tour.

5. Cause + "Season's Givings" campaigns

Rick Steves' Europe runs this every year and it consistently outperforms pure sales posts: for every tour booked by Dec 31, $100 goes to a charity the traveler chooses. They've raised over $800,000 from 8,000+ participants. 

You don't need that scale. A small version works: "$10 from every gift card sold in December goes to [local river cleanup / youth outdoors program]" — it gives people a reason to buy from you instead of a big OTA, and gives you a story to tell in January.

What I'd prioritize if you're seasonal
This week: Launch one gift card product (tour-specific, not just dollar amount) + a bonus tier that ends Dec 23-24
Nov 27-30: Black Friday push to past guests — "Give next summer" email + reel
Early Dec: One giveaway with 2-3 other local businesses, entry = tag a friend who needs to get outside
Dec 1-24: Switch all messaging to last-minute / instant-delivery: "E-gift card delivered in 2 minutes, no shipping"
