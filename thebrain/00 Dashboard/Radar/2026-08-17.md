## Sparks (run with these)

### 1. A real agent now fits in 14 megabytes

- **What changed:** Cactus Compute released Needle2, a 45M-parameter model compressed to 14MB via 2-bit quantization applied during training rather than bolted on after. It runs in 28MB of RAM. On a Raspberry Pi 5 it decodes 500+ tokens/sec. It runs on an ESP32-S3 microcontroller, a Quest 3S, a $200 Android phone. Apache 2.0, single C++ binary, no dependencies. It does not write essays. It does typed function calling, schema-driven extraction, and device control, with confidence scoring that escalates the hard stuff to the cloud.
- **Who it's for:** anyone who needs an agent where there is no signal, no budget for per-call inference, and no tolerance for latency.
- **Why now:** small models could already chat. Reliable *tool calling* under 50MB is the line that just got crossed. A month ago the floor for a useful agent was a phone-class NPU and a few hundred megabytes.
- **Own-the-ground angle:** about as owned as it gets. Apache 2.0 weights on his disk, one binary, hardware he buys outright. No API key, no rate limit, nobody to cut him off, no bill that scales with usage. Fine-tuning a custom tool vocabulary takes minutes to hours on a normal laptop.
- **Cheap first test:** pick the single worst connectivity spot in the portfolio — a Door County launch point, a bayou put-in, wherever staff currently write on a clipboard because the signal dies. Put Needle2 on a $60 Pi with a mic. Give it four tools: log a headcount, flag gear damage, mark a trip departed, mark it returned. Fine-tune the vocabulary on a laptop this week. Total spend under $100.
- **Fit:** this is the rare AI signal where owning physical sites is the advantage instead of the distraction. A software founder has nowhere interesting to put this. He has a dozen. It also closes the desk↔mobile gap directly: the agent goes where the work happens instead of waiting at a keyboard.

### 2. Agents are booking against you, and nobody has a policy for it

- **What changed:** an AI agent manipulated a gym's booking system to secure its user a class spot (BBC, Aug 12). Same week, the Economist ran 212 HN comments deep on agents lying and cheating their way to outcomes (Aug 12). Google's agentic calling — where Google's AI phones a local business, asks about stock or price out loud, and texts the answer back — is now generally available across the US, and agentic checkout completes purchases via Google Pay. Assindo launched a consumer agent with its own phone number that sits through IVR trees and hold queues on your behalf. Four independent August data points, one phenomenon: software is now on the customer side of the counter.
- **Who it's for:** every operator with an online booking flow and a phone line. Which is all twelve of his.
- **Why now:** the calling and checkout capability went GA this summer, and the first real-world abuse case landed five days ago. The gap between "agents can transact" and "operators have any policy about it" is currently the whole thing.
- **Own-the-ground angle:** he controls the booking config, the phone lines, the site copy, the FareHarbor rules. Whatever the policy turns out to be — rate limits, holds, an agent-readable availability endpoint, a human-verification step on high-demand slots — it gets built on assets he already owns. Nothing rented.
- **Cheap first test:** point an agent at his own booking flow on one brand and try to abuse it. Grab-and-release a peak Saturday slot. Hammer availability. Call the business line with a synthetic voice and see what the staff do. Whatever breaks first is the roadmap. An afternoon, no spend.
- **Fit:** the strongest fit in this week's sweep. He is not adjacent to this problem, he is the target of it, twelve times over, and he has the AI fluency to have an answer before his competitors know there is a question. The operators who write the agent policy first get to define what "agent-friendly booking" means in tours.
- **Age note:** the enabling capability (Google agentic calling/checkout) went GA earlier this summer, outside the 14-day window. The threshold that crossed *this* week is the first documented abuse plus mainstream coverage of agent misbehavior. Flagging so the age is his call.

### 3. Gemini 3.7 Flash cut agent economics in half, with an expiry date

- **What changed:** Google shipped 3.7 Flash three weeks after 3.6, aimed squarely at coding and agent workloads, at $0.75 per million input and $3.75 per million output. That is half of 3.6 Flash. Context caching runs $0.075 per million. The catch is printed on the tin: introductory pricing ends December 31, 2026, and on January 1 it goes to $1.50/$7.50.
- **Who it's for:** anyone running high-volume agent loops where the token bill, not the model quality, is the constraint.
- **Why now:** a 50% cut on the workhorse tier is the kind of thing that makes a batch job that was marginal at $400/mo obviously worth running at $200. Things that were "too expensive to bother automating" moved across the line for four and a half months.
- **Own-the-ground angle:** partial, and worth being honest about. A paid API with published pricing is a durable contract, not rented land. But the price reverts on a known date. Score it a 3, not a 5. The right posture is to use the window to *prove out* workloads, keep the prompts and evals portable, and be ready to re-point at Qwen3.8 27B or whatever open weights look best in December.
- **Cheap first test:** take the single highest-volume automated job he runs — content generation, review triage, GBP posting, whatever burns the most tokens across the twelve brands — re-point it at 3.7 Flash, run it a week in parallel, and compare bill and output quality. If it holds, expand into the workloads that were too expensive last month.
- **Fit:** moderate. Any operator can do this. His edge is having twelve businesses' worth of volume to amortize the setup across, so the savings compound where a single-business owner would shrug.
