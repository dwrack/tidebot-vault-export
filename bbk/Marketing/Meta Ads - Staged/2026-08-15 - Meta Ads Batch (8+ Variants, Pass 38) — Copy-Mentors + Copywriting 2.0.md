## Format 1: UGC

### Variant 1: "3 Spots Locals Actually Paddle To"

**Angle:** The Locals' List. New in pass 38, proven, first execution. See pre-note 4 above.

**Evidence quote:** Video hook pattern "Paddling Houston's Hidden Buffalo Bayou Is An Amazing Adventure" (open-web video title, sourced via WebSearch) plus a currently-rising 2026 hyper-local-list Reels format (trend module).

**Objection handled:** I live in Houston, I'd already know if this were worth doing.

**Shot list / visual beats:**
1. Text overlay: "You've lived here your whole life. Have you paddled this?"
2. Cut: launch point near downtown, skyline visible.
3. Text overlay naming spot 1: under the Waugh Bridge.
4. Cut: bridge/bat-emergence area.
5. Text overlay naming spot 2: the heron bend.
6. Cut: heron or wildlife footage.
7. Text overlay naming spot 3: the quiet stretch.
8. Cut: calm water, skyline receding.
9. End card and CTA.

**Body framework:** Schwartz's identity-based hook (Sophistication 5, the market's heard "come try kayaking" a hundred times; this instead challenges the reader's assumption they already know their own city), text-on-screen list mechanic native to the trend.

**Hook (sharpened, one kept of two):** "You've crossed this bridge probably a hundred times. Ever paddled under it?"

**Primary text (sharpened):** You've crossed this bridge probably a hundred times. Ever paddled under it? Three spots on Buffalo Bayou most Houstonians have never seen up close: the stretch under Waugh Bridge, the bend where the herons hang out, and the quiet part that doesn't feel like Houston at all. Three miles from downtown. Not the view from the sidewalk. Houston Skyline Tour, $55 per person, 2 hours.

**Headline:** "3 Spots on Your Own Bayou You've Never Paddled"

**CTA overlay text:** "Book the Local's Route"

**CTA:** Book Your Tour. Sales/Purchase-objective campaign.

**Format spec:** UGC-style vertical, lo-fi text-on-screen list, minimal production. Existing skyline, bridge, and wildlife footage covers most of it; a fresher handheld clip would strengthen the "locals, not tourists" framing but isn't required to ship.

#### Copy-mentors pass

**Channeling:** Schwartz primary, Sugarman secondary. Schwartz because the real obstacle isn't ignorance of kayaking, it's the reader's false confidence that they already know their own city (a Sophistication 5 problem, not a Stage 1 awareness problem). Sugarman for the list mechanic itself, each spot is a small slippery-slide beat that earns the next.

**Framework applied:** Identity challenge instead of a feature pitch; three short curiosity beats instead of one long claim.

**Reasoning:** Ogilvy's factual-specifics instinct is present too (naming the actual spots) but the lead problem here is psychological, not informational, so Schwartz drives the hook.

#### Copywriting-2.0 pass

**Verdict:** Works, hook and headline both needed a rewrite.

**Line-by-line:**
- Draft hook, "3 spots on Buffalo Bayou locals actually paddle to. Not the tourist stuff," fails the ownable test. "Locals' favorite spots" is a listicle cliche any city account could run for any activity. Rewritten to "You've crossed this bridge probably a hundred times. Ever paddled under it?", which is specific to a reader who actually lives here and has actually crossed that actual bridge.
- Draft headline, "3 Spots on Buffalo Bayou Locals Actually Paddle To," has the same generic-listicle problem. Rewritten to "3 Spots on Your Own Bayou You've Never Paddled," which keeps the number (concrete) but replaces "locals actually paddle to" with a direct, slightly needling second-person claim that's harder for a competitor to reuse verbatim.
- "Three miles from downtown. Not the view from the sidewalk." Passes all three: visualizable (a literal distance and a literal vantage point), falsifiable (a checkable distance claim), ownable (this exact three-mile framing is this vault's own repeated, evidence-backed number, not a borrowed generic).
- Cut "Not the tourist stuff" entirely from the primary text draft, it's a hedge/cliche that adds nothing the "most Houstonians have never seen up close" line doesn't already say better.

**Sharpened final:** as written above (hook and headline rewritten, one cliche line cut).

**The one line to keep:** "You've crossed this bridge probably a hundred times. Ever paddled under it?" It does the whole job in one breath: names something true and specific, then flips it into a question the reader can't easily answer "yes" to.

### Variant 4: "The Story You'll Actually Tell"

**Angle:** The Story You'll Tell. New in pass 38, emerging, first execution. See pre-note 2 above before reading.

**Evidence quote:** "Managed to push off and flip our kayak over, landing in the Bayou... successful laughter and entertainment are provided for free." (Take Another Route travel blog, source-attributed, exact operator not confirmed as BBK specifically, used as illustrative pattern per the research doc's guardrail.)

**Objection handled:** Fear of looking foolish if something goes wrong, reframed rather than promised away.

**Shot list / visual beats:**
1. Text overlay: "Nobody remembers the trip that went perfectly."
2. Cut: guests laughing on the water, mid-paddle.
3. Text overlay stating the safety fact plainly.
4. Cut: guide assisting a guest, everyone smiling.
5. Text overlay landing the reframe.
6. End card and CTA.

**Body framework:** Name-the-fear-then-flip-it, Sugarman's sell-with-story-close-with-logic pattern: the reframe is the story, the safety fact is the logic that makes it safe to laugh at.

**Hook (sharpened, one kept of two):** "Nobody comes back talking about the trip that went exactly to plan."

**Primary text (sharpened):** Nobody comes back talking about the trip that went exactly to plan. Life jackets stay on the whole time, and if you tip, you float, your guide's right there. Most groups end up laughing about it for the rest of the day. That's usually the story you'll actually tell. Houston Skyline Tour, $55 per person, 2 hours.

**Headline:** "The Trip You'll Actually Talk About"

**CTA overlay text:** "Book Your Tour"

**CTA:** Book Your Tour. Sales/Purchase-objective campaign.

**Format spec:** Reel. Needs a real clip of guests laughing after a minor mishap, or an honest staged re-creation with real guests' consent; not currently in the asset library, flagged for a dedicated shoot.

#### Copy-mentors pass

**Channeling:** Sugarman primary, Bencivenga secondary. Sugarman for the reframe-as-story mechanic (open on curiosity, "nobody remembers the trip that went perfectly," then resolve it). Bencivenga for the safety line, since a claim this close to "something could go wrong" needs a checkable fact right next to it, not just tone, to clear the "yeah, right" test.

**Framework applied:** Curiosity-loaded reframe opener, closed with a specific, provable safety fact rather than a vibe.

**Reasoning:** Halbert's confiding voice was considered (it fits the topic) but was used for pass 37's "Whisper" angle already; Sugarman's slippery-slide structure fits the reframe mechanic more precisely here, name the fear, delay the resolution one beat, land it.

#### Copywriting-2.0 pass

**Verdict:** Needs rework. Headline and one body line tightened.

**Line-by-line:**
- Draft hook, "Nobody remembers the trip that went perfectly," is close but slightly abstract, "remembers" is passive. Rewritten to "Nobody comes back talking about the trip that went exactly to plan," which is more visualizable (a specific person, home, telling a story) and slightly more falsifiable in tone (it's about what people actually do, not just what they feel).
- Draft headline, "The Trip You'll Actually Talk About," passes on inspection, kept as-is: visualizable (a specific future conversation), falsifiable in the loose sense that it's a plausible, checkable behavioral claim, and reasonably ownable given it's paired with the specific angle beneath it.
- "Life jackets stay on the whole time, and if you tip, you float, your guide's right there." Checked hard against the guardrail (must not read as reckless): passes, this states the safety mechanism plainly rather than joking about it, which is the required pairing per the research doc.
- Original draft's closing line, "This is the story you'll actually tell," repeated the headline almost verbatim, a redundancy the burrito test flags. Rewritten to "That's usually the story you'll actually tell," softened with "usually" so it reads as an honest observation rather than a guaranteed outcome, and varied enough from the headline that cutting either one would lose something.

**Sharpened final:** as written above (hook rewritten, closing line varied from the headline, safety line held).

**The one line to keep:** "Nobody comes back talking about the trip that went exactly to plan." It's the one sentence here that no competitor's generic "book an unforgettable adventure" copy could accidentally duplicate, because it's built on an honest, slightly self-deprecating observation instead of a promise.

**Headline:** "One Tip Nobody Mentions: Wear Shoes With Grip"

**CTA:** Book Your Tour. Sales/Purchase-objective campaign.

**Format spec:** Static. Simple text-forward design over launch-site or dock photography; existing assets likely sufficient.

#### Copy-mentors pass

**Channeling:** Bencivenga, alone. This is a low-stakes, high-specificity claim, exactly the register Bencivenga uses for small honest details that build trust precisely because they're too minor to be a sales pitch.

**Framework applied:** Specific, checkable, low-drama detail in place of a generic reassurance ("we'll make sure you're prepared").

**Reasoning:** No other master fits a genuinely minor practical tip without accidentally inflating it into a bigger claim than the evidence supports, which the research doc explicitly warns against for this angle.

#### Copywriting-2.0 pass

**Verdict:** Works, headline tightened.

- "Everything else, we've got covered." Kept, it does the job of closing the loop (this is the one gap, not a pattern of things you need to worry about) without adding a new claim.

**Sharpened final:** as written above (headline rewritten).

**The one line to keep:** "Small tip before you book: the dock gets slick right at the water's edge." It's specific enough to be useful and small enough to be honest about its own size, exactly the register this thin an angle can actually support.
