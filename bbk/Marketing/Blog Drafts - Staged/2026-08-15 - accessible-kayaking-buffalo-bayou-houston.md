## FAQ SCHEMA

**Q1:**
```
Can you kayak Buffalo Bayou if you have limited mobility?
```
**A1:**
```
Many guests with limited mobility do kayak Buffalo Bayou successfully, since the water is flat with no current or rapids, and getting into a sit-on-top kayak requires less range of motion than a sit-inside kayak. Contact Buffalo Bayou Kayak directly before booking so guides can talk through specific needs and plan accordingly.
```

**Q2:**
```
Do you need to be fit or athletic to kayak?
```
**A2:**
```
No. Kayaking on flat water is low-impact, closer to a steady walk than a workout. Most guests in reasonable general health, including many older adults, complete a full tour without issue. If you can sit upright for about two hours and move your arms in a repetitive motion, you can do this tour.
```

**Q3:**
```
Is there a weight limit for kayaking on Buffalo Bayou?
```
**A3:**
```
The combined weight limit is 450 lbs per kayak. If one person exceeds that limit alone, guides can put them in an individual kayak instead of a tandem. Contact Buffalo Bayou Kayak in advance if you have questions before booking.
```

**Q4:**
```
Can someone in a wheelchair go kayaking on Buffalo Bayou?
```
**A4:**
```
The launch area is not set up as a formal ADA-accessible ramp, and getting from a wheelchair into a kayak on the water still requires some independent upper-body movement or assistance. Guests using a wheelchair should call ahead so guides can walk through exactly what the transfer looks like at 1019 Commerce Street before the day of the tour.
```

**Q5:**
```
Is kayaking hard on your knees or back?
```
**A5:**
```
A sit-on-top kayak keeps your knees bent at a mild angle and your back mostly upright, which is easier on both than many people expect. There's no impact and no sudden movement required. Guests with chronic back or knee issues should mention it when booking so guides can suggest the right seat position and boat.
```

## CONTENT (Markdown body)

```markdown
A guest called last spring before booking a tour for her father, mid-70s, two years out from a hip replacement. She didn't want to show up and find out on the dock that it wasn't going to work. Smart move. Here's the version of that conversation we wish more people had before they book, not after.

## The question we'd rather you ask before you book

"Can I actually do this?" gets asked in a dozen different ways: bad knees, a bad back, limited grip strength, using a cane on land, recovering from surgery, just genuinely out of shape and self-conscious about it. **The honest answer for most of those situations is yes, with a little planning.** The dishonest answer, the one that gets guests hurt or humiliated, is "sure, no problem" without asking a single follow-up question. We don't do that here.

Buffalo Bayou is flat water. No rapids, no real current under normal conditions, no waves to speak of. That's the whole reason this tour works for people who'd never attempt whitewater kayaking or an ocean paddle. Accessibility over difficulty isn't a tagline we made up for this post, it's the actual design of the trip.

## What "low impact" actually means on the water

Kayaking a calm bayou is closer to a steady walk than a workout. You're seated, paddling in a repetitive motion that uses your arms, shoulders, and core, no sudden exertion, no impact on joints. Most guests in reasonable general health finish a full tour without issue, including plenty of guests in their 60s, 70s, and beyond.

If you can sit upright for roughly two hours and move your arms in a steady rhythm, you can do this. That's a genuinely low bar. **It's lower than most people assume before they try it.**

- Grip strength needed: moderate, similar to holding a steering wheel for an extended stretch
- Core strength needed: minimal, the sit-on-top kayak is stable on its own
- Cardio demand: low to moderate, comparable to a brisk walk
- Balance required while seated: minimal, the kayak doesn't tip easily
- Balance required getting in and out: this is the real variable, see below

## Getting in and out of the kayak, honestly

This is the part we won't oversell. Sitting in a kayak once you're in it is easy. Getting into it, and back out, requires bending, some weight-bearing on your legs or arms, and a moment of balance while you transfer your weight from the dock to a floating boat. That's true for every guest, not just guests with mobility limitations, it's just a bigger factor for some than others.

The launch point at 1019 Commerce Street is a paved dock area, not a formal ADA ramp. Guides help with the entry and exit every time, holding the kayak steady and offering a hand, but there's no lift or transfer chair. For most guests with limited mobility, that's still workable with a guide's help. For guests using a wheelchair or with very limited independent movement, it's worth a phone call before you book, not a surprise on the dock.

## What to tell your guide, and when

Call or email before your tour date, not the morning of. **A guide who knows in advance can plan the right kayak, the right entry approach, and the right pacing.** A guide who finds out on the dock is improvising, and improvising with someone's safety isn't the move.

Worth mentioning ahead of time:

- Recent surgery, especially hip, knee, shoulder, or back
- Chronic joint pain or arthritis that limits bending
- Limited grip strength in one or both hands
- Any condition affecting balance
- Whether you use a mobility aid on land (cane, walker, wheelchair)
- Weight over the 450 lb per-kayak combined limit (tandems can split into two singles)
- Any concern at all that feels too minor to mention. It isn't.

None of this disqualifies anyone from booking. It just changes how we set the day up.

## Who this is genuinely a good fit for

Older adults who assume kayaking is a young person's sport are usually surprised. Guests managing chronic pain who've written off "adventure" activities entirely are usually surprised too. **This isn't a rebrand of a hard sport into an easy one. Flat-water kayaking was never the hard version to begin with.**

- Someone recovering from a joint injury, cleared for light activity by their doctor
- An older guest who wants to be outside without a long walk or a strenuous hike
- A guest managing chronic pain who still wants to be on the water
- Someone with limited upper-body strength but decent core stability
- A first-timer who's simply out of shape and nervous about it
- A guest with a cane or walker on land who has upper-body strength for paddling
- Anyone whose main barrier has been assuming "not for me" without asking first

## Where we can't promise everything, and where we can

We can't promise the dock will feel effortless for every guest, and we're not going to pretend otherwise. What we can promise: a guide who asks the right questions before you're on the water, not after, and a trip designed around calm conditions specifically so more people can do it, not fewer.
```
