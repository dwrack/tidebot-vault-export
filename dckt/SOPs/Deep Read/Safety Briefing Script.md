# Safety Briefing Script

## Before You Start Talking

The safety briefing happens after check-in, after PFD fitting, and before anyone gets in a kayak. Guests should be standing together, able to hear you, with their PFDs already on. Don't rush it. The briefing is 5-7 minutes and it's the most important 5-7 minutes of the tour. Everything you say here either prevents problems on the water or gives people the tools to handle them.

Deliver it like a human being, not like you're reading off a card. Make eye contact. Use names if you caught them during check-in. If people laugh, let them. Humor helps retention. But don't sacrifice clarity for comedy. The safety information has to land.

## The Briefing

### PFDs Stay On

"Your PFD stays on for the entire tour. Not around your waist, not unzipped, not 'just for a second while I adjust my shirt.' On, buckled, the whole time. The water here is colder than you'd expect, even in summer. If you go in, that PFD is doing the work while your body figures out what just happened."

This is the most common pushback point, especially on warm days. Be friendly but completely firm. There's no version of this tour where PFDs come off on the water.

### The Kayaks

"We're using sit-on-top kayaks. That means you're sitting on the deck, not inside a cockpit. Big advantage: if you flip, you're not trapped. You just slide off and climb back on. These kayaks are very stable, but they can flip if you lean way to one side or get caught by a wave broadside. So keep your weight centered and face the waves when they come."

For tandem kayaks, add: "Person in the back steers. Person in the front provides power. If you disagree on direction, the back paddler wins. That's just physics."

### Paddle Technique

Demo this while you talk. Hold the paddle up so everyone can see grip and blade angle.

"Grip the paddle with hands about shoulder-width apart. The concave side of the blade, the scooped part, faces you. To go forward, reach forward, plant the blade in the water near the bow, and pull back to your hip. Then switch sides. You don't need to go deep or pull hard. Smooth, even strokes."

"To turn, paddle on the opposite side from where you want to go. If you need to turn hard, do a sweep stroke: reach out to the side and make a wide arc."

"To stop, just stop paddling and drag a blade in the water."

Keep this short. Most guests will figure out paddling within the first 100 yards. The technique demo is mainly so they're not completely lost at launch.

### Staying Together

"This is a group tour, so we stay together. I'll be at the front setting the pace. If you fall behind, that's OK. I'll notice and we'll wait. If you're way out ahead, I'm going to ask you to ease up. The group moves at the speed of the slowest paddler, and there's nothing wrong with that."

"If you need to stop, wave your paddle overhead. That's the universal 'I need attention' signal. I'll come to you."

### Wildlife

"Door County has incredible wildlife and you'll probably see some of it. Bald eagles nest along the shoreline, and you might spot them fishing. Loons are common on the water. Great blue herons along the banks. Cormorants drying their wings on the rocks. If we're lucky, we might see a beaver or muskrat."

"The rule with wildlife is: look, enjoy, don't chase. Paddling toward an eagle nest or a loon with chicks is going to stress the animal and it's not the experience we're going for. If something cool shows up, I'll point it out and we'll hang back at a respectful distance."

No alligators here, which is worth a quick mention if guests ask about it. People who've kayaked in the South sometimes wonder. The biggest animal concern in Door County is actually getting too close to nesting birds.

### Cold Water Awareness

"I mentioned this already, but it bears repeating: the water here is cold. Lake Michigan can be in the upper 50s even in July. If you capsize, you're going to gasp. That's a normal reflex. Don't panic. Your PFD keeps you floating. Grab your kayak, and I'll be there to help you back on or get you to shore."

"If anyone starts feeling cold, shivering, or uncomfortable during the tour, tell me. Don't tough it out. Cold water takes the fight out of you faster than you think, and catching it early is how we keep it from becoming a problem."

This is the section where tone matters most. You're not trying to scare people. You're giving them a realistic picture so they make good decisions on the water.

### Lightning Rule

"If we hear thunder or see lightning, the tour pauses immediately and we head to the nearest shore. Kayaks on open water in a thunderstorm is the one scenario where there's no judgment call to make. We just go. I'll lead you in and we'll wait it out. If the storm passes, we continue. If it doesn't, we'll head back and get you rescheduled."

### Questions

"That's the safety piece. Any questions before we get on the water?"

Pause here for real. Don't blow through it. People often have questions but need a few seconds to formulate them. Common ones: "What if I can't keep up?" (You will, and we'll match the group pace.) "Can I bring my phone?" (Yes, dry bag recommended, we're not responsible if it goes in the lake.) "Is it really that cold?" (Yes, but you're not going in. And if you do, you'll be fine because you're wearing your PFD and I'm right there.)

## Adjustments by Season

**Early season (May-June):** Emphasize cold water more. Mention that wetsuits may be provided. Explain that water temp is independent of air temp, because guests see a 75-degree day and assume the water matches.

**Peak season (July-August):** Standard briefing. Water is at its warmest, but still colder than most people expect. Emphasize sun protection and hydration since guests sometimes forget those basics.

**Late season (September-October):** Back to cold water emphasis. Days are shorter, weather more variable. Mention that conditions can change during the tour and that's normal. Reassure them that you're monitoring it the whole time.

## What the Briefing Isn't

The briefing is not a legal disclaimer read at speed. It's not a PowerPoint. It's not optional for repeat guests ("I've done this before" doesn't matter, you do the briefing every time). It's a conversation between a guide who knows these waters and guests who are trusting you with their safety. Treat it that way.
