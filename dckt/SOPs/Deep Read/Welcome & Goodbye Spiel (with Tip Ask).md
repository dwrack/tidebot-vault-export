# Welcome & Goodbye Spiel (with Tip Ask)

**For:** All DCKT guides
**Use with:** Safety Briefing Script (delivered between the welcome and pushing off)
**Voice check:** Warm, plainspoken, confident. Not corny, not scripted-sounding, not salesy. Deliver it like you mean it because you do.

---

## Why This Matters

The welcome sets whether guests trust you for the next 2–4 hours. The goodbye sets whether they tip well, leave a five-star review, and book their next experience. Both are worth practicing until they feel natural.

Tips at DCKT are 18% suggested. That's real money for you — a typical 10-person Cave Point tour tipped at 18% is ~$130. The difference between a guide who earns tips consistently and one who doesn't is almost never paddling skill. It's the welcome, the goodbye, and how well they made guests feel seen in between.

Two rules on tipping:

1. **Plant the seed early. Ask cleanly at the end.** Never mid-tour, never in a way that makes guests feel obligated in the moment.
2. **Ask for the review before you ask for the tip.** Reviews stack forever. A tip is one transaction; a review pays us back for years.

---

## THE WELCOME SPIEL

Deliver this right after check-in, once everyone has signed waivers and gathered on the beach. Guests are usually a little nervous — first-time paddlers especially. Your job in the next 90 seconds is to lower their heart rate and make them like you.

### Opening — Introduce yourself like a human

> "Hey everybody, welcome to Door County Kayak Tours. My name's [NAME], I'm going to be your guide today. Real quick before we get into the safety stuff — I want you to know who's taking you out on the water.
>
> I'm [one-sentence personal hook: where you're from / what got you into paddling / how many seasons you've been guiding here]. I've been guiding these waters [X seasons / years], and today is [tour name] — [one-sentence tease of what makes it special: 'the sea caves are 420 million years old and the only way to see them is from a kayak,' or 'we're heading out to the Louisiana wreck, which sank in 1913 and is still visible from the surface,' etc.]. It's going to be a good day."

**Why this works:** Guests tip humans, not uniforms. A name, a hometown, and a reason you love the job creates a real person. That's the single biggest lever on end-of-tour tips.

### The team frame — plant the seed (no ask yet)

> "Quick thing about us: DCKT is a small, local outfit based right here in Jacksonport. Our guides are trained in capsize recovery, water rescue, and first aid, we know these waters, and we're a tight crew. Most of us are seasonal, most of us are here because we love the water and we love this county. Same as a restaurant, we work for tips — so if you have an amazing time today, that matters to us. But right now the only thing I want you thinking about is having fun and staying safe. We'll worry about all that at the end."

**Why this works:** You've told them tipping is normal and expected — the same way a restaurant menu tells you before dessert arrives. You did it once, cleanly, in the context of "here's who we are." You will NOT bring it up again until the end. That's the whole trick.

### Set expectations — bring the energy up

> "Here's what to expect: we're going to spend a few minutes on shore going over paddle basics and safety. Then we launch, we paddle as a group at the pace of the slowest paddler — no rush, no race — and I'll be pointing out wildlife, geology, and history the whole way. If you have questions on the water, ask. If you want a photo, I'll take it. If something's wrong, you tell me. This is your tour."

### Handoff to safety briefing

> "Alright — let's talk about staying safe out there."

*(Transition into the Safety Briefing Script.)*

## THE GOODBYE SPIEL

Delivered on the beach at the takeout, after boats are pulled up and everyone's back on land with their gear. Do NOT do this on the water. Do NOT rush it. Guests are riding a high right now — this is the single most valuable 3-minute window in the whole business.

### Step 1 — Land the moment

Start with something specific to the tour they just had. Not generic. Reference something that actually happened.

> "Alright — we made it. That was a great group today. I want to call out [name the moment: the eagle sighting, the way the light hit at the sea caves, how well the tandems worked out, how [name] absolutely crushed her first time in a kayak, whatever was real]. That doesn't happen every tour. You picked a good day."

**Why this works:** Specific > generic. When you name a real moment, guests re-live the feeling. That's the peak emotional state for the next three asks.

### Step 2 — The review ask (do this FIRST)

> "One favor before you go. We're a small local business, and honestly the way people find us is Google and TripAdvisor reviews. If you had a good time today, the biggest thing you can do for us — bigger than anything — is take 30 seconds right now, while it's fresh, and leave us a review. Mention me by name if you want, mention the tour, whatever felt real to you. It genuinely keeps guides like me employed through the winter."

Then — and this is the move — pull out your phone or point them to the QR code:

> "I've got a QR code right here that takes you straight to the Google review page. Takes about a minute. If you can do it right now on the beach, that would mean the world to us."

**Why this works:** Asking in-person, on the beach, with a QR code in hand, converts 10x better than any follow-up email. And you asked for the review before you asked for the tip — which makes the tip ask feel less transactional.

### Step 3 — The tip ask (clean, options-forward, no guilt)

Wait a beat after the review ask. Let people finish scanning if they're doing it. Then pull out the receipts to sign — this is the natural moment.

> "Last thing before you go — I've got your receipts here to sign off on. You'll see there's a tip line on there. Like I mentioned at the start, our guides work for tips, and 18% is the industry standard. Cash or Venmo work too if that's easier — my Venmo's [@yourhandle]. And if today wasn't a tip-worthy tour, tell me why so I can do better next time. Either way, it was a pleasure paddling with you."

Then hand them the receipt and a pen. Don't hover. Give them the space to actually write.

**Why this works:**

- **The receipt is doing the work.** You're not asking for a tip out of thin air — you're handing them a piece of paper that has a tip line on it. The ask is baked into the transaction. That's a fundamentally different (and easier) social interaction than a verbal-only ask.
- **Naming the number** (18%) removes the "what's normal?" hesitation that costs you tips. Guests who don't know the norm often under-tip or skip entirely.
- **Cash and Venmo as backups** cover the guests who paid cash for the tour, or who want to add more than the card tip.
- **The closing line** — *"if today wasn't a tip-worthy tour, tell me why"* — flips the script. It signals confidence, invites feedback, and quietly reframes not-tipping as a specific choice, not a default. This works surprisingly hard.
- **Don't hover.** Standing over someone as they write a tip amount is the fastest way to get a low tip. Hand them the receipt, take two steps back, chat with the rest of the group.

### Step 4 — Plant the seed for the next visit

Soft. Not salesy. One sentence.

> "One more thing — if you're around for a couple more days, our Door Bluff Shipwreck tour is completely different from what we did today: bayside, calmer water, real shipwreck sites. And the Eco Estuary is our best wildlife tour if you have anyone in the group who wants a mellower paddle. Just something to keep in mind. Website's on the van."

### Step 5 — Genuine sign-off

Handshake, high-five, whatever fits the group. Use names.

> "Safe travels, everybody. [Name], [name], [name] — really glad you came out today."

## TIP-ASK VARIATIONS (Use What Fits the Group)

Different groups need different energy. Keep the structure, adjust the language.

### For a group that clearly loved it (laughing, hugging, taking photos)

> "You guys were a dream group. Receipts to sign — there's a tip line on there — or cash or Venmo works too. Whatever's easiest."

### For a quieter group (older, reserved, first-timers)

> "One quick thing our owner has me mention at the end of every tour — the guides work primarily for tips, and 18% is the industry standard. There's a tip line on your receipt when you sign, or cash or Venmo works. No pressure at all — I just want to make sure you know how it works."

### For a family with kids

> "Hey, before you guys head out — this was a blast. Here's the receipt to sign, tip line is right there if you want to add one, or cash works too. And if the kids had a good time, our Eco Estuary tour is honestly the best one for families — calm flatwater, tons of wildlife, and the kids usually end up being the most engaged paddlers out there."

### For a private tour or big-spender group

Never explicitly ask. They know. Just hand over the receipt.

> "It's been a real pleasure. Here's your receipt to sign off on — tip line's right there. Otherwise cash or Venmo. Whatever works. And I'd love it if you left a Google review while it's fresh."

### If a guest asks "what's the standard tip?"

Straight answer, no fumbling.

> "Industry standard is 18% for guided tours. Some folks do more, some do less. Whatever feels right for how the day went."

## Things NOT To Do

- **Don't tip-ask on the water.** Ever. Guests are captive. It's coercive.
- **Don't tip-ask before the review ask.** Reviews compound; tips don't.
- **Don't apologize for asking.** ("I hate to bring this up, but…") It signals the ask is unreasonable. It isn't.
- **Don't over-explain.** Say it once, cleanly, offer the options, move on.
- **Don't guilt.** No sad-story pitches about how you're saving up for grad school. Guests can smell it.
- **Don't skip the goodbye spiel because you're tired.** The last 3 minutes of the tour are worth more than any other 3 minutes. Bring the same energy you brought to the welcome.
- **Do not add tips or extra merch charges to Airbnb Experiences or Viator bookings** — we don't have a card on file for those guests, so there's nothing to run the tip against. If those guests want to tip, cash or Venmo only.

---

## Delivery Notes

- **Practice out loud.** Reading a spiel in your head is not the same as saying it. Do it in the mirror or with another guide until it stops sounding like a script.
- **Vary it.** Never say the exact same thing tour to tour. Guests can tell.
- **Watch the group.** If they're already reaching for phones and wallets, shorten the ask. If they're standing awkwardly, take your time and lead them through it.
- **Own the ask.** The moment you flinch, guests flinch too. If you deliver it like it's a normal, expected part of the tour — because it is — nobody feels weird about it.

## Quick Reference — Structure at a Glance

**Welcome (90 sec, on the beach before safety briefing):**
1. Name + personal hook + why today's tour is cool
2. "We're a small local team, guides work for tips" — one clean mention, no ask
3. Set the vibe: pace, safety, "this is your tour"
4. Hand off to safety briefing

**Goodbye (3 min, on the beach at takeout):**
1. Land a specific moment from today's tour
2. Ask for the review — QR code in hand
3. Hand out receipts to sign — tip line is on the receipt; cash / Venmo as backups
4. Plant next-experience seed (one sentence)
5. Personal sign-off, use names
