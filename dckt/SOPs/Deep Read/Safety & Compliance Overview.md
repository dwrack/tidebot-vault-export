# Safety & Compliance Overview

## Why This Matters More Here

Door County isn't a lazy river. You're putting people on Lake Michigan, Green Bay, or connecting waterways where conditions can shift from glass-calm to whitecaps in under an hour. The water stays cold well into summer. A capsize that would be a funny story in Texas is a genuine medical concern here. Everything in this document exists because the margin for error is smaller than most kayak operations.

## Wisconsin DNR Regulations

Wisconsin Department of Natural Resources governs commercial kayak operations in the state. Here's what you need to know at the guide level:

**PFD Requirements**
Every person on the water must wear a U.S. Coast Guard-approved Type III PFD for the duration of the tour. Not "have access to." Not "carry on the kayak." Wearing it, buckled, the entire time. No exceptions for strong swimmers, experienced kayakers, or people who say they'll be fine. Wisconsin law and our policy align on this, and it's not negotiable.

Type III is the standard for paddle sports. It allows arm mobility while providing adequate buoyancy. We size and fit PFDs during check-in. A PFD that doesn't fit properly is almost as bad as no PFD at all.

**Throw Bags**
Lead guides carry a throw bag on every tour. This is a rescue tool, not decoration. You should be able to deploy it accurately to 50 feet. If you haven't practiced with one, ask a senior guide to drill you before your first solo tour.

**State Registration**
Our kayaks are registered with the state as required for commercial watercraft operations. You don't handle the registration paperwork, but you should know that every boat in our fleet has current registration. If DNR ever shows up at a launch site for an inspection, cooperate fully, be polite, and call management immediately.

**Guide-to-Guest Ratios**
We operate within DNR guidelines for group sizes. Typical ratio is one guide per 8-10 guests, depending on conditions and route. On days with marginal weather or cold water temps, we may tighten that ratio. Management makes that call.

## Cold Water: The Thing That Makes Door County Different

This deserves its own section because it's the single biggest safety differentiator between this operation and warmer-water kayak tours.

Lake Michigan surface temperatures by rough timeframe:
- Late May/early June: 45-55°F
- Mid-June through July: 55-68°F
- August: 65-75°F (warmest you'll see)
- September: 60-70°F and dropping
- October: 50-60°F

These are surface temps. They vary by location, wind patterns, and upwelling events. Green Bay side tends to be slightly warmer than the Lake Michigan side early in the season.

**What cold water does to people:**
When someone goes into water below 60°F unexpectedly, their body's cold shock response kicks in. Involuntary gasping, hyperventilation, spike in heart rate and blood pressure. In water below 50°F, you can lose fine motor control in your hands within 2-3 minutes. Effective swimming ability drops fast.

This means a simple capsize, something you'd barely blink at in warm water, becomes a time-sensitive rescue situation. Get the guest back on their kayak or to shore fast. Monitor for shivering, confusion, slurred speech, and blue lips.

**Wetsuit threshold:**
When water temp is below 50°F, we either require wetsuits or cancel. This isn't a suggestion. It's policy. Early and late season tours should always have the water temp checked that morning, not estimated from last week's reading.

## Incident Reporting

When something goes wrong, the sequence is: stabilize, communicate, document.

**On-water incidents** (capsizes, medical events, weather emergencies): Handle the immediate safety issue first. Then call management. Then document everything within two hours while details are fresh.

**What gets reported to Wisconsin authorities:** Any serious injury, any water rescue requiring emergency services, any fatality (which we've never had and plan to keep that way). Management handles the formal reporting, but your written account of what happened is the foundation of that report. Be factual. Write what you saw and did, not what you think caused it or whose fault it was.

**Near-misses count too.** If a guest almost went over in rough water but didn't, that's still worth logging. Near-miss reports help us spot patterns before they become actual incidents. A guide who reports near-misses isn't admitting failure. They're being professional.

## Waiver Process

Every guest signs a waiver through FareHarbor before they get on the water. The waiver is booking-specific, not a generic blanket form. During check-in, verify waiver status for each person on the booking. If someone hasn't signed, they complete it on-site using the booking-specific link. Nobody paddles without a signed waiver. Not their friend, not their kid, not the person who "already signed one last year."

## Emergency Equipment

Every tour goes out with:
- Guide-carried throw bag
- Whistle (attached to PFD)
- First aid kit (checked and stocked before every tour)
- Phone in a waterproof case or dry bag
- Emergency blanket (early and late season, this is critical)
- Spare dry clothing in a dry bag (shoulder season)

This isn't a checklist you glance at. This is gear that saves lives if things go sideways. Check it before every tour like you're going to need it, because one day you might.
