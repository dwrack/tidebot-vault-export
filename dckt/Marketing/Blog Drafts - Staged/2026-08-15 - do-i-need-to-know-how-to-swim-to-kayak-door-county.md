## FAQ SCHEMA
_(5 Q&As)_

**Q1:**
```
Do I need to know how to swim to kayak in Door County?
```
**A1:**
```
No. On a guided DCKT tour, you wear a life jacket the entire time you're on the water, and our kayaks are sit-on-top, which means there's no cockpit to get trapped in. Swimming ability matters far less than most first-timers assume, because the life jacket and the boat are doing the work of keeping you afloat and on top of the water, not your own stroke. We still ask about comfort level when you book, and our guides adjust pace and route for nervous paddlers.
```

**Q2:**
```
Can non swimmers kayak on a guided tour?
```
**A2:**
```
Yes. Non-swimmers join our guided tours regularly. The life jacket is required at all times, the kayak is a stable, open-deck sit-on-top, and our routes stay in protected water close to shore. If you can't swim, tell us when you book so your guide knows and can keep an eye on you and pace the group accordingly.
```

**Q3:**
```
Is kayaking safe if you can't swim?
```
**A3:**
```
On a guided tour with required life jackets, sit-on-top kayaks, and a trained guide, yes, it's a reasonable activity for a non-swimmer. The life jacket is what actually keeps you afloat if you go in the water, not your swimming ability. That said, always disclose your comfort level to your guide before launch so the tour can be paced to you, and always wear the life jacket exactly as fitted.
```

**Q4:**
```
Do you have to wear a life jacket the whole time kayaking?
```
**A4:**
```
Yes, on every DCKT tour, the entire time you're on the water. It's not optional and it's not just for guests who can't swim. Guides check and fit every life jacket before launch rather than assuming a guest has it right, and it stays on from launch to landing.
```

**Q5:**
```
What happens if a sit-on-top kayak tips over?
```
**A5:**
```
A sit-on-top kayak has an open deck instead of an enclosed cockpit, so if it tips, you slide off and surface right away instead of getting caught underneath or inside the boat. The kayaks we use are wide and stable and built for recreational paddling, not built to flip easily in the calm, protected water our tours run in. Your guide is trained in capsize recovery and water rescue and will help you back onto the kayak.
```

## CONTENT (Markdown body)

```markdown
"I haven't been in a pool since college. Is that going to be a problem?"

We hear some version of that question at the shop counter almost as often as we hear "will I see a shipwreck." It usually comes in quiet, half-embarrassed, right after someone has already paid for the tour and is now wondering if they made a mistake.

They didn't. Here's the honest answer, no hedging.

## Short answer: no, and here's the actual reason

You do not need to know how to swim to kayak with us. Not "technically you're fine." Actually fine. We don't ask about swimming ability when you book, we don't run a swim test at the launch, and plenty of guests who'd never jump in a lake on purpose have paddled Cave Point with us and walked away with one of the best mornings of their whole Door County stay.

That's not us being cavalier about safety. It's the opposite. The whole tour is built so that your ability to swim is not the thing standing between you and a good time on the water. Three things do that job instead: the life jacket, the kayak, and the guide.

## What's really keeping you afloat (hint: not your stroke)

A life jacket is required on every DCKT tour, the entire time you're on the water. Not "recommended." Not "available if you want one." Required, from the moment you launch to the moment you land.

That single rule changes the whole conversation about swimming ability. A Coast Guard-approved life jacket keeps you floating face-up whether or not you know how to swim a stroke. Your guide fits and checks it before you push off, because we don't assume a jacket is on right just because it's on. That's Principle 5 around here: gear checked every outing, life jackets inspected, not assumed.

So the real question isn't "can I swim." It's "will I be wearing a life jacket the whole time." And the answer to that one is always yes.

## Why a sit-on-top kayak changes the math

Every kayak in our fleet is a sit-on-top, meaning an open deck instead of an enclosed cockpit. You sit on top of the boat, legs out, nothing sealing you in.

That matters more than people expect:

- **There's no cockpit to get stuck in.** If a sit-on-top tips, which is uncommon on our protected routes, you slide off the open deck and surface immediately. No wet exit, no trapped legs, no holding your breath while you figure out how to get free.
- **The boats self-drain.** Small holes in the deck, called scupper holes, let water flow through instead of pooling. The kayak doesn't fill up and sink under you.
- **They're wide and stable by design.** These aren't racing boats or whitewater boats. They're built for recreational paddling in calm, protected water, which is exactly what our tours run in.
- **Getting back on is simple.** Two hands on the side, a push, and you're back on the deck. Your guide walks through this in the safety brief and helps if you need it.

Compare that to the mental image most first-timers have, which is some version of a canoe flipping in whitewater. That's not the boat, and it's not the water we paddle.

## What we ask before you ever get in the boat

We don't run a swim test. We do run a real safety brief, and it's short on purpose. Long enough to cover what matters, short enough that you're not standing on the shore rehearsing worst-case scenarios before you've even touched a paddle.

What actually happens before launch:

1. **We fit your life jacket.** Adjusted for your body, checked, not eyeballed.
2. **We ask how you're feeling.** Nervous, first time, not a strong swimmer, whatever it is. Guides are trained to notice this even if you don't say it outright.
3. **We explain the boat.** Open deck, no cockpit, how to get back on if you tip.
4. **We set the pace to the group,** not to the fastest paddler in it. If someone in your group is nervous, the whole tour slows down for them.

A guide who's paying attention will catch a nervous paddler before the launch even starts, the tight grip on the paddle, the quiet questions, the person hanging back from the group. That's the difference between an outfitter who processes you through and one who actually looks at you. We built the whole operation around the second version.

## Still nervous? Here's the tour built for exactly that

If Cave Point still feels like a big first step, start smaller. The [internal link: Eco Estuary tour → /kayak-tours/eco-estuary-tour/] runs on Rieboldt Creek out to Moonlight Bay, glass-flat protected water with no Lake Michigan swell at all. It's the calmest paddle we run, and it's the one we point nervous first-timers and non-swimmers toward most often. Same life jacket rule, same sit-on-top kayaks, same guide-paced group, just a gentler stretch of water to get comfortable on.

Once you've done that one, [internal link: the guided Cave Point kayak tour → /kayak-tours/cave-point-county-park/] and the [internal link: Door Bluff Shipwreck tour → /kayak-tours/door-bluff-county-park-shipwreck/] are both built the same way: life jackets required, sit-on-top kayaks, guides who pace to the group. The water is a little more open, but the safety setup underneath it doesn't change.

This is the same logic behind our most-read post on the site, which answers a different kind of nervous question: [internal link: kayaking while pregnant → /blog/embracing-kayaking-during-pregnancy-a-guide-to-safe-paddling/]. Different anxiety, same honest answer. Tell us what's going on, and we'll tell you straight whether it works and how we'll handle it.

## The one rule that never bends

Skip the swim test worry. You don't need one, and we don't run one. What you need is a life jacket worn the whole time, a sit-on-top kayak with nowhere to get stuck, and a guide who's actually watching the group instead of just counting boats.

That's not us lowering the bar on safety. It's us building the tour so swimming ability was never the bar in the first place.

If you're still not sure, say so when you book. That's not a weird question to us. It's one of the most common ones we get, and it's the easiest one to answer well before you're standing at the launch wondering if you should've spoken up sooner.
```
