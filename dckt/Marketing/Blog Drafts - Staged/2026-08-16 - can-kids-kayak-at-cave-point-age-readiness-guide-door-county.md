## FAQ SCHEMA
_(5 Q&As)_

**Q1:**
```
What is the minimum age to kayak in Door County?
```
**A1:**
```
On Door County Kayak Tours trips, the minimum age is 5 years old. Kids under 5 can't be accommodated, mostly because a life jacket can't be fitted safely on a body that small and a two-hour tour is too long to ask of a toddler. Kids 5 and up ride in a tandem kayak with a participating adult. Whether an older kid can paddle their own single kayak is a call our guide makes at check-in, based on size, ability, and conditions that day, not a fixed age cutoff.
```

**Q2:**
```
Is my kid too young to kayak, even if they meet the age minimum?
```
**A2:**
```
Age minimum and actual readiness are two different questions, and the second one matters more. A 5-year-old who's calm around water, can sit still for 30 to 45 minutes, and listens to a stranger's one-step instructions will probably have a great time. A 7-year-old who melts down if they're bored, or who's never been comfortable getting splashed, might struggle even though they clear the age bar easily. You know your kid better than any age chart does.
```

**Q3:**
```
Can a kid paddle their own kayak or do they have to be in a tandem?
```
**A3:**
```
Every kid 5 and up starts in a tandem with a parent or guardian doing most of the steering. Whether a kid can move to a single kayak of their own is decided by the guide at check-in that day, based on the kid's size, demonstrated paddling ability, and how the water looks. There's no set age where a kid automatically graduates to solo. Some 10-year-olds aren't ready. Some 8-year-olds are.
```

**Q4:**
```
What's the best Door County kayak tour for kids?
```
**A4:**
```
For first-time kid paddlers, the Eco Estuary tour is the better pick. It runs through protected wetland on Rieboldt Creek, glass-flat water, no open-lake swell, and the wildlife (herons, otters, the occasional eagle) keeps kids' attention better than scenery alone. Cave Point is the flagship tour and plenty of families do it with older, water-comfortable kids, but it's a longer paddle in more open water, so it's a bigger ask for a first-timer.
```

**Q5:**
```
What if my kid is too small or too scared to make it through a whole tour?
```
**A5:**
```
Tell your guide before you launch, not after your kid is already crying at the halfway point. Guides can shorten the route, add a shoreline break, or pull a family out early if it's not working, none of which is a big deal to us. It's a bigger deal to force a miserable hour on a kid who wasn't ready, and it's not what we want for your family's one shot at the caves this trip.
```

## CONTENT (Markdown body)

```markdown
"Is my 7-year-old going to be okay out there?" We get some version of that question almost every week, usually from a parent standing at the shop counter with a life jacket already in her hands, still not sure she's making the right call.

Here's the thing nobody tells her: "ages 4+" on a website is not an answer. It's a liability number. It tells you the floor, not whether your specific kid, the one who melts down at the grocery store checkout or the one who's been asking to see a real cave since she was three, is actually ready for two hours in a boat.

We'll give you both. The real minimum, and the readiness questions that matter more than the number.

## The actual minimum age, and why it's 5

Five years old. That's the floor on every DCKT tour, no exceptions below it. Two reasons, and neither one is arbitrary.

A Coast Guard-approved life jacket has to fit snug against a real chest and torso to do its job. Below a certain body size, there isn't a jacket that fits right, and a life jacket that doesn't fit is worse than no plan at all. Second, our tours run 1.5 to 2 hours on the water. That's a long stretch for a toddler's attention span and bladder, kayak or not.

Kids 5 and up ride in a tandem kayak with a parent or guardian doing most of the steering from the back seat. That's not negotiable either. It's the setup that makes the age-5 floor workable in the first place.

## Age gets you in the door. Readiness gets you through the tour.

Here's where most kayak outfitters stop talking and we keep going, because the age number and the actual experience are two different questions.

We've had 5-year-olds who paddled the whole Eco Estuary route wide-eyed and asking when we could come back. We've also had 9-year-olds who were done at the 40-minute mark, not because anything went wrong, just because sitting in a boat isn't their thing. Neither kid did anything wrong. The parent just had different information going in.

A guide's honest read, after years of watching this play out: the kid who does great isn't necessarily the oldest or the biggest. It's the one who's calm in unfamiliar situations and doesn't need constant entertainment.

## The 6-question gut check

Before you book, ask yourself these. Not a test your kid has to pass, just an honest look at what you're signing up for.

1. **Can they sit reasonably still for 30 to 45 minutes at a stretch?** Not motionless, just not needing to stand up and move around every five minutes.
2. **Are they okay getting a little wet?** Splashes happen. A kid who panics at a wet sock is going to have a rough morning.
3. **Have they been around open water before?** A lake, a pool, a beach, anything. This doesn't need to be extensive. It just shouldn't be their first time near water bigger than a bathtub.
4. **Can they follow a one-step instruction from someone who isn't you?** "Paddle on this side" or "stay close to the group" from a guide they just met.
5. **How do they handle a boring stretch?** There will be a few minutes of nothing much happening between the exciting parts. A kid who needs constant stimulation will feel every one of those minutes.
6. **Are they excited, or are you excited for them?** Worth being honest here. A kid who's actually asking about the caves is a different booking than one being brought along.

If you land on mostly yes, you're probably in good shape. A couple of no's doesn't mean don't book. It means come with a plan: shorter expectations, the calmer tour, maybe a first trip that's more about the experience than the mileage.

## Tandem or solo: how that decision actually gets made

Every kid starts in a tandem. Whether an older kid moves to a single kayak of their own is a decision our guide makes at the launch, not something you can lock in when you book online.

The guide looks at the kid's size relative to the boat, how they handle the first few minutes in the tandem, and what the water's doing that day. A calm, protected launch on a still morning gives a guide more room to say yes than a breezy day with some chop. There's no chart for this. It's judgment, made by someone who does this every day and has a real stake in getting it right.

Parents sometimes push for solo because their kid "did it at camp" or "has a kayak at the cabin." We hear that, and we still make the call in person, on the day, on our water. That's not us being difficult. It's the same reason a ski instructor doesn't take your word for it that your kid can handle the black diamond.

## Which tour to book for a first-time kid paddler

If this is your family's first kayak tour and you've got a younger or less experienced kid, [internal link: the Eco Estuary tour → /kayak-tours/eco-estuary-tour/] is the one we'd point you toward. It runs through protected wetland along Rieboldt Creek, water that barely moves, and the route is built around spotting wildlife: herons, otters, sometimes a bald eagle. Kids who'd get bored staring at open water tend to stay locked in when there's something to look for around every bend.

[internal link: The Cave Point kayak tour → /kayak-tours/cave-point-county-park/] is the flagship experience, and families do it with kids all the time. It's also a longer paddle with more open-lake exposure than the Eco Estuary, so it's a bigger ask for a five or six-year-old on their first time in a boat. If your kid has done well on a calmer tour before, or is naturally comfortable on the water, Cave Point is a real option. If you're not sure, start calmer and build up. Nobody's ever regretted an easier first tour.

This is the same logic behind how we handle another nervous booking question, [internal link: kayaking while pregnant → /blog/embracing-kayaking-during-pregnancy-a-guide-to-safe-paddling/]: tell us what's actually going on, and we'll give you a straight answer about which tour fits instead of a generic yes.

## If it's not working once you're out there

Say you did everything right and your kid still hits a wall twenty minutes in. It happens, and it's not a big deal on our end.

Tell your guide right away. Don't wait to see if it passes. A guide can slow the pace, point out something to shift the kid's attention, add a shoreline break, or in some cases end the tour early and paddle back. None of that is a failure. What actually goes badly is when a parent tries to push through a miserable stretch because they already paid and don't want to "waste" the tour. Your guide would rather cut a trip short than watch a family grind through an hour nobody's enjoying.

The kid problem doesn't have a clean, one-size answer, and anyone who tells you it does hasn't actually watched enough families come through the launch. What Door County Kayak Tours can tell you is the real floor (5, tandem, guide's call on solo), the honest readiness questions, and which tour gives a first-timer the best shot at a good day. The rest is you knowing your own kid, which you already do better than we ever will.
```
