## FAQ SCHEMA
_(3-5 Q&As)_

**Q1:**
```
What should I wear kayaking in Door County?
```
**A1:**
```
Wear quick-dry layers and shoes you don't mind getting wet. A synthetic or wool base layer, a light fleece or sun shirt, and a hat work for most summer tours. Skip cotton entirely, since it stays cold and wet once it's splashed. Lake Michigan runs cool even in summer, so dressing a little warmer than the air temperature suggests is the right call. We provide the life jacket and the dry bag.
```

**Q2:**
```
Is Lake Michigan cold for kayaking in summer?
```
**A2:**
```
Yes, colder than most people expect. The lake holds onto winter well into summer, and even in July the water can sit in the 50s or low 60s. You sit on top of the water rather than in it, so it's comfortable for a guided paddle, but you'll feel the temperature if you get splashed or go in. That's why we dress for the water, not just the sunny forecast.
```

**Q3:**
```
What should I bring on a Door County kayak tour?
```
**A3:**
```
Bring water, sunscreen, sunglasses with a strap, a hat, and a full change of dry clothes left in your car for after. Wear shoes that can get wet. Phones go in a dry bag, which you can buy at our shop if you don't have one. Skip anything you'd be upset to lose in the lake, like loose jewelry or a wallet you don't need on the water.
```

**Q4:**
```
Do I need my own gear for a guided kayak tour?
```
**A4:**
```
No. The kayak, paddle, and life jacket are all included, so you show up with your clothes, your water, and yourself. We also provide professional photos on the water. Dry bags and phone dry bags are available to buy at the shop if you want to bring a phone along. Everything else is handled, which is the point of going guided.
```

## CONTENT (Markdown body)

```markdown
The number one thing first-timers get wrong about a Door County kayak tour isn't paddling technique. It's what they wear. People check the forecast, see 80 and sunny, and show up in cotton shorts and a tank top ready for a beach day. Then they feel the water.

Lake Michigan does not care about your forecast. So before you book or pack, here's exactly what to wear and bring, and the handful of things to leave at home.

## The lake is colder than the forecast

Here's the part nobody puts on a postcard. Lake Michigan holds onto winter for a long time. Even in July, the water near our shoreline can sit in the 50s or low 60s while the air is 80 and gorgeous. It's a big, deep, cold lake, and it warms up slowly.

For a guided paddle that's totally fine, because you sit on top of the water, not in it. But you will feel that temperature the second a wave splashes over the bow or you dip a hand in. We've watched plenty of guests in shorts realize about ten minutes in that they dressed for the beach, not the lake. Nobody's miserable, but they're cooler than they need to be, and that's avoidable.

The rule we give everyone: **dress for the water, not the weather report.**

## What to wear, top to bottom

You don't need fancy paddling gear for a summer tour. You need the right basics. Here's what actually works:

- **Base layer:** a synthetic or wool top that dries fast. A sun shirt is perfect. It blocks UV and dries in minutes.
- **A light layer to add or shed:** a thin fleece or a windbreaker. Mornings on the water run cool even in July, and you can tie it around your waist once you warm up.
- **Bottoms:** quick-dry shorts or athletic pants. Swim trunks work. Yoga pants are fine.
- **Shoes that can get wet:** water shoes, old sneakers, or sport sandals with a heel strap. You'll be stepping in shallow water at the launch.
- **A hat with a brim** and, if it's breezy, something to keep it on your head.

That's it. You don't need a wetsuit for a summer guided tour. You need layers that don't quit the moment they get wet.

## The one fabric to leave at home

Cotton. Just don't.

A cotton t-shirt or cotton sweatpants will soak up the first splash and stay cold and clammy for the rest of the paddle. There's an old saying in the outdoor world that cotton kills, and while a summer tour on Lake Michigan isn't life-or-death, the principle holds: wet cotton makes you cold and stays that way. Swap it for anything synthetic or wool and you've solved 90% of the comfort problem before you even reach the water.

## What to bring (and leave in the car)

Pack light. You're getting in a kayak, not loading a campsite. Here's the short list:

1. **Water.** You'll be in the sun and working a little, even on an easy paddle. Bring a bottle.
2. **A full change of dry clothes.** Leave it in the car for after. Even if you don't fully get wet, slipping into dry clothes post-paddle feels great.
3. **A towel** in the car for the same reason.
4. **Sunscreen,** applied before you launch.
5. **Sunglasses with a strap.** The glare off the water is real, and unstrapped sunglasses end up on the lakebed. We've seen it many times.

Leave anything you'd be upset to lose. Loose jewelry, a watch you care about, a wallet stuffed with stuff you don't need on the water. The lake keeps what it takes.

## What we handle so you don't have to

This is the easy part, and it's most of the gear. When you book a [internal link: guided Cave Point kayak tour → /kayak-tours/cave-point-county-park/], the kayak, paddle, and life jacket are all included. So are professional photos on the water, which beats trying to take your own and risking a phone in the lake.

Speaking of phones: if you want to bring one, it goes in a dry bag. We sell dry bags and phone-specific dry bags at the shop if you don't have one, but honestly some of our favorite tours are the ones where guests leave the phone in the car and just look around. Up to you.

The point of going guided is that you show up with your clothes and yourself, and the rest is handled. No gear rental upsells, no surprise fees.

## Sunscreen, sunglasses, and the small stuff people forget

A few last things experience has taught us to call out, because they're the ones people kick themselves over:

- **Reapply sunscreen.** The water reflects sun back up at you, so you burn faster than on land. The backs of your hands and your face take the worst of it.
- **Strap your glasses.** Mentioned it already. Mentioning it again because it happens on nearly every trip.
- **Tie down your hat** if there's any wind.
- **Tell us if you're pregnant, nervous, or new to this.** We adjust. If you're paddling while expecting, we wrote a whole guide on [internal link: kayaking while pregnant → /blog/embracing-kayaking-during-pregnancy-a-guide-to-safe-paddling/] for exactly that reason.

None of this is complicated. It's just the stuff that's obvious to people who do this every day and invisible to people doing it for the first time.

## Dress for the water and the rest takes care of itself

Strip all of this down to one sentence and it's this: skip the cotton, wear quick-dry layers, leave dry clothes in the car, and let us handle the gear.

Do that, and the only thing you'll be thinking about on the water is the water. That's the whole idea.
```
