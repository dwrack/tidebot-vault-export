# DCKT Customer Avatar — Built From Real Customer Voice

**Source:** Google Voice export, 2016–2020 (Apres Vacations / DCKT business line)
**Corpus mined:** 2,380 voicemail transcripts + 8,950 incoming customer texts
**Analyst:** Built 2026-05-05 by grepping the actual words customers used — not personas, not surveys, real calls and texts

---

## TL;DR — The composite

She's a 35–55 year old mom from Wisconsin or the Chicago/Milwaukee/Minneapolis suburbs (920, 414, 608, 312, 630, 773, 847, 612 area codes dominate). She's on vacation in Door County for 3–7 days and has been Googling "things to do." Kayaking sounds magical, especially the cave tour, but she's scared of two things: tipping over, and the weather not cooperating. She has at least one kid in tow (often 7–12) and her husband is on board but won't be the one calling. She wants to talk to a human before she pays. She's looking at the website right now, on her phone, in a vacation rental.

The second-most common avatar: a couple in their 60s–70s celebrating an anniversary, just as cautious about water and physical ability, and even more reliant on a phone call.

Third: a bachelorette/birthday party organizer trying to coordinate 6–12 women on one specific day.

## Pains

**1. "I can't book online without talking to someone first."**
Half the voicemails open with some version of "I want to book but I have a few questions first." The website doesn't answer them. The booking widget is too final to use as research.

> "Hi my name is Diane Rose, and I have some questions about the cave point Kayak tours if you could call me back. I'm not going to be able to book it online cuz I need these questions answered first."

> "Before I booked online I just wanted to talk to somebody."

**2. "Where do I actually go?"** (167 voicemails, hundreds of texts)
Meeting location is the single most common text question. The address, parking, "is the launch the same as the meeting place," directions from Egg Harbor / Fish Creek / Sister Bay.

> "Where do we meet for the tour?"
> "Can you verify the address? 5890 WI-57 sturgeon Bay wi 54235"
> "When we get to the park were do we go from there"
> "How far is the meeting location from egg harbor?"

**3. The weather is the #1 booking killer.** (219 voicemails)
Customers obsessively watch the forecast. Wind is the word they use, not weather. They want a human read on whether it's "really going to be too rough" or whether DCKT is just being cautious.

> "I'm a little worried about the weather. It doesn't seem to be clearing and it's supposed to be winds out of the north at 10 miles an hour all day."
> "We are concerned about the wind. Wondering if it will be too rough."
> "I'm a novice paddler and I just want to make sure I'm understanding it correctly… I just look like somebody who knows more about this area and the adventure than I do to talk to me about it."

**4. Reschedule chaos.** (374 voicemails — biggest single theme)
Customers have to reschedule constantly because of weather, sickness, car trouble, or because the trip got pushed. They want it to be painless and they're stressed about deposits, photo packages, and gift certificates surviving the change.

> "Our tour was canceled for tomorrow… can we reschedule for the following Sunday? What happens to the photo deposit?"
> "Need to cancel my reservation for Saturday, I'm not able to find a way to get the tire cuz back and forth."

**5. The kid problem.** (100+ voicemails)
Every parent wants the same answer: "Is my kid old enough?" They're terrified of either being turned away at the launch or having a meltdown 90 minutes in.

> "Concerned about our 5 year old making it 2 hours without a potty break."
> "We've got a 7 year old who can paddle around a little bit, but we were wondering if you had like a tandem kayak that she and my wife could take out together."
> "Just had some questions about how kid-friendly we are. We've got a 5 year old and a 10 year old."

## Problems (the friction the customer is solving for)

- **Trip planning under pressure.** They're already in Door County or driving up. They have 1–3 days to make a tour happen. Weather is a wildcard.
- **Coordinating multiple people** with different ability levels (Grandma + the 7-year-old + the cousin who hates water).
- **Booking systems that don't tell them what they need to know:** Is this OK for a 5yo? What if it rains? Where do I park? Can I bring my dog?
- **Last-minute changes** (sick kid, flat tire, husband stuck in a meeting, weather pivot).
- **Gift certificate redemption** — they were given one, the code doesn't work online, expiration is approaching.

> "I have a gift certificate that is supposed to expire next Saturday for a kayak tour and I'm looking to come up there with a friend, but I heard this Saturday may not be an option."

## Goals

- **Have the moment.** They want the cave-paddle photo. The "we did it" memory. The thing their friends in Illinois will ask about.
- **Not screw up the vacation day.** This is one of 4–6 things they're trying to fit in. If it gets canceled they lose a whole afternoon.
- **Look competent in front of their kids/spouse/in-laws.** They don't want to be the one who picked the wrong tour and made everyone miserable.
- **Tick the bucket list.** "Always wanted to kayak the caves." It's a Door County rite of passage in their head.
- **Mark the occasion** (anniversary, 75th birthday, bachelorette, family reunion). For these, a "nice" generic outing isn't enough.

> "We're going to be up there for our anniversary."
> "This is my 75th birthday present from my kids."
> "Bachelorette party in May, group of 9, can you give me a call back."

---

## Desires (what they're really buying)

- **Reassurance.** That they won't tip. That the kid will be fine. That the guide will handle them.
- **A guide who knows more than they do.** They explicitly say it: "somebody who knows more about this area and the adventure than I do." They don't want gear rental; they want a human who can read the water and tell them what to do.
- **A photogenic, share-worthy experience.** Cave shots, sea kayak shots, sunset shots. The number of callers asking specifically about the photo package shows this is a primary desire, not an upsell.
- **The Door County signature experience.** The caves are mentioned in 17% of all voicemails — it's THE thing.
- **Calm water and a soft launch.** Most haven't kayaked before; "easy" is the magic word.

---

## Experience level (the data shocked me a little)

The vast majority of callers self-identify as **beginners or absolute first-timers.** This is not an expert paddler market. It's a vacation activity market.

- "We've never kayaked before"
- "We're beginners"
- "I'm a novice"
- "She's never been in a kayak"
- "Just had a few questions, we've never done the kayak experience"
- "16-year-old has never been kayaking, she's a little concerned"

The few who say they're experienced are usually solo travelers asking about advanced routes, or locals. **The marketing reality: this is a "I've never done this" audience, not a paddler audience.**

Implication: every page, every ad, every IG caption should pass the "would a complete first-timer feel safe?" test. The competitive edge is not "best tour in Door County" — it's **"the tour to do if you've never paddled before."**

## Objections (in order of frequency)

### 1. "I'm worried about tipping over." / "I'm scared of the water."
The single most common emotional objection. Often phrased as "concerned" by the booker on behalf of a nervous spouse, kid, or friend.

> "She is mostly concerned about tipping over, so will that make a difference?"
> "I was kind of scared so I'd like to get a refund."
> "Have a 16 year old beginner that's concerned about doing cave tour."

**How to handle:** Sit-on-tops, calm-water defaults, the safety record (24 years, 2,000+ 5-star reviews), guide ratio. Don't pretend the fear isn't real — name it and show why DCKT picked sit-on-tops specifically (the story David already tells about the larger guest tipping in a sit-in is gold here).

### 2. "What if the weather is bad?"
The constant fear that they'll book and get rained out, or get out there and find the water too rough.

> "I'm a little worried about the weather."
> "We are concerned about the wind."
> "I'm worried we would have our trip cut short."

**How to handle:** Lead with the free reschedule / 24-hour cancellation. Make it visible above the fold, not buried in policies. Show that the safety call is a feature, not a bug.

### 3. "Is my kid old enough / big enough / capable?"
Explicit ages range from 3 to 16. Real worry about potty breaks, meltdowns, weight, height, ability to follow instructions.

> "Wondering about how kid-friendly we are."
> "We have a 4 in our party two adults and then two sixteen-year-olds."

**How to handle:** A clear age and weight matrix on every tour page. "Best for families with kids 7+" beats "all ages welcome." Specific minimum age + tandem availability called out by name.

### 4. "How much, exactly?" (and the gift certificate question)
Price isn't a primary objection but they want certainty. The most common confusion: per kayak vs per person.

> "Is it $55 per person or per kayak? Are there two person kayaks?"
> "I have a gift certificate, the code doesn't work online."

**How to handle:** Per-person pricing stated explicitly on every tour page. Gift certificate redemption that doesn't break.

### 5. "Where do I go and where do I park?"
A logistics objection, but it absolutely stops people from clicking book.

> "Where do we meet for the tour?"
> "When we get to the park where do we go from there?"

**How to handle:** Map + photo of the launch on every confirmation email. Pre-tour text 24 hours out with the meeting pin. (You may already do this, but the volume of these messages says either it's not arriving or it's not enough.)

### 6. "Can I bring my dog?"
Surprisingly common. Usually a "no" but people keep asking.

### 7. "Are you still running this late in the season?"
Late-September and October callers are a real segment, scared they're past the cutoff.

## What this should change in the marketing

1. **The website needs to answer "I have questions before I book"** — better FAQs, more weather/safety reassurance above the fold, an easy "text us a question" button. The volume of "call me back" voicemails says the booking funnel is leaking at the question stage.
2. **Lead with "first time? perfect."** Not "expert tours." The audience is overwhelmingly beginners.
3. **Cancellation/reschedule policy needs to be the headline reassurance** on every tour page, not buried in fine print. It's the #1 unspoken objection.
4. **Tipping fear needs a direct, named answer** — sit-on-top kayaks, the David story, why this isn't whitewater.
5. **Kids policy should be on every tour page with specific ages**, not "family-friendly" which means nothing.
6. **Meeting location needs photos and a map** at the top of the post-booking flow, not just an address.
7. **Cave tour is THE flagship.** 17% of all calls reference it specifically. Build the funnel around it; everything else is secondary.

**Same-day or next-day mentions outnumber "next week / next weekend / next month" by 22:1.**

This is a **hot, in-market, mobile-first inquiry channel.** She is in Door County (or driving in) and is making a tour decision in real time. She's checking weather, looking at availability, and calling from a cottage living room or a passenger seat.

**Time of day (CT):**
- Calls start at 6am CT, peak 10am–12pm and again 4–5pm
- Almost nothing after 7pm

- **Friday + Saturday are peaks** (387 + 409 voicemails)

- Jul (586) + Aug (736) = **56% of all voicemails**

- October still has 118 voicemails — the late-season segment is real
- May (147) is when planning starts

**Implication:** The peak-season ad budget should weight Jul–Aug heavily, but the smart spend layers in **September** as a near-equal priority, not an afterthought.

## The hidden segments

Things the data surfaced that don't fit the three primary avatars:

### "Locals on a weekend" (~19% of all callers)
The 920 caller. Could be from Green Bay, Appleton, Sturgeon Bay, Sevastopol. Lower trip-planning anxiety. Cares more about "any spots Saturday afternoon?" than "what should I wear?" Probably more price-sensitive. Possibly a repeat customer.

> "Hi, we're up in egg Harbor next weekend. The weather's not necessarily locked in as being beautiful. What your day-of rental availability looks like..."

### "Pregnant / babymoon" (small but distinct)
A real segment. They ask explicitly if it's safe. Currently the SEO content is doing well on this query (per memory: DCKT wins on "kayaking while pregnant"), so the page is doing its job — but the call volume says they still want a person to confirm it's OK.

> "I'm pregnant. I just wanted to make sure that would be OK for me to do."

### "Multi-gen with elderly parent"
Adult kid bringing parents in their 70s. The booker is worried about the parent, not themselves. Looking for tandem options, slow pace, no surprises.

> "We have two elderly people who I doubt will be able to kayak, so I want to see if you have those tandem kayaks…"

### "Bachelorette / friend group of 6–12"
Disproportionately Saturday morning. Booker has zero kayak experience but is responsible for 8 women's day. Wants a private tour or a guaranteed group time. Books 4–8 weeks ahead (the rare planner).

### "We brought our own kayaks / we're already kayakers"
Small but real. Different ask entirely — they want to know the put-in, the route, the wind read. The DCKT website has nothing for this audience right now and they're calling to ask. Could be a guide-services upsell or a "we'll meet you at the launch" product.

### "Lost item / left something behind"
A surprising number of voicemails are post-tour, looking for a phone, wallet, jacket, photo download. **This is a reputation-management opportunity.** A clear "left something? text us at..." flow on the post-tour email/SMS would prevent the angry voicemails like the Karen Hash key one and the lost wallet calls.

### "Yelp / TourPPC / business pitch spam"
Worth flagging: a non-trivial chunk of voicemails are sales calls (Yelp, NBC 26, J-1 visa office, payroll companies). They're noise to the customer-voice analysis but worth knowing the business gets a lot of inbound sales pressure.

## Decision-triggers — what tips her from "looking" to "booking"

Reading between the lines of the voicemails, the moments the booking happens are:

1. **A human picks up or texts back fast.** The voicemails are "I called and you didn't answer, so now I have to leave a message." If a text response comes within minutes, the booking happens. If the voicemail goes 4 hours without a callback, she calls Lakeshore Adventures.
2. **A specific, named reassurance about her exact concern.** Not "we have a great safety record." Specifically: "your 8-year-old does great in our tandem with mom in the back."
3. **A green light on weather.** She's looking for permission to commit. "Tomorrow looks like calm 5–10mph winds, perfect for the cave tour" gets her to click Book.
4. **A clear price-per-person.** "It's $69 per person, $138 for the two of you" beats anything ambiguous.
5. **A meeting-place pin she can hand to her husband.** Once she has the address and a photo, the husband stops asking.

## Updated copy / marketing implications

(in addition to the 7 from V1)

**8. Wisconsin is the #1 ad audience, not Chicago.** The marketing instinct is to chase Chicago because it's bigger, but the data says Wisconsin metros (Milwaukee, Madison, Fox Valley) outperform Chicago by ~3:1. Reweight the ad geo accordingly.

**9. Build for "I'm here today and want to book this afternoon."** The booking flow needs to function on a phone, in a car, in a cottage with bad WiFi, while she's holding a sandwich. Not on a desktop in March.

**10. Text-back is the killer feature.** The voicemails are people who called and got voicemail. Most of them would have texted if they could. A "text us at 555..." button on every page with sub-15-minute response would convert a meaningful chunk of these calls.

**11. September deserves equal billing with June in marketing calendars.** It outperforms June for inbound demand. The fall foliage / shoulder-season story should be a real campaign, not an afterthought.

**12. Co-market with vacation rentals in Fish Creek and Egg Harbor.** A rack-card / QR-code partnership with property managers there reaches the customer at the moment she's planning her week.

**13. Build a real referral program.** Word-of-mouth is the largest single referral source. There's no reason DCKT shouldn't be paying $10–15 per converted referral and tracking it. "Refer a friend, both get $20 off" would compound.

**14. Speak to her, not them.** Web copy, ad copy, IG captions — the audience is a 35–55 woman from Wisconsin or the Chicago burbs, planning a family vacation. "Bring the family" not "for adventure seekers."

**15. The cave tour gets its own funnel.** Separate landing page, separate ad copy, separate SEO focus. Other tours can share a hub. Cave is the only one that warrants dedicated budget.
