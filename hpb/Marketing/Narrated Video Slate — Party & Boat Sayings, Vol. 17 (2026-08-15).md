## How this was written

Two-pass process, same as every prior volume, run fresh against this batch. `/copy-mentors` and `/copywriting-2.0` were both loaded fresh for this pass before any line was drafted.

1. **`/copy-mentors`** — master routing by piece:
   - **Schwartz's identification principle** drove 260, 262, 263, 265, 266, 270, and 272 — each assumes the viewer already recognizes the moment (someone asking to go faster, the photo at the wheel, a shanty running out of words, ice hitting the deck, "bless your heart" landing two ways, a wake rocking every cup at once, the last look at the dock) and just names it rather than arguing it's true.
   - **Ogilvy's factual-specific-over-adjective principle** shaped the etymology cluster directly — 257, 258, and 259 each open with a plain factual claim about where the phrase actually comes from (a sheet is a rope, not a sail; battens secured real hatch covers; scuttlebutt was a literal water cask) before the joke lands on the modern version.
   - **Halbert's conversational directness** drove 264's and 269's closers — "You'll hear him before you ever see him" and "Book it before the parade's even scheduled" are flat, unhedged statements.
   - **Sugarman's slippery-slide short openers** drove 261 and 271 — each opens with a short, plain, easy-to-swallow factual line before the bit builds.
   - **Blair Warren's 27 words** (applied during drafting, from copywriting-2.0): 268 leans on "allay their fears" from the couple's own side — a renewal doesn't need to out-produce the original wedding to count. 269 leans on "justify their failures/confirm their suspicions" from the fan's side — of course the boat fills up the second the parade's over, that's just how this city works.

2. **`/copywriting-2.0`** — every spoken line run through the 3-test filter (can I visualize it, can I falsify it, could nobody else say it). What that pass caught and fixed in this batch specifically:
   - **The most consequential catch: an exact-phrase collision with piece 177.** Piece 267's first draft closed on "We just bring the boat. You bring the rest," which is a near-verbatim reuse of piece 177's own closing line, "We just bring the boat" (Vol. 12). Caught on a targeted content check (not just the title-level pass) once 267's premise — BYOB — was flagged as adjacent to 177's. Rewritten to close on "Wide open. That's the actual rule," and the body line now leans on "nobody's checking what's in the cooler" instead, which also shifts the piece from 177's surprise-reveal angle to a scope-clarification angle (does BYOB cover food and mixers, not just booze) — a genuinely different question than 177 answers.
   - **Fixed a hedge that broke the direct-statement rule in piece 260.** The first draft closed "That's kind of the whole point," which hedges with "kind of" and reads soft. Rewritten to "That's the whole business model, actually" — unhedged, specific, and funnier because it names the thing (a business model) instead of gesturing at a vague point.
   - **Fixed an exact opener collision between 260 and 261.** Both first drafts opened "Every trip, somebody..." — caught on a full read-through of this batch's sixteen openers against each other, the same discipline Vol. 16 applied when it caught 249 and 251 sharing a "two hours..." frame. Piece 261 was rewritten to open "The air horn gets found before the safety briefing even starts," which drops the shared frame and leads with the object instead of the pattern.
