## Why these 16 topics

- **Fifteen of sixteen pieces are built around a specific, everyday idiom that actually started on a boat** — the pitch this pass is that a phrase people already say on land (dead in the water, all hands on deck, son of a gun) gets a literal, funny payoff the moment it's said on an actual boat. That's a different comedic engine than most of Vols. 1-21, which built more often from a recurring social moment (a toast, a rule, a character) than from a phrase.
- **One piece breaks the idiom pattern on purpose**: 352, the closer, uses "fair-weather friend" the way this series' closers always work — sincere, non-comedic, closing register — but doesn't play the phrase for the literal-boat joke the other fifteen do. It plays it straight, matching the tradition set by 240, 256, 272, 288, 304, 319, and 336.
- **Three pieces double back on the cast itself rather than a fresh face**: 345 and 346 are a matched pair (Old Salt says "son of a gun," the Groomsman admits two summers on this boat taught it to him), and 348 gives the Regular a second, unrelated deadpan bit about dock courtesy. This series has eleven named recurring characters now; using two of them to riff off each other in the same volume is new for this series and worth doing sparingly.
- **No piece in this batch requires advance guest coordination or a release beyond the standard opportunistic-filming waiver** — a deliberate choice after Vol. 21 flagged two pieces (325, 332) that needed a pre-cleared real booking group. Every piece in Vol. 22 can be shot on any normal trip.

## How this was written

The `/copy-mentors` and `/copywriting-2.0` skills were both loaded directly for this pass (not applied from memory) before a line of copy was drafted.

1. **Copy-mentors framing, master routing by piece:**
   - **Schwartz's identification principle** (name the moment, don't argue it) drove 337, 339, 343, and 347 — each assumes the viewer already half-recognizes the beat (the group's designated wildcard, the friend who didn't read the group chat, the dead-silent song request, the cannonball countdown) and just states it rather than explaining it.
   - **Ogilvy's factual-specific-over-adjective principle** shaped 338 and 340 — 338 opens on "toe the line, that's not a metaphor" instead of "we have safety rules," and 340 builds the whole joke on one specific, small task (a speaker moving ten feet) instead of a vague "we all pitch in."
   - **Bencivenga's specificity-builds-belief principle** shaped 342 and 349 — a specific detail (forty-two songs queued by six people; breakfast tacos before boarding, lunch on the boat, dessert also somehow on the boat) reads as more credible and funnier than a generalized version of either bit.
   - **Halbert's conversational directness** drove 345's and 351's closers — "Good jib. Every trip's got one." and "Either way, it's accurate." are flat, unhedged statements delivered like a person talking, not a brand.
   - **Sugarman's slippery-slide short openers** drove 341 and 350 — "Every planner has a line for the nervous ones. Mine is always the same." and "Word of the day for the morning trip: groggy." both open short and pull the viewer into the payoff.
   - **Blair Warren's 27 words** (applied during the copywriting-2.0 pass) shaped 344 and 352 — both lean on the audience already recognizing the feeling (a toast said knowing the water won't always be smooth; the double meaning of "fair-weather friend") rather than arguing the sentiment.

2. **Copywriting-2.0 3-test filter, applied line by line and cross-checked against the batch and against all 336 prior pieces:**
   - **Hedge caught and cut in piece 349.** A first draft of the closer read "Nobody's ever really left hungry," which hedges with "really." Rewritten to "Nobody's ever once left hungry" — a flat, checkable-sounding claim in the planner's own voice, not a marketing promise.
   - **Ownability check on all fifteen idiom pieces.** Every idiom used (loose cannon, toe the line, etc.) is public-domain English, so the test that actually matters here isn't "can nobody else say this phrase," it's "can nobody else say it about a two-hour deck party the way this crew just did." Each piece was rewritten until the payoff was specific to this boat (the painted line on deck, the exact rope motion, the cooler running dry) rather than a generic idiom explainer that any account could post.
   - **Visualize-it check on 343 and 347.** Early drafts of both leaned on the idea of the moment ("everyone stops dancing," "everyone jumps") rather than a shot anyone could see in their head. Rewritten to name the physical beat: nobody moving a hip, the three-two-one countdown and the actual splash.
   - **Cross-checked every opener in this batch against the other 15** — none share an opening sentence shape or frame with another piece in this same volume.
   - **Cross-checked closing lines against prior volumes' near-collisions** — no closing line in this batch shares more than incidental word overlap with any prior volume's closer.
   - **Title collisions caught before drafting, not after** (see Pass note above) — two candidate premises were dropped pre-draft once each turned up a near-match against an existing title or an existing joke structure.
