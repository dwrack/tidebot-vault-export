**Q1:**

```
How does a Houston party barge work?
```
**A1:**
```
A Houston party barge is a captained 26-passenger boat on Clear Lake in Seabrook, Texas. A licensed captain drives a real motor, so guests don't power anything. Cruises run 1 hour 45 minutes and pass the Kemah Boardwalk by water. You bring your own drinks and food. The boat has a roof, a bathroom, and a Bluetooth sound system.
```

**Q2:**

```
Do you have to pedal the whole time on a party barge?
```

**A2:**
```
No. The boats are fully motorized and a captain handles the driving. The pedal stations are there as an optional bit of fun, and most people use them for about fifteen minutes before grabbing another drink and sitting down. You can ignore them completely and still have the full experience.
```

```
Both boats launch from Clear Lake in the Seabrook and Kemah area, minutes from the Kemah Boardwalk. The cruise stays on open water with skyline and bay views the whole way. Parking is at the launch dock, and the captain gives the safety rundown before you push off.
```

## CONTENT (Markdown body)

```markdown
"Wait, do we have to pedal the whole time?"

It's the first thing people ask, and I get it. The name throws everyone. So let's clear it up before anything else: no. You don't power the boat. A licensed captain drives a real motor while you stand around with a drink and watch the Houston skyline slide by. That's the whole deal.

Houston has a waterfront. Most people who live here forget that. They picture traffic, heat, and the same three restaurants. Meanwhile there's a 26-person boat doing loops on Clear Lake every weekend and half the city has no idea it exists. Let me walk you through how it actually works.

## What a party barge actually is

A party barge is a flat, stable, roofed boat built for a group to hang out on. Ours run 30 feet, hold up to 26 guests, and sit on Clear Lake out by Seabrook, minutes from the Kemah Boardwalk. Think of it less like a tour and more like renting a backyard that happens to float and have a great view.

There are two of them. **Delta Dream** runs cleaner and brighter. **Mama Tried** has a little more edge to it. Same capacity, same setup, different vibe. If your group is bigger than 26, we can run both at once and keep everybody together on the water.

What it's not: it's not a yacht, it's not a cruise line, and it's not a bar. Nobody hands you a buffet or a dress code. You bring your people, your cooler, and your playlist. We handle the rest.

## The captain drives, you don't

Here's the part that costs us bookings when people get it wrong. The boats are **fully motorized**. There's a USCG-certified captain at the helm the entire time. You are not the engine.

The pedal stations? They're there for fun, like a built-in thing to mess around with. In a typical trip, a couple people hop on, pedal for maybe fifteen minutes, take a few photos, then go grab another drink and sit down. Nobody's breaking a sweat. If you never touch them, the boat goes exactly the same speed and you have exactly the same trip.

I've watched groups show up genuinely nervous they signed up for a workout. By minute ten they're laughing about it. So if that's the thing holding you back, let it go.

## What 1 hour 45 on Clear Lake looks like

A cruise runs **one hour and forty-five minutes**. Long enough to actually unwind, short enough that nobody's checking their watch. Here's roughly how it goes:

- You meet at the launch dock, captain does a quick safety rundown, you load your cooler.
- Boat pushes off into open water. Music's already going off your phone over the Bluetooth speakers.
- You cruise past the Kemah Boardwalk by water, which is an angle most Houstonians have literally never seen.
- Sunset trips hit golden hour over Galveston Bay, and the group photos basically take themselves.
- You circle back to the dock with a phone full of pictures and a half-empty cooler.

A guest told one of our captains last season, "I've lived here ten years and never seen the skyline from out here." That reaction happens almost every trip. It's the entire point.

## What's on the boat

People want to know what they're working with before they book. So, plainly:

- **A roof.** Houston sun is no joke. You've got shade.
- **A bathroom.** Real one, on board. Big deal for a 105-minute trip.
- **A Bluetooth sound system.** Your playlist, your volume.
- **Party lights** for evening cruises.
- **Coolers welcome.** Bring drinks, bring food, bring a full charcuterie spread if that's your thing. People do.
- **A captain and crew** who actually want the group to have a good time, not just punch a clock.

## Who shows up on these things

Honestly, all kinds. The boat bends to whatever the group wants. A bachelorette crew blasting Megan Thee Stallion gets one kind of trip. A corporate team that wants to talk and chill gets a different one. We've had birthday groups from age 8 on up (kids are welcome), retirement parties, first dates, you name it.

The common thread is people who wanted something better than dinner-and-a-movie for the fourth time. **Houston's default answer to "what should we do" is too often "I dunno, get food?"** This is the thing you actually tell your friends about later.

## Booking it without overthinking it

Two ways in. Grab public tickets per person if it's just you and a few friends, or book a **private charter** for the whole boat if you've got a group. Private charters start around $800 for up to 26 people, with a small deposit to lock the date and weekday discounts if you're flexible. A group of 20 at a Houston bar runs an $800 tab easy, so for a private boat with your own drinks, the math is friendly.

Peak summer dates fill up fast, especially Saturday sunsets. If you've got a weekend in mind, book it now rather than three days out and hope.

Houston has a waterfront. Get on it.
```
