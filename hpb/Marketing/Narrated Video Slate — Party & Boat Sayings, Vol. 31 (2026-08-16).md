## Pass note — pass 93, Vol. 31, pieces 481-496

**Dedup filter for this pass:** rather than re-reading all 480 existing NOTES.txt files in full, an Explore agent read the "Pass note" / device section of all 30 prior volumes and returned a compact device registry. Nine volumes (22 through 30) each centered on one named structural device: nautical-idiom-literal-payoff (22), overheard quotable line (23), small system run within one trip (24), hand-me-down object/role passed across trips (25), the instant ruling that settles a dispute (26), the standing prophecy/wager (27), the running tally (28), the guest-coined word that spreads (29), and the on-the-spot award (30). Volumes 1-21 predate the "one named device per volume" convention and worked from occasion-type sweeps and real-source mining instead.

**The device this volume: the message relay.** An announcement, request, or piece of news gets passed person-to-person down the length of the boat — or boat-to-boat over the radio — and arrives mutated at the far end. The comedic payoff is always the specific mangled version and what someone does in response to it, landing cold with no narrator explaining that a relay happened. This is structurally distinct from all nine prior named devices: an idiom explains a phrase, a coined word invents new vocabulary that sticks going forward, a tally counts, a prophecy predicts before the fact, an award crowns a winner, a ruling settles a dispute, a handoff moves an object across trips/years. A relay is none of those — it's one message, said once, degrading in real time as it crosses one boat, one trip. Distribution: 3 pieces per bucket (deck-hand slang, captain lines, bachelorette-party lines, toasts, running jokes) plus the closer, matching the even split used since Vol. 24.

## How this was written

`/copy-mentors` and `/copywriting-2.0` were both run fresh for this pass before a line of copy was drafted.

**Copy-mentors framing:** this is a Halbert-and-Sugarman problem, not an Ogilvy one — these are fifteen-second spoken scripts that have to sound like something actually said out loud on a moving boat, not persuasive prose. Halbert's kitchen-table rule set the register for every archetype: the deckhand talks fast and half out of breath, the captain talks dry and unbothered, the bridal party talks loud and half-laughing at itself, the toast-giver talks a beat too formal until it breaks. Sugarman's Slippery Slide governed the shape of all 16: line 1 is the original message, said once, clean, no setup; line 2 is the visible hand-off, mouth to ear, moving down the boat; line 3 is the arrival — the mangled version, delivered cold, no narrator flagging that it changed; line 4 is the specific, checkable reaction. Blair Warren's 27 words did real work in three pieces specifically: 488 confirms a suspicion everyone already has about bachelorette-party guests and cash, 484 allays the mild fear of getting caught with contraband snacks, and 486 lets the group throw a joke rock at the captain's deadpan storm announcements.

**Copywriting-2.0, 3-test filter applied line by line, catches made during drafting:**
- **Narrator-summary catch, systemic.** First drafts of 482, 487, and 493 all opened with a line explaining that a message was being passed down the boat ("word starts moving down the deck…") before delivering the original message itself. Rewritten so line 1 is always the real, clean message, stated once, cold — the relay only becomes visible in line 2.
- **Vague-payoff catch on 483 and 495.** 483's first draft closed "and everyone had a laugh about it" — cut for being exactly the wallpaper phrase this series keeps flagging — rewritten to a specific, visualizable beat: the group actually scanning faces for whose stepmom is on board. 495's first draft closed "the list kept growing the whole way back" (unfalsifiable, no count) — rewritten to a specific number, eight items, and the cooler guy visibly checking a mental list against what's in his hands.
- **Falsifiability catch on 491 and 494.** 491's first draft had the captain getting put on the spot "every single toast" — softened to one specific toast, once, since a standing pattern is a bigger claim than a fifteen-second script should carry. 494's first draft claimed the cannonball story "spread across the whole dock" — cut as unverifiable — rewritten to the specific, checkable chain: it crosses exactly four people before it reaches the bow.
- **Genericness catch, applied to the whole batch.** Every piece checked against whether it could belong to any boat business. 481 and 482 anchor to the actual BYOB-cooler and dock-return sequence specific to how Houston Pedal Barge trips run. 488 and 489 anchor to bachelorette-specific rituals (the sash, the shoe game) rather than a generic "party" beat. 493 anchors to two boats passing on the same lake, which only reads as real for an operator running more than one trip in the same water at once.
- **Repetition catch across the batch.** First drafts of 481 and 493 both used a salute/military-adjacent sight gag as the closing beat — flagged as too close inside one volume. 493 was rewritten so the payoff is the other boat waving and cheering instead of a salute, keeping 481's salute gag exclusive to that piece.
- **Mishearing-plausibility check, new this volume.** Every mutated message was tested for whether the sound-shift is actually plausible over engine noise and wind, not just funny on the page: cooler/Colonel, dock/doc, step/step-mom, wake/cake, buoy/boy (band), storm's/warp, fired/fired (kept, escalated by "get the manager" not the sound itself), sash/cash, shoe/sushi, Mark and Elena/markets and lemurs. Two early candidates were cut for failing this — "aft" mishearing as "half" (no real acoustic path) and "anchor" as "anger" (too big a leap for one relay).
