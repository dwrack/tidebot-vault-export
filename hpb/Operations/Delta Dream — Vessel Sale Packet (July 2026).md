## What I still need from you

I built the listing off the vault. These gaps have to be filled before it goes live, and getting them wrong in a listing is how a deal blows up at survey:

- **Hull ID / USCG Official Number** (the brand docs say 2021 in one place and you said 2022, so confirm the model year off the documentation)
- **Engine:** make, HP, hours. Trident's 26-pax platform typically ships with a 90HP Suzuki 4-stroke. Confirm what's actually hanging off the transom.
- **COI status:** expiration date, next drydock due, any outstanding requirements or 835 items from the last inspection
- **Documentation:** USCG documented or state titled? Documented vessels transfer through the National Vessel Documentation Center, state-titled goes through TPWD.
- **Liens:** any bank paper, marine mortgage, or preferred ship mortgage on file
- **Trailer:** included or not
- **Recent work:** bottom paint date, last service, anything replaced in the last 24 months

Pull the COI and the last inspection report. Those two documents do more selling than any photo will.

## Pricing, and where the numbers come from

The used cycleboat market is small and mostly transacts privately, so public comps are thin. What I could find:

| New TourCraft Cycleboat 32 | Current production, 26-30 pax | Contact for price |

Two real comps is not a market. But the shape is clear: a COI'd 26-passenger boat sits well north of a COI'd 16-passenger boat, and a 2022 in running commercial service should ask close to what a 2021 asks.

**What moves your number up:**
- Current COI with runway left on it
- Clean inspection history, drydock not immediately due
- Running in commercial service today (a boat that's been sitting reads as a problem)
- Roof, head, sound system, party lights already installed and working
- Turnkey delivery: you hand over the manuals, the checklists, the captain standards doc

**What moves it down:**
- Drydock coming due inside 12 months (buyer prices that in at $8k-$15k)
- Any open USCG requirements
- Buyer having to relocate it out of Texas

## Two ways to sell this, and my pick

**Option A: clean asset sale.** Just the boat. Simpler, faster, and given the legal posture, easier to document at arm's length fair market value.

**Option B: boat plus the operating position.** Delta Dream plus the Clear Lake launch spot, the FareHarbor setup, and a brand with 4.9 stars across 400+ reviews. To an operator who wants into the Houston market, that package is worth real money above the hull, because the reviews and the ramp access are the two things that take years to build.

**Take Option A.** Option B is worth more on paper but it drags the brand, the legal situation, and the Mama Tried claim into due diligence, and it makes the transaction far harder to characterize cleanly. Sell the boat. Keep the brand. If you later want out of the whole thing, that's a separate conversation with different advisors.

## Photos and video you need

There is no usable Delta Dream photography in the vault right now. One decent shoot is worth several thousand dollars on a boat at this price. Shoot on a clear day, boat clean, dock lines coiled:

- Bow-on and stern-on, dockside, full boat in frame
- Both profiles (port and starboard)
- Overhead drone, boat underway with a wake
- Deck from the bow looking aft, and from the stern looking forward

- Helm station

- Engine, close, clean, cowling on and off

Then a 60-90 second walkthrough video, phone is fine, no music, just narrate what you're showing. Commercial buyers watch those all the way through.

## Paperwork checklist

- [ ] Confirm with Ritter that the sale structure is clean before signing anything
- [ ] Pull the current COI and last USCG inspection report
- [ ] Confirm documented vs. state titled, and pull the Certificate of Documentation
- [ ] Run an abstract of title through NVDC to confirm no recorded liens
- [ ] Confirm no marine mortgage or bank lien outstanding
- [ ] Bill of Sale — use CG-1340 for a documented vessel
- [ ] Buyer applies for their own COI. A COI does not transfer with the boat. The new owner files with the OCMI (Sector Houston-Galveston if it stays local) and the vessel's clean inspection history is what makes that fast instead of painful. Say this plainly in every buyer conversation so nobody is surprised at closing. Worth confirming the current process with Sector Houston-Galveston directly, since it drives the buyer's timeline.
- [ ] Escrow through a marine title company or a documentation service. Do not take a wire straight to the operating account on a six-figure boat.
- [ ] Remove Delta Dream from the insurance policy only after transfer completes
- [ ] Pull the vessel from FareHarbor resources and update capacity so you're not selling 52-passenger charters you can no longer run

## Buyer screening

Ask these three questions on the first call and you'll cut the tire-kickers in one pass:

1. Are you currently operating a passenger vessel, or would this be your first?
2. Where are you planning to run it, and have you talked to that Sector about the COI?
3. Cash, or are you financing? (Commercial passenger vessel financing is slow and often falls through. A financed buyer isn't a bad buyer, but you should know on day one.)

An operator who already has a COI'd boat somewhere else is your best buyer. They know what the certificate is worth, they won't haggle over the paddle wheel, and they close.

## Operational fallout to plan for

Once Delta Dream goes, you're a one-boat operation. That kills the 52-passenger simultaneous-run offering and it means one mechanical issue takes the whole business offline for the day. Before the boat leaves:

- Update every place that says "two boats" or "up to 52 guests": website, FareHarbor, OpenCX agent instructions, the Agent Instructions doc, GBP, entity.json, Brand Story & Values
- Decide what happens to peak Saturday demand with half the capacity. Raising private charter pricing is the obvious lever.
- Tell Connor before it's listed, not after. He'll be the one fielding questions from crew.
