# Guest Experience Quiz

**Passing score: 80% (8 out of 10 correct)**

The difference between a 3-star review and a 5-star review is almost never the swamp. It's you.

---

### Question 1

A guest booked for 2 shows up at Rampart with 4 people. The other 2 don't have bookings or waivers. What do you do?

A) Turn the extra 2 away. They didn't book.
B) Check FareHarbor for availability. If there's room, add them on-site, get waivers signed through their booking-specific links, and proceed.
C) Let them come along and sort out the booking later.
D) Call management and wait for instructions.

**Best answer: B**

More guests is more revenue, and walk-ups happen. If you have capacity (enough kayaks, PFDs, space), add them through FareHarbor so waivers are properly tied to a booking. Never let someone on the water without a signed waiver and a booking, which rules out C. Turning them away without checking is unnecessary. Calling management for something you can handle on-site is a waste of everyone's time, unless you're truly at capacity.

### Question 2

A guest is visibly nervous at the launch. They're gripping the dock, watching other people get in kayaks, and haven't moved toward the water. What's your approach?

A) Give them extra time. They'll come around.
B) Walk over, acknowledge it directly. "A little nervous? Totally normal. Half the people on this tour were nervous their first time. These boats are super stable, and I'll be right there the whole time. Want me to help you get in?"
C) Put them in a tandem with someone confident so they feel safer.
D) Suggest they sit this one out and wait in the van.

**Best answer: B**

Acknowledge the fear, normalize it, give them specific reassurance (boats are stable, you'll be nearby), and offer hands-on help. Most nervous guests just need someone to say "this is normal and I've got you." Answer A leaves them alone with their anxiety. Answer C doesn't address the fear, and they didn't ask for a partner. Answer D kills the experience before it starts.

### Question 3

Mid-tour, a guest says "So, how much should we tip?" What do you say?

A) "Whatever you feel is fair! Tips are always appreciated."
B) "20% is standard." 
C) "Honestly, tips are totally up to you. If you had a good time, that's what matters. Anything on top of that is a bonus and I appreciate it."
D) "We're not really supposed to talk about tips."

**Best answer: C**

Be genuine, not greedy and not dismissive. Answer C is honest and low-pressure. It makes the guest feel good about tipping without creating an awkward transactional vibe in the middle of a swamp tour. Answer A is fine but a little hollow. Answer B is too direct and presumptuous. Answer D makes it weird.

### Question 4

A guest from a Viator booking insists they don't need the shuttle because they have their own car and want to meet at the launch. What do you tell them?

A) "Sure, here's the address. Meet us there."
B) "All third-party bookings include shuttle service. It's part of the package and the way we keep the tour running smoothly. We'll pick you up at 740 N. Rampart. I promise the drive is part of the experience."
C) "Let me check with my manager."
D) "That's not how we do things. Shuttle is mandatory."

**Best answer: B**

Third-party bookings always include shuttle, no exceptions. But the way you deliver this matters. Frame it as part of the experience, not a restriction. The drive to the swamp is 45 minutes and it's where you start building rapport. Answer A violates the policy. Answer D is correct but rude. Answer C is unnecessary because this isn't a judgment call.

### Question 5

A guest complains that the drive to the launch site is too long. "I didn't sign up for an hour in a van." How do you handle it?

A) Apologize and offer a partial refund.
B) "Yeah, the swamp doesn't come to us unfortunately. But the drive is when I get to give you the background on what we're about to see, so by the time we get there, you'll know exactly what you're looking at. That's the part most people end up being glad about."
C) "It's listed on the booking page."
D) Ignore it and focus on driving.

**Best answer: B**

Reframe the drive as a feature, not a bug. The best guides use drive time for storytelling, ecology context, and building group energy. Answer C is defensive and makes the guest feel stupid. Answer A sets a precedent for refunding anyone who complains about something that was disclosed. Answer D lets resentment simmer.

### Question 6

You're at the photo stop and one group is taking forever with selfies while the rest of the group waits. How do you manage this?

A) Let them take their time. Photos are important to guests.
B) Give a clear, friendly time limit: "Let's do 5 more minutes here, then we've got some amazing stuff around the next bend." Keep the energy forward.
C) Skip the photo stop on busy days.
D) Tell them to wrap it up, you're on a schedule.

**Best answer: B**

A time limit delivered with enthusiasm keeps things moving without making anyone feel rushed. Teasing what's ahead ("amazing stuff around the next bend") creates pull instead of push. Unlimited photo time derails the tour for everyone else. Skipping the stop entirely removes a highlight. Being blunt about the schedule makes you sound like a bus driver.

### Question 7

A guest shows up clearly intoxicated at check-in. Not falling-down drunk, but obviously had a few and smells like alcohol. What do you do?

A) Refuse to take them on the tour.
B) Assess their coordination and judgment. If they can walk, talk, and follow instructions, take them but keep an eye on them. Put them in a tandem with a sober partner if possible. If they're impaired enough that they pose a safety risk, explain that you can't take them out and offer a reschedule.
C) Let them come. It's not that serious for a kayak tour.
D) Call management and let them make the call.

**Best answer: B**

This is a judgment call, not a blanket policy. Someone who had a couple mimosas at brunch is different from someone who can't stand straight. Assess the individual. If they're functional, manage the risk (tandem with a sober partner, keep them close). If they're genuinely impaired, it's a safety issue and you explain that with respect. The goal is guest safety, not moral policing.

### Question 8

You learn at the launch that a guest has a prosthetic leg, which they didn't mention when booking. How do you handle it?

A) Tell them they can't do the tour for safety reasons.
B) Have a quick, private conversation. Ask them what they need: "Hey, I want to make sure you're comfortable out there. Is there anything about getting in and out of the kayak I can help with? Any adjustments that would make this better for you?" Then adapt. 
C) Pretend you don't notice and treat them exactly like everyone else.
D) Call management to ask if they're allowed on the tour.

**Best answer: B**

A private, respectful conversation that puts the guest in control of their own needs. Don't assume they can't do it because most people with prosthetics who book kayak tours know exactly what they're capable of. But also don't pretend you didn't notice, because they might actually need help and won't ask unless you open the door. Answer A is discriminatory. Answer C risks missing a real accommodation need.

### Question 9

When is the right time to bring up merchandise (t-shirts, phone cases, sunglass straps)?

A) During check-in before the tour.
B) At the launch while fitting PFDs.
C) During or after the tour, when there's a natural moment. Sunglass straps sell themselves when a guest almost drops their sunglasses in the water. T-shirts come up when guests are feeling good about the experience.
D) Never bring it up. If they want merch, they'll ask.

**Best answer: C**

Selling merchandise works when it's contextual and natural. A sunglass strap mention after someone nearly loses their glasses in the water is helpful, not salesy. A t-shirt mention when the group is laughing and bonding feels like you're offering a souvenir of a great day, not hawking product. Pushing merch during check-in or PFD fitting feels transactional because the guest hasn't had the experience yet.

### Question 10

At the end of the tour, how do you ask for a review without sounding scripted?

A) "Please leave us a review on Google or TripAdvisor!"
B) Hand them a card with a QR code and say nothing.
C) Reference something specific from the tour: "That alligator sighting was incredible, right? If you had a great time, a quick Google or TripAdvisor review honestly makes a huge difference for us. It's how most people find us." Keep it real.
D) Don't ask. Good reviews happen on their own.

**Best answer: C**

The review ask works when it's personal and tied to the actual experience you just shared. Referencing a specific moment from the tour makes it feel like a human conversation, not a corporate script. Generic asks get ignored. Not asking at all means you're leaving reviews to chance, and most people need a small nudge.

### Question 11

A family with two young kids (ages 6 and 8) shows up for the tour. The kids are excited but the parents look nervous. How do you handle this?

A) Reassure the parents that kids this age do great on the tour. Put each kid in a tandem with a parent. During the safety briefing, make sure the kids understand the PFD rules by making it fun, not scary. Check on them a couple extra times during the tour.
B) Tell the parents the tour might be too advanced for kids that young.
C) Let the kids sit in singles since they're light enough.
D) Treat them exactly like adult guests.

**Best answer: A**

Kids in tandems with parents is the safe and fun setup. Reassuring nervous parents sets the right tone. Making the safety briefing kid-friendly (not dumbed down, just engaging) means the kids actually absorb the important stuff. Extra check-ins during the tour show the parents you care. Answer B is presumptuous. Answer C puts small children alone in kayaks, which is a terrible idea. Answer D misses obvious adjustments that make the experience better for everyone.
