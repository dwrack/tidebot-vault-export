# Safety & Compliance Overview

## Why This Document Exists

Safety in a kayak operation isn't dramatic most of the time. It's the boring, repetitive stuff: checking PFDs, verifying waivers, looking at a water gauge website the night before. The dramatic moments, a capsizing, a lightning strike, a medical emergency, are rare. But when they happen, your response is built entirely on whether you internalized the boring stuff.

This document explains the regulatory framework we operate under, why each safety protocol exists, and the reasoning behind decisions that might otherwise seem arbitrary.

## PFD Requirements

Every guest and every guide wears a USCG Type III PFD for the entire time they are on the water. No exceptions. Not for strong swimmers, not for guests who complain, not for a quick paddle back to the launch.

Type III PFDs are designed for calm inland water where rescue is likely to be quick, which describes our operating environment perfectly. They won't turn an unconscious person face-up (that's Type I), but they provide enough buoyancy to keep a conscious person afloat with minimal effort.

Every PFD must have an attached whistle. This is a Coast Guard requirement for vessels, and while individual kayaks exist in a gray area, we treat it as mandatory. Three whistle blasts is the universal distress signal on the water. A guest who capsizes 200 yards from the group has a much better chance of getting attention with a whistle than by yelling.

### Fitting Matters

A PFD that rides up over someone's head when they're in the water is worse than useless because it gives them false confidence while providing zero function. Every PFD gets fitted to the individual guest. Loosen, put on, cinch, then pull test. If it comes over their head, tighten. If it still comes over their head, switch sizes.

For larger guests, we have triangle-style PFDs that provide a more comfortable and secure fit. The language matters here. Say "this one will be more comfortable for you," not anything that calls attention to body size. Guests remember how you made them feel.

### PFD Maintenance

MiraZyme after every tour. Hang to dry after every tour. This isn't optional and it isn't a "when you get around to it" task. PFDs that smell like mildew create an immediate negative impression that colors the entire guest experience. A guest who wrinkles their nose putting on their PFD has already downgraded their review before they touch the water.

Foul odor that persists after MiraZyme treatment gets flagged to management for replacement.

## Throw Bag

A throw bag with 50 feet minimum of line is carried on every tour. In a capsizing scenario where the guide can't paddle directly to the guest, the throw bag provides a lifeline. Practice throwing it. An untrained throw goes about 15 feet and lands nowhere near the target. A practiced throw hits 40+ feet accurately.

## Waivers

Every waiver is tied to a specific booking in FareHarbor. This is not a bureaucratic inconvenience, it's a liability structure that protects the company, the guide, and ultimately the guest.

A generic QR code waiver that isn't tied to a booking creates a documentation gap. If there's an incident and the waiver can't be definitively linked to that specific guest on that specific tour, the waiver's enforceability drops significantly. An attorney will find that gap.

The process: if a guest hasn't signed their waiver, pull up their booking-specific waiver link on your phone and hand it to them. It takes 60 seconds. No guest enters the van without a signed waiver.

## Weather Decision Framework

### Wind
Sustained winds over 15 mph are a hold/cancel threshold. Kayaks have a low profile, but our guests are inexperienced paddlers. A 15 mph headwind turns a pleasant paddle into an exhausting fight that guests didn't sign up for. More importantly, crosswinds make boat control very difficult for beginners, which leads to collisions, capsizing, and group separation.

### Lightning
Lightning within 10 miles is an immediate cancellation or evacuation. Water is the single most dangerous place to be during a lightning storm. There is no "wait and see" with lightning. If you can hear thunder, you're within strike range. Get off the water, get guests into the van, and wait 30 minutes after the last thunder before even considering going back out.

### Visibility
Below 0.25 mile visibility (heavy fog, rain), you can't maintain group cohesion. If you can't see your lead and sweep boats simultaneously, you've lost control of the group.

### Water Temperature
Below 50 degrees F, hypothermia risk increases dramatically for a capsized guest. Cold water removes body heat 25 times faster than cold air. If water temps are below 50 F, require wetsuits or cancel. We don't currently stock wetsuits, so in practice this means cancellation during rare cold snaps.

## Water Gauge: Honey Island

The Honey Island route depends on water levels at the Pearl River gauge (PERL1). Check the gauge at wx.aerisweather.com/rivers/gauge/PERL1.html the night before any Honey Island tour.

**Below 5 feet:** Too shallow. Kayaks will drag on the bottom, making the tour miserable and potentially damaging equipment. Move to Manchac.

**5-12 feet:** Safe operating range. The sweet spot is roughly 6-9 feet, where current is manageable and water depth allows comfortable paddling with good wildlife visibility.

**Above 12 feet:** Current becomes too strong for inexperienced paddlers. The risk of group separation and capsizing increases significantly. Move to Manchac.

When you move routes, notify management and text all booked guests with the change. Most guests don't care which swamp they paddle through, they care that you're making smart decisions about their safety.

## Emergency Procedures: The Reasoning

### Why we say "Stay with your boat"
Sit-on-top kayaks don't sink. A guest who panics and swims away from their kayak is now an unattached person in open water with no flotation beyond their PFD. The kayak is their biggest, most visible flotation device. Stay with it.

### Why guides approach capsized guests, not the other way around
A panicking guest will grab anything, including another kayak or another person, and can capsize a rescue attempt. The guide approaches with training and a plan. Other guests stay put.

### Why we don't move potential spinal injuries
A fall from a kayak combined with underwater impact (stump, rock, shallow bottom) can cause spinal injury. Moving someone with a spinal injury risks permanent paralysis. Stabilize, keep their head above water if possible, and wait for EMS.

## Incident Reporting

Every incident gets documented the same day. Not the next morning, not "when I get a chance." Same day. Memory degrades fast, and an incident report written 48 hours later is significantly less useful (and less credible) than one written within hours.

The report includes: what happened, when, who was involved, what actions were taken, and witness names. Photograph everything relevant. These reports protect the guide, the company, and the guests.

## Louisiana Regulatory Context

Louisiana requires commercial kayak operations to maintain appropriate insurance, ensure PFD compliance, and follow USCG guidelines for navigable waterways. Our operating areas include both state-managed WMA land (Maurepas Swamp) and navigable waterways, which means we operate under both state and federal jurisdiction depending on the specific location.

Keep your certifications current. Keep your driving record clean. These aren't just company policies, they're the baseline for legal operation in this state.
