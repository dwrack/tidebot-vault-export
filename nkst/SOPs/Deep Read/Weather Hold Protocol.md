# Weather Hold Protocol

## Who Makes the Call

The guide leading the tour makes the initial weather call. If there's any doubt, the contractor on duty (Jeffrey/JT) makes the final decision. When in doubt, call. Nobody has ever been reprimanded for being cautious about weather. People have been reprimanded for ignoring it.

If you're mid-tour and conditions change, you don't need permission to get off the water. Get off the water and then call management.

## Decision Thresholds

### Wind: >15 mph sustained

Kayaks sit low on the water, which helps with stability in normal conditions. But our guests are inexperienced paddlers who are still figuring out which end of the paddle goes in the water. Sustained winds above 15 mph create three problems simultaneously:

1. Headwinds make forward progress exhausting. Guests fatigue fast and stop having fun.
2. Crosswinds push boats off course. A guest who can barely steer in calm water has zero chance in a crosswind.
3. Wind creates chop on open water sections. Small waves don't capsize a kayak, but they scare guests who are already nervous.

Check wind forecasts the night before. If it's borderline (12-15 mph), check again in the morning. Gusts above 20 mph with sustained winds near 15 are a cancel.

### Lightning: within 10 miles

This is the simplest rule in the book. If you see lightning or hear thunder, get off the water. Don't time the gap between flash and thunder to calculate distance. Don't look at the radar to see if it's "moving away." Get off the water.

Water is the most dangerous place to be in a lightning storm. A strike on open water is lethal at distances that would be survivable on land. There is no acceptable level of lightning risk when you have guests in kayaks.

After clearing the water, wait 30 minutes from the last observed lightning or thunder before returning. If the storm is clearly moving through, you might get back out. If it's a summer afternoon pattern where storms keep firing, call the tour.

### Visibility: <0.25 mile

Heavy fog or rain that drops visibility below a quarter mile means you can't see your full group. On a typical tour with 6-8 kayaks spread across 100-200 yards of waterway, you need clear sightlines to maintain safety. If you can't see both the lead boat and the tail from the middle of the group, visibility is too low.

Morning fog in the swamp is common, especially in fall and spring. It usually burns off by 9:30-10 AM. If your tour is early and fog is thick, delay the launch rather than cancel. Give it 30-45 minutes.

### Water Temperature: <50 degrees F

This one rarely comes up in New Orleans, but during winter cold snaps, water temperatures can drop into the dangerous range. Cold water removes body heat 25 times faster than air at the same temperature. A guest who capsizes in 48-degree water has minutes before cold incapacitation sets in, not hours.

Below 50 F, the rule is: require wetsuits or cancel. Since we don't currently stock wetsuits, this effectively means cancellation. It only happens a few times a year, if that.

### Water Gauge: Honey Island Route

Check the Pearl River gauge at wx.aerisweather.com/rivers/gauge/PERL1.html the night before.

| Reading | Condition | Action |
|---------|-----------|--------|
| Below 5 ft | Too shallow | Move to Manchac |
| 5-12 ft | Safe range | Run Honey Island |
| Above 12 ft | Current too strong | Move to Manchac |

The sweet spot is 6-9 ft. At the low end of the safe range (5-6 ft), you may encounter some shallow spots that require portaging or route adjustment. At the high end (10-12 ft), current is noticeable and you'll want to plan your route to minimize fighting it, especially with the group paddling upstream.

## Seasonal Patterns to Watch

**Summer (June-September):** Afternoon thunderstorms are nearly daily from June through August. Morning tours are almost always fine. Afternoon tours need careful monitoring. Lightning is the primary concern. Heat is secondary but real: hydration breaks, shade when available, and watch for heat exhaustion symptoms.

**Fall (October-November):** The best touring weather. Lower humidity, comfortable temps, and generally stable conditions. Morning fog can be an issue in October.

**Winter (December-February):** Cold snaps bring water temp concerns. Wind tends to be more consistent (frontal passages). Tour volume is lower, so cancellations have less financial impact. Don't push marginal conditions in winter to fill a tour.

**Spring (March-May):** Storm season. Watch for frontal boundaries and the associated wind shifts and thunderstorms. Spring is also high water season, so gauge readings tend to run higher. You'll be on Manchac more often in March-April.

## Communication Protocol

When you hold or cancel a tour, three things happen immediately:

### 1. Text All Booked Guests
Be direct and warm. Don't over-explain the meteorology. Something like: "Hey [name], we're going to need to reschedule today's tour due to weather conditions on the water. Everyone's safety comes first. We'll reach out with available dates, or you can call us at 504-571-9975 to rebook."

If it's a route change (Honey Island to Manchac) rather than a cancellation: "Hey [name], quick heads up: we're moving today's tour to our Manchac Swamp location due to water levels at Honey Island. Same great experience, beautiful route. Everything else stays the same."

### 2. Notify Management
Text or call Jeffrey/JT. Include: what you're cancelling or holding, why, and current conditions. If you're mid-tour and evacuating, call, don't text.

### 3. Process the Administrative Side
- Offer reschedule first (always)
- If rescheduling isn't possible, process refund per current policy
- Update FareHarbor to reflect the cancellation
- Document the weather call in your end-of-day notes

## The Judgment Part

Thresholds are guides, not laws. A 14 mph sustained wind with gusts to 22 is more dangerous than a steady 16 mph. A lightning cell 12 miles away and tracking toward you is different from one 12 miles away and tracking parallel. A group of athletic 25-year-olds handles marginal wind better than a family with three kids under 10.

Use the thresholds as your starting point and then apply judgment. The one rule that never bends: when in doubt, don't go out. A cancelled tour is a logistical inconvenience. A preventable injury is a catastrophe.
