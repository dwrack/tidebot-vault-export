# Safety Briefing Script

## How to Use This

This is not a script you read word-for-word to guests. It's the content you need to internalize and then deliver conversationally, in your own voice, making eye contact, reading the room. A guide who memorizes this verbatim and recites it sounds like a flight attendant nobody listens to. A guide who knows this material and talks through it naturally sounds like someone guests trust with their safety.

Practice it until you can cover every point without looking at notes. Then forget the order and just make sure you hit everything.

## PFDs Stay On

Start here. It's the most important rule and the one guests are most likely to push back on.

"Your PFD stays on for the entire time we're on the water. I know it's warm, and I know some of you are strong swimmers, but this isn't about whether you can swim. It's about what happens when you go under unexpectedly, you're disoriented, maybe you've hit something, and your body needs to get back to the surface without you thinking about it. The PFD does that work for you. So it stays on. Deal?"

Deliver this with a smile, but don't make it sound optional. It isn't.

## Your Kayak Doesn't Sink

This is the single most reassuring thing you can tell nervous guests.

"These are sit-on-top kayaks. They're basically big plastic surfboards with seats. If you fall off, and some of you might, the kayak doesn't go anywhere. It doesn't sink, it doesn't flip upside down, it just sits there and waits for you. So the worst case scenario is you get wet. Which, honestly, on a day like today, might feel pretty good."

## Staying Together

"We paddle as a group. I know some of you are going to want to race ahead, and some of you are going to want to drift and take photos, and both of those are great, but stay within earshot of me. If I can't see you, something's wrong. We'll have a lead boat up front [point to them] and I'll be in the back making sure nobody gets left behind."

## Wildlife

"You're going to see incredible wildlife out here. Alligators, turtles, herons, maybe snakes, definitely some fish jumping. The rule is simple: observe, never approach, and absolutely never feed. I know that alligator looks chill right now, but a fed alligator is a dangerous alligator. They start associating boats with food, and that's bad for every tour that comes after us."

Specific wildlife notes to weave in during the tour:

- **Fishing lines in branches:** Point these out. They're a tangle and cut hazard. Steer guests around them.
- **Snakes:** If a snake drops near or on a kayak (it happens), stay calm. Use your paddle to gently scoop it back toward the water. Your calm sets the tone for the group. A guide who panics creates 12 panicking guests.
- **Alligators:** If one is nearby, tell guests to stop paddling and let the alligator move away on its own. They almost always do. Don't chase, don't corner, don't approach for photos. Let them come and go on their terms.

## Lightning

"If I tell you we're heading to shore, we're heading to shore. No debate, no 'but we just got out here.' Lightning and open water are the most dangerous combination there is, and I take it seriously. It probably won't happen, but if it does, just follow me and we'll be off the water fast."

## Questions

"Ask me anything at any point during the tour. There's no dumb question out here. If you're nervous about something, I'd rather know now than find out when we're in the middle of the swamp."

## Paddle Technique

Walk through this on dry land before anyone gets in a boat.

### Forward Stroke
"Hold the paddle with both hands shoulder-width apart. The curved part of the blade faces away from you. Reach forward, plant the blade in the water near your toes, and pull it back past your hip. Then do the other side. Don't dig deep, and don't muscle it. Smooth, easy strokes. Let the paddle do the work."

### Turning
"To turn right, paddle on the left. To turn left, paddle on the right. If you need a sharper turn, do a sweep: start the stroke near the front of the boat and sweep it out wide in an arc."

### Stopping
"Drag your paddle flat in the water on both sides, like brakes. Or just stop paddling, these boats slow down fast."

### Reverse
"Same as forward but backwards. Reach behind you, plant the blade, push forward. You won't need this much, but it's handy for backing away from branches."

### Body Position
"Keep your shoulders low and relaxed. Your hips should be loose, let the boat move under you. Think about your upper body and lower body moving independently. Beginners tend to tense up and grip the paddle like they're trying to strangle it. Relax your hands. Loose grip, smooth stroke."

## Paddle Signals

Go through these and have the group practice responding:

| Signal | Meaning |
|--------|---------|
| Paddle held vertical, stationary | Come to me |
| Paddle held horizontal | Stop/hold position |
| Paddle at 45 degrees, pointing | Go that direction |
| Paddle held vertical, waving | Distress |
| 3 whistle blasts | Help needed |

"If you hear three whistle blasts, from me or from anyone, stop paddling and look for who's blowing. That means someone needs help."

## If You Fall Out

"If you go in the water, here's what to do: don't panic. Your PFD has you. Grab your kayak and your paddle. If your buddy is in a tandem with you, go to opposite sides of the boat and take turns pulling yourselves back up. It's graceless but it works. If you can't get back in, hold onto your boat, blow your whistle, and I'll paddle over to help. Stay with your boat. It's your biggest flotation device and it's the easiest thing for me to spot."

Demonstrate the re-entry if your launch site allows it (or describe it vividly): "Grab the far edge from the water, kick your legs up behind you like you're swimming, and pull yourself across the deck on your belly. Then swing your legs in. It's basically like getting out of a pool with no ladder."

## The Closing Energy

End the briefing with confidence, not fear. You just covered a lot of safety material, and some guests might be more nervous now than when you started.

"That's the serious stuff. Here's the good news: this is one of the safest water activities you can do. These boats are incredibly stable, the water is calm, and you've got me out there the whole time. People who've never been in a kayak before do this tour every single day and have the time of their lives. So let's get out there."

Get them in the boats while the energy is high.
