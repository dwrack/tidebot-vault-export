# Captions — Bayou Voice

Created 2026-11-02. Source: David's own paddle write-ups (fall 2026). Use this doc as the voice reference for feed captions on @neworleanskayakswamptours and @kayaknola, plus Facebook.

## How this voice works

- First person, present tense where it fits. It reads like a guide texting a friend after a paddle, not a brand.
- Open on a feeling, close on a feeling. The middle is a field report.
- Specific over pretty: counts (over 50 gators), species (osprey, black-bellied whistlers), what the light did, what the water did.
- One "whew" or "y'all" or "I sure miss" moment per caption. Not three.
- Pet names are fine: swamp puppies, big papas, mawmaws. Never assure safety.
- Ellipses and the occasional CAPS word are part of the voice. No em dashes.
- Season is always in it. Every caption should tell you what month it is without saying the month.
- End on stillness, not a sales line. If there's a CTA, it's one soft line after the feeling.
- 80 to 140 words. Four short paragraphs max.
- Second sample pattern (David's "mirrored glass" post): a quoted thought to self in the middle ("How do I even put this into words?"), then a fast list of sensory hits, then the "I say this every time" admission. Use that admission sparingly, maybe once a month.

## Batch 1 (Nov 2026)

### 1. Rewrite of David's original

That first push off the bank...I don't think I'll ever get tired of it. And this week out on the bayou was something else.

The cypress are going full copper right now, cane fields smoking off on the horizon, and the water so still the trees were standing on their own heads. Once those needles drop, the moss finally gets the whole stage to itself.

Gators were OUT. Lost count somewhere past 50 in a single afternoon, from hatchlings no longer than my paddle blade to a couple of big papas stretched across logs like they pay rent there.

Two bald eagles this week. Osprey still hanging on. Fish rolling everywhere you look. The whistling ducks have moved on though, and I miss that sound more than I expected.

The swamp slows all the way down in fall. But sit in the middle of it long enough and you realize nothing stopped. It just got quieter.

### 1b. Rewrite of David's second post (mirrored glass)

Y'all. Yesterday.

The water was one flat sheet of glass from bank to bank, holding the whole sky and every strand of moss upside down and perfectly still. I kept drifting along trying to figure out how I'd ever describe it to somebody who wasn't there.

Air so clean it almost tasted like something. Cypress turning. That sweet cane smell rolling across the bayou. Gators stretched out on logs, a hawk circling overhead, mullet jumping on both sides of the boat like they were showing off for me.

I know I say this every trip. But it really is different every single time. All these hours out here and it still stops me cold.

### 2. Fog morning

Fog so thick at launch this morning I couldn't see the far bank. Then the sun found it and the whole bayou went gold in about four minutes flat.

That's the thing about November out here. You get mornings that feel like you're paddling through somebody's dream, and by ten o'clock it's a bluebird day with a dozen gators hauled out on the sunny side of every log.

Egrets working the shallows. A kingfisher cussing me out the entire way down the channel. One nutria that was NOT pleased about sharing the water.

Cool enough for a flannel at the dock, warm enough to shed it by the first bend. My favorite kind of day.

### 3. Spanish moss

Nobody talks enough about the moss.

In summer the cypress canopy hides it. Now, with the needles coming down copper and rust, the Spanish moss is finally hanging out in the open, swaying on every branch like the swamp put on its good jewelry.

Paddled under a tunnel of it this afternoon with the water gone to glass, and for a stretch there wasn't a sound but the drip off my blade.

Then a big heron lifted off ten feet from the bow and reminded me whose house I was in.

### 4. Gator count

Whew. The swamp puppies came out to play today.

Counted 38 before I quit counting. Little ones stacked up on a cypress knee like a pile of pencils, a few mid-size cruising with just eyes and nostrils showing, and one big mawmaw on the bank who did not move a muscle the whole time we drifted by.

Cooler water means they're spending more time sunning, so fall is when you see the most of them, not the least. Folks are always surprised by that.

Two osprey overhead the whole trip. Fish rolling. Cane smoke on the breeze. I'll take November all day long.

### 5. Sounds change

The bayou sounds different in fall and I can't stop noticing it.

Summer is a wall of noise, cicadas and frogs and that constant buzz. Now it's quiet enough to hear a fish roll fifty yards off. A woodpecker somewhere back in the cypress. The creak of the boat. And every so often a splash you didn't see coming that gets your heart going for a second.

The black-bellied whistlers are gone till spring and I feel their absence every time I pass the spot they used to sit.

But there's so much life in this stillness. You just have to sit in it a minute to hear it.

### 6. Eagle

Bald eagle sitting in a dead cypress at the mouth of the bayou this morning, just watching us come in.

Didn't fly. Didn't flinch. Let us drift right under and look up at it like a couple of tourists, which I suppose we were.

Everything else was doing its November thing. Copper needles on the water, moss showing off, gators sunning on logs now that the mornings are cool. But that bird sat with me all day.

Some paddles you remember for the whole trip. Some you remember for one minute of it.

### 7. Late light

Last tour of the day and the sun was already sliding down behind the cypress when we pushed off.

Everything turns rust and amber this time of year anyway, but in that low light the whole swamp looked like it was lit from inside. Water flat as a mirror. Moss catching the last of it. A line of ibis heading home over the treetops.

Gators still out, soaking up what was left of the warm. One big one slipped off a log as we passed and the ripple took a full minute to reach us.

Then the light was gone and it was just the sound of paddles. That settles something in me every time.

## Batch 2 (Nov 2026) — mystery and desire

Direction from David 2026-11-02: keep them poetic, full of mystery and desire. Every other caption gets an alternate version (A/B).

### 8. The bend

There's a bend in the bayou where the cypress close in and the light goes green and you stop being sure which way is out.

I've paddled it a hundred times. Still, every time I come around it, something in my chest leans forward. Like the swamp is about to tell me something it hasn't told anybody else.

Water black and still. Moss hanging low enough to touch. Somewhere ahead, a splash I never see.

I go back for that bend. I think about it on days I'm not out there. If that's not a kind of love, I don't know what is.

### 9. Fog, two versions

**A.**

Launched into fog this morning and the world shrank to about twenty feet of water and whatever was in it.

A cypress knee would drift out of the white like a ghost and slide back into it. A gator's eyes, there and then not. The only proof the bayou kept going was the sound of it, dripping and ticking and breathing somewhere past where I could see.

Then the sun came up behind it all and set the fog on fire from the inside.

I don't know what I'm looking for out there. But mornings like that, I get close.

**B.**

The fog had the bayou this morning and wasn't giving it back.

I paddled into it anyway. Everything past the bow was a rumor. Trees you could feel more than see. A heron that announced itself with one slow wingbeat and was gone. The water so still my own wake was the only thing moving in the whole world.

And then, slow, the light. Copper needles showing up on the surface one at a time like someone was placing them there.

Some mornings the swamp lets you in. This was one.

### 10. What's underneath

You can see maybe a foot down into that water. Below that it's all tannin and secrets.

I think about that every time something rolls under the boat. A fish, probably. A gar the length of my leg. Something older. The bayou doesn't tell you and that's half of why I keep coming back.

Up top it was all mirror and moss today, cypress going to rust, a kingfisher stitching back and forth across the channel.

But I kept looking down. I always do.

### 11. Sunset, two versions

**A.**

The last hour on the water is the one I'd bottle if I could.

The sun gets low and the whole swamp turns the color of a struck match. Cypress black against it. Moss lit from behind. Water holding all of it like it's trying to remember.

A big gator slid off a log just as the light went and the ripple carried the orange all the way to my hull.

Then it was dark and quiet and I wasn't ready to leave. I'm never ready to leave.

**B.**

The swamp at dusk doesn't look like a place. It looks like a feeling you had once and couldn't name.

Amber on the water. Cypress gone to silhouette. Spanish moss catching the last of the light and holding it a few seconds longer than it should be able to.

Ibis crossing overhead toward wherever ibis go. One gator, eyes only, watching the boat the way you watch a door.

I drifted until I couldn't see the paddle. Didn't want to break it.

### 12. The sound you don't see

Every paddle there's one splash you don't catch. Big, close, and gone before your head turns.

Out here in fall it's quiet enough that you really hear it. The cicadas have quit. The frogs have thinned out. So that one splash lands in the silence like a stone in a church.

I used to try to figure out what it was. Now I just let it be whatever it wants.

The bayou keeps a few things to itself. I've made my peace with that. Mostly.

### 13. Homesick for it, two versions

**A.**

Two days off the water and I'm already homesick for it.

Not the sights, exactly. The smell. Cane smoke and wet cypress and that sweet rot that sounds bad and isn't. The way the air sits on your skin in the morning like a hand. The sound a paddle makes when the water is that still.

I know the gators are out there right now, stacked on logs in the sun, not thinking about me at all.

Tomorrow. First light. I'll be back where I belong.

**B.**

There's a particular ache that only the bayou fixes.

I don't know what to call it. It shows up after a few days inside, a restlessness in the hands, a wanting for still water and copper light and the smell of cane smoke on the wind.

The swamp is out there doing what it does without me. Moss swaying. Gators sunning. Fish rolling in the glass.

I'll be on it by sunrise. I always am, eventually. It always takes me back.

### 14. The old ones

Some of these cypress were standing when the river still ran wild through here. Before the levees. Before the city.

I paddle up to one and put my hand on it and the bark is cool and ridged and completely uninterested in me. It's seen gators the size of boats. Storms with names. Centuries of fog.

The moss on it now is just this year's. The needles coming down copper are just this fall's.

I'm just this morning's. That doesn't bother me as much out there as it probably should.

### 15. Alone with it, two versions

**A.**

Got a stretch of bayou completely to myself this afternoon. No boats. No voices. Just water and trees and whatever was watching from the bank.

I stopped paddling and let it hold me. The current out there is so slow you can't feel it, but twenty minutes later the cypress had rearranged themselves around me and I hadn't moved a muscle.

A gator surfaced close enough to count the scutes on its back. Looked at me. Went under. Didn't come back up where I could see.

I don't tell everybody about that stretch.

**B.**

There's a channel I don't put on the map.

Narrow, dark, moss down to the waterline. The kind of place that makes you lower your voice without deciding to. I slipped in this afternoon with the water like black glass and the cypress leaning in like they wanted to hear.

Something big moved under the boat. The ripple went out and the trees caught it and sent it back.

I stayed longer than I meant to. I always do in there. Some places you don't visit. You get invited.
