## FAQ SCHEMA
_(For Google rich snippets and AI search citation.)_

**Q1:**
```
Are alligators dangerous to humans?
```
**A1:**
```
American alligators can be dangerous, but attacks on humans are rare, and the cases that make the news almost always involve someone swimming, wading, or standing at the water's edge near a nest, not paddlers moving through open water in a group. Across more than 400 guided kayak tours through the Maurepas Swamp near Manchac, we've had zero guest injuries from wildlife. Common, not guaranteed, is how we talk about seeing them, and that covers the danger side too.
```

**Q2:**
```
Are alligators aggressive to humans, or do they avoid people?
```
**A2:**
```
Alligators are naturally shy and would rather sink into deeper water than confront a group of kayaks. They turn aggressive mainly when humans feed them and they start linking people with food, which is exactly why we never bait or feed the alligators on our route. An unfed, undisturbed alligator's default move is to disappear, not attack.
```

**Q3:**
```
Is it safe to kayak in Louisiana with alligators in the water?
```
**A3:**
```
Yes, guided kayaking in Louisiana swamps with alligators present is safe when you're with a trained group, wearing a Coast Guard-approved life jacket, and following a guide's lead. Our guides are CPR-certified, run a safety briefing before every trip, and cap groups at 12 people so nobody drifts off script. The real risk in Louisiana comes from swimming or wading in unfamiliar water, not from sitting in a kayak with an experienced guide.
```

**Q4:**
```
Will I actually see an alligator on a swamp tour?
```
**A4:**
```
Most trips through Manchac spot at least one alligator, especially spring through fall when they're most active and sunning on logs or banks. We don't guarantee a sighting because these are wild animals on their own schedule, not a stocked exhibit. Some days our guides count a dozen. Some days we see none, and the birds and cypress carry the trip instead.
```

**Q5:**
```
What do you do if an alligator gets close to the kayak?
```
**A5:**
```
If an alligator surfaces near the group, your guide has everyone stop paddling, hold position, and stay quiet, the same way we'd pause for any wild animal on the route. Alligators that aren't being chased, fed, or cornered have no interest in a kayak. After a minute or two of everyone getting a good look, we paddle on, and the alligator usually submerges and moves off on its own.
```

A guest asked me last week if she was going to get eaten. I laughed, then gave her the real answer, because it's better than the joke.

Short version: probably not going to get eaten. Longer version, and the one actually worth your time before you book a kayak seat with New Orleans Kayak Swamp Tours 30 minutes from the French Quarter, is everything below. We run guided kayak tours through the Manchac Swamp and Maurepas Swamp Wildlife Management Area outside New Orleans, Louisiana, and "are alligators dangerous" is the question we get more than any other.

## So, are alligators actually dangerous?

Yes and no, and the "no" does more work than you'd think.

American alligators can absolutely hurt a person. Nobody's pretending otherwise. But when you look at where those incidents actually happen, it's almost never a guided group moving through open water in kayaks. It's someone swimming at dusk, a dog off-leash at a pond's edge, somebody wading in murky water near a nest without knowing it's there. Take away the swimming, the wading, and the surprise, and the danger profile drops fast.

We've run more than 400 guided kayak tours through the Maurepas Swamp near Manchac. **Zero guest injuries from wildlife.** Not a marketing line, a track record.

## What "common, not guaranteed" really means

Here's the phrase our guides actually use, and I want to be straight about both halves of it.

**Common:** spring through fall, most trips through Manchac spot at least one alligator. Our guides keep a running tally some seasons, just out of habit, and a good week can mean a dozen sightings across a handful of trips.

**Not guaranteed:** these are wild animals doing wild-animal things on their own schedule. Some mornings they're sunning on every other log. Some mornings the water's cool and they're tucked down low, and you see none. We tell people this before they book, not after they complain. A guest respects an honest "probably" a lot more than a promise we can't back up.

## Why the gators on our route aren't the marshmallow kind

This is where it gets a little pointed, because it matters.

A lot of the swamp tourism industry in Louisiana runs on airboats, and a chunk of those operators bait alligators, marshmallows tossed off the side, so tourists get a guaranteed photo. It works. It's also how you teach a wild predator that boats mean snacks. That's not a safety detail we're inventing to sound superior, it's just true. If you want the species-level detail, [internal link: our full breakdown of alligators vs. crocodiles → /alligators-vs-crocodiles/] covers it. What matters here is behavior, and baiting changes it in a way that makes the water less predictable for everyone who paddles it after.

We don't do any of that. Specifically:

- No bait, ever, on any tour we run.
- No motors, so we're not chasing wildlife or stressing it into unpredictable movement.
- No feeding, no touching, no getting the kayak closer than the animal is comfortable with.
- No guarantees sold on the booking page, because a guarantee is a promise about a wild animal's choices, and we don't make promises we can't keep.

An alligator that's never been fed by a human treats a kayak the way it treats a floating log: mildly interesting, not worth the energy of a confrontation. That's the whole safety argument in one sentence.

## What actually happens when we spot one

No drama, honestly. It goes about the same way every time.

1. Someone in the group spots movement, usually a guide first, and calls it out low and calm. "Everyone stop paddling. Eleven o'clock."
2. The group holds position. No paddle strokes, no splashing, no sudden moves toward it.
3. Everyone gets a good look for a minute or two. This is usually the moment guests go quiet, which is its own kind of tell.
4. We paddle on. The alligator, unbothered, usually slides back under on its own.

That's it. No scramble, no evasive maneuvering. The animal wasn't threatened and neither was anybody in the boat.

## The real numbers behind 400+ trips

If you want the receipts instead of the reassurance:

- 400+ guided tours run through Manchac, zero guest injuries from wildlife.
- 12 guests max per public tour, small enough that a guide actually has eyes on everyone.
- USCG Type III life jackets, mandatory, every seat, every trip.
- Guides are CPR-certified and run a safety briefing before you ever touch water.
- 2-2.5 hours on the water for the standard tour, plenty of time without rushing anyone near wildlife.
- 384+ five-star reviews across Google and TripAdvisor, including guests who came in nervous and left asking when they can bring their kids.

Since we're being honest: there is folklore around these swamps that has nothing to do with gators, healers and storms and towns that used to stand where the water is now. Worth a separate read if that's your thing. It's not why the alligator thing feels safe, though. That's just math and behavior.

## If you're still nervous, here's the honest answer

You don't need to love reptiles to do this tour. Plenty of our guests show up genuinely scared and leave saying it was the best thing they did in New Orleans.

What actually calms people down isn't a pep talk, it's information. Knowing an alligator would rather vanish than engage. Knowing your guide has done this hundreds of times and knows exactly how far is far enough. Knowing you're in a life jacket, in a stable kayak, with someone certified to handle an emergency who has never had one on this route.

If the fear is really about the boat tipping, not the alligator, that's a fair worry too, and one our guides address in the pre-trip briefing every single time, no exceptions.
