## CONTENT (Markdown body)

```markdown
Somebody asks us this at the dock almost every single day: airboat or kayak? We're biased, obviously, New Orleans Kayak Swamp Tours runs kayak tours. But we've also sat in enough airboats scouting other operators' routes to give you a straight answer instead of a sales pitch. Here's what actually happens on each one.

## What an Airboat Tour Actually Is

An airboat is a flat-bottomed boat pushed by a giant aircraft propeller mounted on the back, no rudder or motor in the water. That's what lets it run through 6 inches of water and skate over grass beds a regular boat would get stuck on. Most airboat tours in the New Orleans area run 15-30 people, last 60 to 90 minutes, and move fast, we're talking 30-40 mph on open stretches.

You'll wear ear protection. That should tell you something.

The captain usually knows the swamp well and can cover a lot of ground fast, which is genuinely useful if you're short on time or want a big loud thrill on your NOLA trip. Some operators run a genuinely good, informative tour. Others lean hard into the theme-park version: gunning the engine for effect, doing donuts, and baiting alligators with marshmallows or hot dogs to guarantee a photo op.

## What a Kayak Swamp Tour Actually Is

No engine. No propeller. Just you, a paddle, and a guide who's been out here enough times to read the water. Our Manchac Swamp Wildlife Tour runs 2 hours, caps at 12 guests, and moves at whatever pace the group wants, sometimes fast when we're covering ground, mostly slow because that's when you actually see things.

Last spring we had a group that barely paddled 200 yards in 45 minutes because a barred owl kept calling back and forth with our guide. Nobody wanted to leave. That doesn't happen on an airboat doing 35 mph past the same tree.

## Noise: The Difference That Changes Everything Else

This is the whole ballgame, honestly. An airboat prop runs somewhere north of 90 decibels up close, closer to a chainsaw or a lawnmower than a boat engine. That's why you wear ear protection. A kayak runs at whatever volume your paddle stroke and your guide's voice make, usually under 60 decibels.

**Loud changes what you see.** It doesn't eliminate wildlife, birds and gators are used to boat traffic in a lot of these waterways, but it changes what they do. An alligator that hears an airboat coming from 200 yards away either submerges or holds still and waits it out. An alligator that doesn't hear you coming keeps doing what it was doing: sunning, hunting, or just floating there with its eyes above the water line, watching you back.

We don't guarantee alligator sightings. Nobody honest should. But when guests do see one on a kayak tour, they're usually seeing natural behavior, not a startled animal or one that's been trained to associate boats with a marshmallow.

## What the Wildlife Does When You Show Up

Here's the part nobody tells you before you book: **wake matters.** An airboat throws a wake and pushes air pressure ahead of it that spooks nesting birds, especially herons and egrets, who'll flush off a nest they'd otherwise sit tight on. We've watched it happen from our own kayaks, a rookery goes still, then an airboat comes around the bend a quarter mile out, and every bird on that tree is airborne before the boat's anywhere close.

A kayak doesn't do that. We paddle wide of active nests anyway, but even when we're within sight, most birds don't move. That's not a claim, that's just what 400+ tours with zero incidents and guides who log this stuff will tell you.

Gator baiting is the other piece. Some airboat operators toss marshmallows or hot dogs to pull gators toward the boat for a guaranteed photo. It works, that's why they do it. It also trains wild alligators to associate boats and people with food, which is exactly the kind of habituation that gets an animal euthanized down the road when it does the same thing to the wrong boat. We don't do it. Ever. "Common, not guaranteed" is how we talk about wildlife, and we mean it.

## Group Size and How Close You Get to the Guide

Airboat tours typically run 15 to 30 people per boat, sometimes more on the bigger operators. You're getting a PA-system narration, not a conversation. Good luck asking a follow-up question about cypress knee root systems mid-tour.

Our kayak tours cap at 12 guests. Guides know names by the halfway point. If you want to ask why the water's the color of tea (tannins from decomposing cypress leaves, in case you're wondering now too), you just ask, and you get a real answer from someone who's studied this ecosystem, not a script.

## Price, Length, and What's Included

Airboat tours in the greater New Orleans area generally run **$50 to $90 per person** for 60 to 90 minutes, and most include hotel or French Quarter pickup.

Our Manchac Swamp Wildlife Tour is **$65 per person** for 2 hours on the water (plus $25 round trip if you need transportation from the city), and the Extended Manchac Tour runs 4 hours at **$130 per person**. You're on the water longer, moving slower, and covering the ecology and history in more depth than a fast-paced narrated loop can fit in.

Neither price is wrong. They're paying for different things.

## Who Each Tour Is Actually Built For

**Airboats make sense if:** you're tight on time, you want speed and adrenaline more than quiet observation, or a member of your group can't manage a kayak due to mobility.

**Kayak tours make sense if:** you want to actually hear the swamp, you're the kind of traveler who'd rather watch a heron for five minutes than get five photos in five minutes, or you care how the wildlife is treated to get you that photo.

We had a guest last fall tell our guide, "I did the airboat thing in Florida years ago, this is the first time I actually felt like I was *in* the swamp instead of watching it go by." That's the difference in one sentence.

## Which One Should You Book

If all you want is a fast, loud, 45-minute thrill ride and you've got somewhere else to be, book the airboat. We won't pretend that's not a real option for a real kind of traveler.

But if you came to New Orleans wanting to actually understand the ecosystem 30 minutes outside the French Quarter, one that's disappearing at the rate of a football field every hour, get in a kayak. No motor, no baiting, no wake damage, just you, a guide who knows this place, and whatever the swamp decides to show you that day.
