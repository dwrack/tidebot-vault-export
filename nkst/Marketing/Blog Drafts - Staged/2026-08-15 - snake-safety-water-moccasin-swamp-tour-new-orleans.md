```markdown
A guest emailed us the night before her trip last spring with one question: "Is it snakes? I'm fine with the alligators. Please tell me about the snakes." So here's the honest answer we gave her, the one that doesn't get asked nearly as often as the gator question but probably should.

Yes. There are snakes in the swamp. Anyone who tells you otherwise is selling you something. Here's what's actually out there, what to do about it, and why the fear is bigger than the real risk.

## So, are there snakes out there?

The Maurepas Swamp near Manchac, where New Orleans Kayak Swamp Tours runs the Manchac Mystic and Extended tours, is a wetland. Wetlands have snakes. That's just biology, not a scare tactic.

Most of what our guides point out on a given trip is a **banded water snake** or a **diamondback water snake**, both completely harmless to humans. They're common, they're often mistaken for something scarier, and they're one of the more interesting sightings on a slow gator day. Guests usually relax the moment they realize that's what they're looking at.

There's exactly one venomous species you actually need to know about out here, and it's the one everybody's already heard of.

## The one species that actually matters

**The cottonmouth**, also called the water moccasin, is Louisiana's only venomous snake that spends most of its life in the water. It's real, it's out here, and pretending otherwise would be dishonest.

It's also not the swamp full of coiled danger people picture. Cottonmouths are shy around people. Their entire strategy when a kayak approaches is to leave, quietly, the same way a lot of the wildlife out here does when it isn't being chased or baited. Bites happen almost exclusively when someone corners one, steps on one in shallow water, or picks one up thinking it's harmless. None of that describes sitting in a kayak with a guide keeping distance from wildlife on purpose.

## Cottonmouth vs. harmless water snake: the tell

Guests ask us to teach them the difference constantly, so here's the version our guides actually use on the water:

- **Cottonmouths swim high.** The whole body rides on top of the water, almost floating. A harmless water snake swims with its head up and most of its body dipped below the surface.
- **Head shape gives it away.** Cottonmouths have a thick, blocky, almost triangular head noticeably wider than the neck. Water snakes have a slimmer head roughly the same width as the neck behind it.
- **Cottonmouths are slow and deliberate.** They don't spook easily and tend to hold their ground or move off unhurried. Harmless water snakes are jumpy, fast, and usually gone the second they notice you.
- **When threatened, a cottonmouth opens its mouth wide**, showing the pale white lining that gives it the name. It's a warning display, not an attack. If you ever see that, you're already too close, and the fix is simple: back off.
- **Coloring lies more than shape does.** Both species range from dark olive to nearly black, especially as adults, so don't trust "it was dark" as an ID. Trust the swim posture and the head.
- **When in doubt, don't guess.** Ask your guide. This is the actual answer we give guests more than any other, and it's the right one.

## What our guides actually do when we spot one

There's no drama to it. It goes about the same way every time.

1. A guide spots it first, calls it out low and calm, points to exactly where.
2. Everyone stays in their kayak, hands and paddles pulled in, no reaching.
3. We give it room and let it move on its own schedule.
4. The guide identifies it out loud, snake or moccasin, so guests actually learn something instead of just being scared.

No one has ever touched a snake on one of our tours. That rule alone does most of the safety work.

## Why a kayak is safer than people assume

Here's the thing that surprises most guests: sitting in a kayak is one of the lower-risk ways to be around a Louisiana wetland, snakes included. You're elevated off the water, moving slowly and predictably, and never wading through the shallow, grassy edges where most snake encounters actually happen. The people who get bitten in Louisiana are almost always on foot: gardening, clearing brush, walking through tall grass at the water's edge, not paddling through open water with a trained guide fifteen feet away.

We also don't run motors, which matters here too. A quiet kayak gives wildlife, snakes included, time to notice you and leave before you're ever close enough for it to matter. An engine startles animals into unpredictable movement. Silence gives them the option to just quietly go somewhere else, which is what they almost always choose.

## If you're still nervous

That's fair, and you're not the only one. Snakes rank near the top of "things guests text us about before they book," right alongside alligators. What actually helps isn't a pep talk, it's knowing three things: you're not walking through underbrush, you're not reaching for anything in the water, and a trained guide is watching for wildlife before you ever spot it yourself. Take those three off the table and most of the fear goes with them.
```
