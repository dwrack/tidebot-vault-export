## CONTENT (Markdown body)

```markdown
Ask a Honey Island guide about pirates and half of them will start with Jean Lafitte. The other half will tell you about a man who isn't nearly as famous, and whose story is a lot stranger.

His name, as the legend goes, was Pierre Rameau. And unlike Lafitte, he wasn't fighting for New Orleans.

## A Different Pirate Story Than the One You Know

Every swamp in south Louisiana seems to have a Jean Lafitte rumor attached to it somewhere. Honey Island has one too. But the more specific, more local story out of St. Tammany Parish belongs to a different figure entirely, one most visitors have never heard of, and most guidebooks skip.

We found this story the way we find most of what we tell guests: digging through local-history sources, not inventing atmosphere. It came up in a St. Tammany Parish local-history blog, the kind of source that's closer to community record-keeping than a peer-reviewed history text. **That distinction matters, and we're not going to blur it for the sake of a better story.**

## Kirk McCullough, Scotland, and the Screech Owls

The account holds that Pierre Rameau was born Kirk McCullough, in Scotland, and that by the time he'd settled into southeast Louisiana he was running a criminal outfit called the **Chats-Huants**, French for "the Screech Owls." Their base of operations, per the same local-history record: Honey Island Swamp, in the far southeast corner of St. Tammany Parish.

That's not a random pin on a map. It's the same corner of the Pearl River system our guides paddle today. Whether Rameau's actual hideout sits along our route is something we genuinely don't know and won't claim. But the geography lines up in a way that makes the story stick differently than a generic "pirates buried treasure somewhere in Louisiana" tale.

## Colonel Loring: The Double Life

Here's the part that makes this more than a swamp-outlaw footnote. Rameau reportedly kept a second identity, posing in New Orleans high society as **Colonel Loring**, while running robbery and worse out of the swamp under his real name. A man who could dine with the city's elite one week and disappear into Honey Island the next.

Our guides who've heard the full version describe it as less "pirate" and more "con man with a hideout," which is a genuinely different flavor of outlaw story than the treasure-chest version most visitors expect.

## The Battle of New Orleans Twist

This is the detail that separates Rameau from every other regional pirate legend, and the one we'd flag hardest for a healthy dose of skepticism. The story claims that during the lead-up to the Battle of New Orleans in 1815, Rameau communicated with British General Pakenham and handed over detailed maps of American defenses. He was reportedly badly wounded for it.

If true, that makes him a spy and a traitor to the American side, not just a smuggler working the margins. That's a much bigger claim than "outlaw hid in a swamp," and it's exactly the kind of claim that needs more than one local-history source before anyone should treat it as settled.

**We're telling you the story. We're not telling you it's proven.**

## What's Documented and What's Legend

Short version, and we mean it: this is single-source, local-history-tier material, not a peer-reviewed historical record. The account also says Rameau was eventually killed by a former associate named Vasseur, and buried in a brick tomb under a spreading oak. We have no way to confirm where that tomb is, whether it still exists, or whether it's anywhere near our tour route, and we're not going to pretend otherwise for the sake of a better closing line.

That's the same rule we apply to every legend we tell on the water. Julia Brown at Manchac gets the same treatment. So does the Rougarou. History first, embellishment labeled as embellishment, always.

## Not Jean Lafitte, and Why That Matters

Guests occasionally ask if this is just the Jean Lafitte story with a different name attached. It isn't. Lafitte is well-documented, tied to Barataria Bay, and actually fought **for** the Americans at the Battle of New Orleans, working with Andrew Jackson rather than against him. Rameau, in this local telling, did the opposite: he's the outlaw who allegedly sold out the American defense.

Two different men, two different swamps, two different loyalties. Lumping them together flattens a story that's more interesting kept separate.
```
