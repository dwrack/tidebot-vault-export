## CONTENT (Markdown body)

```markdown
Every November we get some version of the same message: "Is the swamp tour even open in January?" Usually from someone planning a trip around a work conference or a family visit, half convinced swamp tours are a summer-only, spring-break kind of thing. They're not. We run Manchac 365 days a year, and the guides who've done a hundred winter mornings out there will tell you it's not the consolation prize people assume.

Here's the real version. No spooky sales pitch, no pretending the gators show up on cue in January, just what's actually true.

## Yes, we run all winter. Here's the actual weather

The **Manchac Mystic 2-Hour Tour** and the **Extended Manchac 4-Hour Tour** both run straight through December, January, and February. Water-side temps sit in the 40s to 60s°F depending on the morning, which is colder than most visitors picture Louisiana. This isn't Florida. A cold front off the Gulf can drop a 68°F afternoon into the low 40s by the next sunrise.

One thing that doesn't run in winter: the **Honey Island Swamp Tour**, which is a summer-only trip on our schedule. If Honey Island was on your list, plan for warmer months. Manchac and the Whitney Plantation combo are your winter options.

## What happens to the alligators

Straight answer: they mostly disappear from view, and we're not going to pretend otherwise.

Alligators are cold-blooded. Once water temperature drops below 65°F, their metabolism slows way down and they spend most of their time submerged, barely moving, conserving energy. April through October is peak activity. December through February is the opposite end of that curve.

That doesn't mean zero. On a warm winter afternoon, above 65°F, you'll sometimes catch one sunning itself on a log, soaking up whatever heat the day offers. It happens. It's just not the way to bet if an alligator sighting is the entire reason you're booking.

We say this about every season, not just winter: wildlife is **common, not guaranteed**. In winter, lean harder on "not guaranteed" and you won't leave disappointed.

## What shows up instead: birds, quiet, and open channels

This is the part people don't expect, and it's the actual argument for a winter trip.

- **Migratory birds move in.** Species that pass through or winter over in the Maurepas basin show up in numbers you won't see in July.
- **Water hyacinth dies back.** That invasive plant chokes off narrower channels every summer. In winter it thins out, and guides can push into stretches of bayou that are half-blocked the rest of the year.
- **The cypress goes bare.** No leaves means the canopy opens up. You can actually see the structure of the trees, the knees breaking the water line, and a lot more sky than a July paddle gives you.
- **The swamp gets quiet in a different way.** Summer has cicadas, frogs, a constant hum. Winter mornings can be still enough that a heron lifting off sounds loud.
- **Fewer bugs, full stop.** Mosquito season is roughly April through October here. Winter is the one stretch where bug spray is optional instead of mandatory.
- **The fog hits different.** Cold mornings over warmer water throw up a low fog that hangs on the cypress until the sun burns through it. It's the same atmosphere that gave Manchac its [internal link: haunted-swamp reputation → /blog/haunted-manchac-swamp-legend/] in the first place, minus any of the summer crowd noise.

A guest once told us it was "the most beautiful kayak tour I've taken in the world." That wasn't a winter trip, but I've had mornings on a January paddle that made me think of that line more than most summer ones did.

## What to wear so you're not miserable

You're sitting mostly still in an open kayak for two to four hours. That's the detail people underestimate. Walking around the Quarter in a coat is one thing. Sitting nearly motionless on open water at 45°F is another.

1. Wool or fleece base layer, not cotton. Cotton holds moisture and cools you down fast.
2. A mid layer you can peel off if the sun comes out and the morning warms up 15 degrees, which happens more than you'd think.
3. A wind-resistant outer shell. Wind chill on open water is worse than the air temp suggests.
4. Closed-toe shoes, always, every season, but especially now.
5. Gloves. Paddles get cold in your hands fast, and stiff fingers make for a rough second hour.
6. A hat that covers your ears.
7. Hand warmers if you run cold. Guides carry extras but bring your own if you know yourself.

For the full packing rundown across every season, [internal link: read our full guide to what to wear on a swamp kayak tour → /blog/what-to-wear-louisiana-swamp-kayak-tour/].

## The tour most guests skip and shouldn't

If you're doing a winter trip, take the **Extended 4-Hour Tour** over the 2-hour version. This is the one our guides push, and it's not the upsell it might sound like.

The 2-hour Manchac Mystic gets you a solid taste. The Extended tour, at $130 a person, gets you deep enough into the canal system that the hyacinth die-back actually matters, into channels that stay half-shut most of the year. Add the extra time and the smaller winter crowds, and it's a genuinely different pace than the same route in July.

We're a small operation on purpose. Twelve guests max on a public tour, CPR-certified guides, and we've run 400-plus tours without a guest injury. That doesn't change in winter. What changes is how much of the swamp you actually get to see, and honestly, winter opens up more of it than it closes off.

## Booking a winter trip

Winter is our lowest-demand season, which cuts both ways. Fewer people on the water with you, but also more schedule flexibility if a cold snap or storm moves your date. We monitor conditions closely and will always tell you straight if a morning isn't safe to run.

Pickup runs from 740 N. Rampart St in Tremé, about an hour and fifteen minutes ahead of your tour time, or drive yourself to the Manchac launch. Call or text 504-571-9975 if you want a read on what a specific week's conditions look like before you book.

The gators are quieter in January. The swamp isn't.
