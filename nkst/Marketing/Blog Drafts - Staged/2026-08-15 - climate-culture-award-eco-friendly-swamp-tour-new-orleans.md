## CONTENT (Markdown body)

```markdown
We didn't set out to win an award. We set out to stop feeling weird about a swamp we could see dying from our own launch dock.

Here's the short version: New Orleans Kayak Swamp Tours was selected by Climate Culture NOLA as a "See the Vision" award recipient this spring, for a proposal to turn Bayou Bienvenue into a working climate education space instead of just a launch point. That's genuinely nice to say out loud. But if you run a tour company and you're reading this wondering whether "eco-friendly" on our homepage is real or just a word we picked because it converts, this post is the long answer.

## The swamp we could see dying from our own dock

Bayou Bienvenue sits at 2102 Paris Rd, on the eastern edge of the city, sharing a marina with our sister brand NOLA Party Barge. Before the 1960s it was a 30,000-acre cypress and tupelo swamp. A shipping canal changed that. Saltwater crept in, the cypress died over decades, and Hurricane Katrina's storm surge finished most of what was left. What's still standing out there is bleached, dead trunks in open water. Guests call it beautiful and eerie in the same sentence, and they're not wrong on either count.

We launch kayaks from that marina every week. Watching a dying ecosystem from your own dock changes how you think about the word "eco-tour." At some point it stops being a category on a booking site and starts being a thing you either actually do something about or quietly stop mentioning.

## What "See the Vision" actually recognizes

Climate Culture NOLA isn't a government program or a grant committee handing out a plaque. It's a community-engagement initiative co-founded by Ray Fontaine (CEO, Bywater Branding Services) and Leroy Brown (Connextive), run in partnership with Tulane University's Small Center for Collaborative Design. Since October 2024 it's held five community salons pulling anywhere from 70 to over 200 people, at spots like Music Box Village and Scale New Orleans. The mission, in their own words, is putting culture bearers, not just engineers and policy people, in the driver's seat of climate work.

"See the Vision" is the competitive piece of that. A handful of organizations get selected each cycle to develop a proposal for a real site, run a public workshop to shape it, and present it at a launch exhibition alongside curated local art and live performance. We were one of the recipients this year. That's the whole factual claim. We're not going to inflate it into something it wasn't.

## The pitch we put in front of the judges

Our proposal was straightforward on paper and a lot harder in practice: turn Bayou Bienvenue Marina from a place people launch kayaks from into a site that teaches wetland restoration through direct experience, not signage.

We already had a few pieces to build on. A 1,000-gallon rainwater catchment system sits on site. Reclaimed solar panels were available to install. There's no municipal water at that marina, which sounds like a limitation and is actually the whole point, it forces any infrastructure we add to solve a real problem instead of decorating one. The proposal leaned into that: public art installations that also do work, like a water wall that shows the catchment system filtering in real time, or a tide gauge readable from the dock that's designed to be looked at, not just glanced past.

Michelle Healey, our Idea Lab facilitator, put it plainer than we could in a press release: "When people are out on the water in a place like Bayou Bienvenue, something beautiful happens. They're not reading about conservation, they're experiencing it firsthand. This project is about connecting people to our native landscape. Plant something. Come back. Watch it grow. That's how people become stewards." Michelle has a bachelor's degree in environmental science and wetland conservation and used to work in Louisiana state government's environmental epidemiology division before she ran the kayak side of our operations. She's the one who wrote most of this proposal, and she's the one who ran the workshop that shaped it further.

## A cypress you plant and come back to check on

One idea kept surfacing through the whole process, in the proposal, in the workshop, in casual conversation with guides: a cypress adoption program. A guest plants a tree at Bayou Bienvenue. Not a symbolic gesture, an actual bald cypress sapling, in an actual restoration site. They come back next year, and the year after, and watch it get taller.

To be straight with you: this isn't a bookable product yet. It's an idea we're actively developing, and it grew out of the same instinct that runs [internal link: our full guide to Bayou Bienvenue, the ghost forest → /bayou-bienvenue-vanishing-swamp/]. A photo of a beautiful swamp gets forgotten in a camera roll. A tree you helped plant, that you have to drive back out to Paris Rd to check on, doesn't. We'll update this post and our tour listings the moment it's something you can actually book.

## Why we build in the ugly places, not just the pretty ones

Here's an idea we've been kicking around internally, and it's honestly become something close to a company philosophy: most tourism businesses invest in the places that already look good. We think there's a stronger case for investing in the places that don't.

[internal link: Manchac → /tours/swamp-kayak-tours/manchac-mystic-kayak-tour/] is what's worth fighting for, second-largest bald cypress swamp left in the country, dense canopy, gators sliding off logs. It's an easy place to sell. Bayou Bienvenue is not. It's dead trees in open water five minutes from the French Quarter, in the Lower 9th Ward, a neighborhood that's had more than its share of people showing up to extract something and leave. We think the harder, more honest work is developing the site nobody would naturally pick for a postcard, instead of only capitalizing on the swamp that already sells itself. That's not charity framing. It's the actual bet behind the Climate Culture proposal.

## The room that showed up on April 2nd

On April 2nd we ran a one-hour community workshop at the Albert and Tina Small Center, 1725 Baronne St. We split the room into groups of four to six, handed out Post-its, and asked two rounds of questions: what should someone feel the moment they arrive at Bayou Bienvenue, and what installation would actually solve a real problem while being something people want to touch.

Past guests showed up. Current and former guides showed up. People who'd never heard of us before that night showed up because a friend forwarded the invite. The themes that kept surfacing across nearly every table: water as a teacher, not a backdrop. Art that does real work instead of just looking nice. A sense that this place should belong to the people who show up, not just to us or to tourists passing through. Those themes are shaping which concepts move toward funding and design next, alongside the other "See the Vision" proposals presented at Climate Culture's launch exhibition, also at the Small Center, in May.

## What "eco-friendly" means when we say it, not just claim it

We've watched other operators around this city put "eco" in a tagline while running motorboats that bait alligators with marshmallows for a guaranteed photo. That's not a dig at anyone specific. It's just the plain difference between us and a lot of what's marketed the same way we are.

Our version of eco-friendly is mechanical before it's philosophical:

- **No motors on our kayak tours.** No wake damage, no engine noise pushing wildlife off their natural patterns.
- **12 guests max** on any public tour, small enough that a guide actually knows who's in the boat.
- **We don't bait wildlife.** "Common, not guaranteed" is how we talk about gator sightings, and guests respect the honesty more than a promise we can't keep.
- **Guides train on real ecology,** cypress regeneration, tannin chemistry, Choctaw place-name history, coastal land loss rates, not a laminated script.
- **We do restoration work, not just talk about it.** The Bayou Bienvenue site connects to work by Sankofa Community Development Corporation on the Caffin Avenue viewing platform, and by Common Ground Relief on cypress replanting nearby, and now to the Climate Culture proposal itself.
- **We say what we don't know.** If a guide can't answer a question, they say so and find out, instead of improvising.

That list is the actual product behind the award, not a marketing gloss on top of one. The award recognized a proposal we hadn't finished writing when we started doing the work it's based on.

If you want to see the site this whole thing is about, [internal link: book the Manchac Mystic Kayak Tour → /tours/swamp-kayak-tours/manchac-mystic-kayak-tour/] first, it's the healthiest version of what we're trying to protect, and ask your guide about Bayou Bienvenue while you're out there. They'll have more to say about it than fits in this post.
```
