## CONTENT (Markdown body)

```markdown
A guest named Julie asked us this on Yelp, in public, where anyone could see it: "Is this something a plus size person could do? Or do the kayaks only fit a certain size?"

We answered her directly, because it's a fair question and she's not the only one asking it quietly to herself before she books anything.

Here's the honest version, with real numbers instead of vague reassurance.

## The question we get more than you'd think

Body size worry keeps more people off the water than bad weather does. We know this because guests tell us, on the phone, in the parking lot before a tour, sometimes in the kayak itself once they've realized they're fine. "I almost didn't book this" is a sentence we hear more often than you'd guess, usually followed by "I wish I'd just called."

So we'd rather answer this in public, in detail, than let someone talk themselves out of a tour over a question we can solve in one phone call.

## The real number: what 400 lbs actually means

Our tandem sit-on-top kayaks hold up to **400 lbs total for two paddlers combined**. Not per person. Combined. That distinction matters more than the number itself.

Two guests who each weigh under 200 lbs are well within range together. A guest who weighs more, paired with a lighter partner, often balances out fine too. The math is about the pair, not a single cutoff line you either clear or don't.

We use sit-on-top kayaks, not the closed-cockpit kind you're picturing from a whitewater video. There's no tight seat to squeeze into, no spray skirt, nowhere your legs get stuck. You sit on top of a wide, stable hull. **Freeboard**, the gap between the waterline and the kayak's edge, is what actually determines comfort, and it's a function of total weight in the boat, not body shape.

## Solo vs. tandem: why the boat you're in changes the math

If you're paddling solo, the number changes. Solo paddlers are **most comfortable under 250 lbs**, mostly for maneuverability and freeboard, not because the kayak can't physically hold more weight. A tandem kayak splits that same load across a longer, wider hull built for two, which is why the combined tandem number is higher than double the solo comfort number.

Most of our guests paddle tandem anyway, often with a partner, a friend, or a guide close by. If a tandem setup fits your situation better, that's the default we'll steer you toward.

## What if you have a physical limitation, not a weight concern

Weight and mobility are two different questions, and we treat them that way.

Our sit-on-top kayaks sit low to the water, which makes them **accessible for many people with limited mobility**. That's a real, meaningful accessibility feature, not a marketing line. It's also not a blanket promise, because "many people" isn't "everyone," and situations vary enough that a phone call beats a guess every time.

If you have a joint issue, a balance concern, a recent surgery, anything that makes you hesitate, call or text us at **504-571-9975** before you book. We'll ask a few questions, tell you straight whether the setup works for your situation, and if it doesn't, we'll say so instead of letting you show up to a tour that isn't right for you.

## Why we'd rather you call than not book at all

We have options for guests of all sizes, and we want the tour to be comfortable for everyone who books one. That's not a slogan, it's the actual operating standard our guides work from.

Here's the thing: a guest who calls ahead with a weight or mobility question takes us two minutes to help. A guest who never calls, assumes she won't fit, and books an airboat tour instead because it "looks easier" from a photo, we've lost that guest to a worse experience over a question we could have answered on the phone.

So call. Text. Ask the awkward question. We've heard it before, and we'd rather talk it through than have you sit this one out.

## What to expect once you're in the kayak

Once you're actually on the water, most of what people worried about stops mattering fast. If this is your first time in a kayak at all, [internal link: your first kayak tour, what to expect → first-time-kayak-swamp-tour-new-orleans] walks through the rest of the day in more detail.

- **No prior kayaking experience is needed.** Guides give a safety briefing and paddling basics before you launch.
- **The pace is slow.** This is a wildlife-watching tour, not a race. Nobody's timing your strokes.
- **There's no whitewater, no open-water chop.** Manchac and the routes we run are calm, tree-lined water, not conditions that punish a shaky first stroke.
- **You wear a USCG Type III life jacket the entire time.** That's non-negotiable for every guest, every size, every skill level.
- **Guides paddle near you, not ahead of you.** If you're working harder than the group, your guide notices and adjusts.
- **Twelve guests max on a public tour.** Small enough that nobody's rushing you to keep pace with a crowd.

Most of what stops someone from booking a swamp tour isn't actually about the kayak. It's the story they've told themselves about not belonging in one. We'd rather you find out on the water that the story was wrong, but if you want to know before you book, that's what the phone number is for. For the practical packing side of things, [internal link: what to wear and bring → what-to-wear-louisiana-swamp-kayak-tour] covers the rest.

[Book the Manchac Mystic Kayak Tour, or call 504-571-9975 with any question about fit before you do.]
```
