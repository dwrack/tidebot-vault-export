## FAQ SCHEMA
_(For Google rich snippets and AI search citation.)_

**Q1:**
```
What's the difference between a swamp and a bayou?
```
**A1:**
```
A swamp is a forested wetland, dominated by water-tolerant trees like bald cypress and tupelo gum, that holds standing water for most of the year. A bayou is a slow-moving water channel, often a former river distributary, that can run through a swamp, a marsh, or open land. Swamp describes the habitat. Bayou describes the water route through it. Manchac Swamp, where New Orleans Kayak Swamp Tours runs its guided paddles, is a forested swamp threaded with bayou-like canals.
```

**Q2:**
```
Is Louisiana a swamp or a marsh?
```
**A2:**
```
Louisiana has both, plus everything in between, because it sits at the bottom of the Mississippi River's delta. Freshwater forested swamp dominates areas like Manchac and the Atchafalaya Basin. Coastal marsh, treeless and grass-dominated, dominates areas closer to the Gulf, like Bayou Sauvage National Wildlife Refuge east of New Orleans. Neither term covers the whole state on its own.
```

**Q3:**
```
Why do people call it a "bayou" if it's technically a swamp?
```
**A3:**
```
Because in everyday Louisiana usage, "bayou" often means the water itself, and "swamp" means the forest around it, and a lot of places have both at once. Bayou St. John and Bayou Bienvenue are named for their water channels, even though Bayou Bienvenue was historically a cypress swamp before saltwater intrusion killed the trees and opened it into something closer to a marsh or shallow lake today.
```

**Q4:**
```
What's the difference between a marsh and a swamp?
```
**A4:**
```
Trees. A swamp is forested wetland with trees like bald cypress standing in the water. A marsh has no trees, just grasses, reeds, and other soft-stemmed plants growing in shallow water or saturated soil. Marshes are more common in Louisiana's coastal zone, where saltwater intrusion makes it hard for trees to survive, while swamps dominate further inland where the water stays fresher.
```

**Q5:**
```
Is your tour a swamp tour or a bayou tour?
```
**A5:**
```
Both, technically, and that's true of most Louisiana paddling routes. Guests launch on bayou-like canal water and paddle into Manchac Swamp itself, the forested cypress habitat the tour is actually named for. We call it a swamp tour because the swamp is the destination, but you're on bayou water most of the way there.
```

## CONTENT (Markdown body)

```markdown
A guest asked me last month why we're called a "swamp tour" when the launch site sits on something that sure looks like a bayou. Fair question. Most visitors use swamp, bayou, and marsh like they're interchangeable. Around here, they're not, and once you know the difference, the drive out to Manchac actually makes more sense.

Short version up front: **swamp** is a place, **bayou** is a road made of water, and **marsh** is what you get when the trees give up. Long version below, from someone who paddles through all three most weeks of the year.

## Swamp: the short definition

A swamp is a forested wetland. Trees standing in standing water, for most of the year, is the whole definition. In south Louisiana that almost always means **bald cypress and tupelo gum**, two of the only trees on the continent built to survive with their roots submerged for months at a stretch. Cypress knees, those woody knobs poking up around the trunks, are part of that adaptation.

Manchac Swamp, where New Orleans Kayak Swamp Tours runs its guided kayak trips, is a real, working example. It's the second-largest bald cypress swamp in the United States, part of the larger Maurepas Swamp Wildlife Management Area. Dense canopy, dark tannin-stained water, the whole picture people have in their head when they hear "Louisiana swamp." [internal link: the Manchac Swamp visitor guide → /blog/manchac-swamp-visitor-guide/] goes deeper on the ecology if you want the full write-up.

## Bayou: the short definition

A bayou is a slow-moving, often meandering water channel. The word comes from the Choctaw *bayuk*, meaning a small stream. Historically, a lot of Louisiana's bayous started as former distributaries of the Mississippi River, channels the river used to run through before it shifted course, left to keep flowing slowly on their own.

Here's the part that trips people up: **a bayou isn't a habitat, it's a route.** A bayou can run through a swamp, through open marsh, through farmland, through a city. Bayou St. John cuts through Mid-City New Orleans. Bayou Bienvenue runs through what used to be cypress swamp in the Lower 9th Ward. Neither one tells you what's growing on the banks. That's a separate question.

## Marsh: the short definition

Marsh is the wetland type with no trees. Grasses, reeds, and other soft, herbaceous plants growing out of shallow water or saturated soil, usually flat and open to the horizon. No canopy, no cypress knees, a completely different feel from a swamp paddle.

Louisiana's coastal zone, closer to the Gulf, runs heavier to marsh than swamp, mostly because **saltwater intrusion** makes it hard for cypress and tupelo to survive that close to open water. Bayou Sauvage National Wildlife Refuge, east of the city, is mostly marsh today, not swamp, even though "bayou" is right there in the name. [internal link: Bayou Sauvage Wildlife Refuge, our marsh guide → /blog/bayou-sauvage-wildlife-refuge-new-orleans/] covers what that habitat actually looks like from a kayak.

## Where the words get tangled (and one word for why)

The confusion isn't really your fault. Louisiana's wetlands change into each other, sometimes within the length of a single paddle, and the naming hasn't always kept up.

The clearest example we run into professionally: **Bayou Bienvenue.** It's named for its bayou, the water channel, but it used to be a genuine cypress swamp before the MRGO shipping channel let saltwater flood in and killed most of the trees in the 1960s and 70s. What's left today is closer to open marsh and shallow lake than the dense forest it used to be. Same name. Completely different habitat than it had fifty years ago. [internal link: why Bayou Bienvenue lost its cypress forest → /blog/bayou-bienvenue-vanishing-swamp/] tells that whole story, and it's the single best real-world example of why these three words aren't interchangeable even when locals use them that way.

One more note, since the vocabulary comes up: a lot of Louisiana folklore, including the [internal link: rougarou legend → /blog/rougarou-louisiana-swamp-legend/], is usually said to roam "the bayous," not "the marsh." That's not an accident either. Bayous run close to towns and farms, the kind of edge-of-settlement water where a scary story about something watching from the tree line actually makes sense. Open marsh doesn't have the tree line to hide behind.

## Where you actually cross from one to the other on our route

This is the part that answers the guest's original question, and it's worth walking through because it's the same on almost every trip we run.

You launch near LaPlace on a channel that behaves like a bayou: slow, relatively open, banks visible on both sides. A few hundred yards in, the canopy closes overhead, cypress knees start poking through the water, and the light changes. That's the moment you've crossed from bayou water into the swamp itself. Nobody rings a bell for it. It just gets quieter and darker and a lot more like the postcard.

By the time you're deep in Manchac, you're fully in forested swamp habitat, tannin-black water, moss hanging off every branch, the kind of place that doesn't look like anywhere else in the country. The bayou got you there. The swamp is why you came.

## Why the distinction isn't just trivia

Knowing the difference actually changes what you should expect from a trip. A swamp tour promises trees, shade, and the dense, closed-in feeling most people picture. A marsh tour, like a paddle through Bayou Sauvage, promises open sky, big bird sightings, and a completely different kind of beautiful. A bayou can be either one, or neither, depending on where you are on it.

If you're picking a Louisiana paddling trip and the listing just says "bayou tour," ask what's actually on the banks. That one word won't tell you.
```
