```
What happens if it rains on the day of my swamp tour?
```
**A1:**

```
We paddle in light rain. It doesn't cancel a tour, and honestly it's one of the more atmospheric ways to see the swamp with the sound of rain on the cypress canopy. We only cancel for lightning within 10 miles, sustained wind above 15 mph, or visibility below a quarter mile from fog or heavy rain. A cloudy forecast is not a reason to cancel your plans.
```

```
Does a swamp tour cancel for thunderstorms?
```

```
Yes, always. If a guide sees lightning or hears thunder, the rule is to get off the water immediately, no exceptions, no waiting to see if it passes. After the storm clears, we wait 30 minutes from the last lightning or thunder before deciding whether to relaunch or call the tour entirely. Louisiana summer afternoons bring near-daily thunderstorms from June through August, which is why morning tours are the safer booking in peak summer.
```

```
Full refund, no questions asked, every time. That's separate from our standard cancellation policy, which requires 48 hours notice from the guest for a refund. ## The short answer: rain doesn't cancel your tour
```

```markdown
Light rain doesn't stop a New Orleans Kayak Swamp Tours trip. Guides run tours through drizzle and steady light rain regularly, and plenty of guests say it's the best version of the swamp: rain on the cypress canopy, mist rising off the water, the whole place gone quiet in a different way than usual. You're already wearing a life jacket. Getting a little wet from above changes almost nothing about the experience.

What actually stops a tour is specific, not vibes-based. A gray sky or a 40% chance in the weather app means basically nothing to us. We watch three things: lightning, wind, and visibility.

## What actually gets guides off the water

**Lightning, always, no exceptions.** If a guide sees a flash or hears thunder, the rule is get off the water immediately. No timing the gap between flash and rumble to guess distance, no checking radar to see if it's "probably moving away." Water is one of the worst places to be in a lightning storm, a strike is lethal at distances that would be survivable standing on land. After the sky clears, guides wait a full 30 minutes from the last lightning or thunder before deciding whether to relaunch or call the tour for good.

**Wind above 15 mph sustained.** Sounds like a small number until you're a first-time paddler getting pushed sideways by a crosswind. Above that threshold, guests fatigue fast, steering gets genuinely hard, and small chop on open stretches starts to spook people who are already nervous about tipping.

**Visibility under a quarter mile.** Heavy rain or fog thick enough that a guide can't see the front and back of the group at the same time is a safety problem, not a comfort one. Morning fog shows up more in fall and spring, and it usually burns off by 9:30 or 10am, so a foggy 8am launch might just get delayed 30-45 minutes rather than cancelled outright.

Notice what's not on that list: gray skies, drizzle, a humid forecast, or the word "storm" appearing somewhere in a seven-day outlook. Those get a tour running exactly as planned.

## Who makes the call, and how you find out

The guide on the water makes the initial read. If it's borderline, it goes up to the contractor on duty for a final decision. Nobody on our team has ever gotten in trouble for being cautious about weather. People have gotten in trouble for pushing through conditions they shouldn't have.

If a tour needs to be held or cancelled, you hear from us, not the other way around. Guides text every booked guest directly, something like "we're rescheduling today's tour due to conditions on the water, safety comes first, here's how to rebook." You won't be standing at a marina wondering if anyone's coming to get you. If the call happens mid-tour, guests already on the water get brought in and the group is notified from there.

## Refunds, reschedules, and the 48-hour rule that doesn't apply here

Our standard cancellation policy requires 48 hours notice from a guest for a refund, cancel later than that and you're generally out of luck. Weather works differently. If we make the call to cancel or hold a tour for weather, that 48-hour window is irrelevant, you get a full refund, no questions asked, regardless of how last-minute the call was. We'll offer to reschedule first since most guests would rather see the swamp than get their money back, but if a new date doesn't work for your trip, call 504-571-9975 and we'll process the refund.

## The seasonal reality: when storms are more likely

Louisiana doesn't have a true wet or dry season the way some destinations do, but there's a real pattern worth knowing before you book.

- **Summer (June through August):** Afternoon thunderstorms are close to a daily occurrence. Morning tours are almost always fine, afternoon tours need real monitoring.
- **Fall (September through November):** The most stable weather of the year. Lower humidity, fewer storm interruptions, and honestly our favorite stretch to guide.
- **Winter (December through February):** Wind is more consistent from frontal passages, but tour volume is lower and storms are less frequent overall.
- **Spring (March through May):** Storm season proper. Frontal boundaries bring wind shifts and thunderstorms, and it's also high-water season on some routes.

None of this means don't book a summer trip. It means a summer afternoon booking carries a slightly higher chance of a weather call than a fall morning one, and now you know why if it happens.

## Should you book a morning tour just to be safe?

If you're visiting during peak summer and have flexibility, yes, mornings are the safer bet, mostly because Louisiana thunderstorms are an afternoon phenomenon, not a morning one. Outside of summer, time of day matters a lot less for weather. If your only available slot is an afternoon in July, book it anyway. Weather cancellations happen, but they're the exception, not the norm, and if it does get called, you're covered either way.
```
