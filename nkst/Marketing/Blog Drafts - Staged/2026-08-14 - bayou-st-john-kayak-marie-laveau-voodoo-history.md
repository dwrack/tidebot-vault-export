## CONTENT (Markdown body)

```markdown
A guest asked me last summer, halfway down the bayou, if we were paddling through "the voodoo one." I get that a lot on this tour specifically, more than on any tour we run outside the city. She was right to ask. This stretch of water, a few blocks from City Park, shows up by name in New Orleans newspaper archives from the 1870s, tied directly to Marie Laveau. Most people paddling it have no idea.

**Bayou St. John** is not the swamp. It's inside New Orleans, running through Mid-City and Faubourg St. John, lined with shotgun houses and joggers on the Wisner path instead of cypress knees and duckweed. But paddle past the bend near Dumaine Street and you're floating over ground that New Orleans newspapers were writing about back in 1874, when a Voodoo ceremony on this exact water reportedly drew a crowd large enough to make the front page.

## Who Marie Laveau actually was

Skip the movie version. Marie Laveau was a real, working New Orleans woman, born free around 1801, who made her living as a hairdresser to some of the city's wealthiest families. She was also, by most contemporary accounts, a devout Catholic who attended Mass at St. Louis Cathedral regularly. Voodoo and Catholicism weren't opposites to her. She practiced both, and that blend is a huge part of why New Orleans Voodoo looks the way it does today.

She became known as the city's most prominent Voodoo practitioner, a title the newspapers of the time called "Voodoo Queen." People came to her for cures, advice, protection, and ceremony, not for a Halloween costume version of magic but for a real religious and spiritual practice with African, Catholic, and Indigenous roots.

Here's the part most tourist-trap versions of her story skip entirely: **there were two Marie Laveaus.** Her daughter, Marie Laveau II, took over much of the public ceremonial role as her mother aged, and historians who've dug into the newspaper record, including biographer Carolyn Morrow Long, think the famous 1874 St. John's Eve ceremony was likely led by the daughter, not the mother, who would have been in her seventies by then. Most retellings collapse the two women into one figure. We'd rather tell you the real, slightly messier version.

## What happened on St. John's Eve, in the city's own newspapers

**St. John's Eve** is June 23rd, the night before the Catholic feast day of St. John the Baptist. By the mid-1800s it had also become the single biggest night of the year for public Voodoo ceremony in New Orleans, and Bayou St. John was where it happened.

Local papers covered it like an event, because it was one. The most famous account, from 1874, describes a large crowd gathered on the bayou banks to witness rites that blended drumming, dance, and Catholic ritual elements. Newspaper attendance estimates from that era tend to run wildly inflated, that's just how 1870s New Orleans press worked, so take any specific headcount with a grain of salt. What's not in dispute is that it happened, it happened here, and it happened more than once. Reporters showed up nearly every June for over a decade.

That's a different kind of history than a campfire legend. It's documented in print, by name, on a date, on a specific bend of water you can paddle to this afternoon.

## Where on the water it happened, and what's there now

The ceremonies are generally placed near a curve in the bayou close to where **Dumaine Street** runs into the water, in what's now the Faubourg St. John neighborhood, not far from where Cabrini High School sits today.

Don't expect a plaque or a roped-off site. The bayou has shifted shape over 150 years, the neighborhood grew up dense around it, and nobody marked the spot for tourism. That's honestly part of what makes it interesting. You're not visiting a museum exhibit. You're paddling the actual working water, past people's back fences and kayak-club docks, over ground that was once packed shoulder to shoulder with a crowd watching a ceremony most of them had only heard rumors about.

## Congo Square is a different story, and it matters that you know that

Quick correction, because a lot of the online voodoo content gets this wrong: **Congo Square isn't on Bayou St. John.** It's about two miles away, on the edge of the French Quarter and Tremé, now inside Louis Armstrong Park.

Congo Square was where enslaved and free Black New Orleanians gathered on Sundays through the 1700s and 1800s for music, dance, and drumming, a foundational site for African-derived culture in this city and a real root of American music history. It's deeply connected to the broader story of Voodoo in New Orleans. It is not, however, the same location as the St. John's Eve ceremonies. Two important sites, two different pieces of the city, often mashed together into one vague "voodoo swamp" story online. We'd rather keep them straight.

## What you actually see paddling Bayou St. John today

This is the part that surprises first-timers. Bayou St. John doesn't look or feel like our Manchac trips at all.

The water is flat and slow-moving, more urban lake than swamp channel. You're paddling past **raised Creole cottages and 1920s bungalows**, under a couple of pedestrian footbridges, in view of the oaks lining City Park. Turtles sun themselves on downed branches. Egrets and herons work the banks, common on most mornings, never guaranteed, the same as anywhere we guide. On a weekend you'll pass runners, second-line practice, somebody's dog swimming laps.

It's quiet in a completely different register than the swamp is quiet. Manchac's silence feels like being somewhere the world forgot. Bayou St. John's stillness feels like the city holding its breath in the middle of a Tuesday. Both are worth experiencing. They are not the same experience, and we tell guests that up front.

Our guides post real trip photos on the tour's Instagram after most outings, turtles stacked on a log, somebody's kid pointing at a heron, no stock-photo filler. If you want to see what a random Tuesday actually looks like before you book, that's where to look.

## How this bayou is nothing like our Manchac tour

We run both, so we get asked to compare them constantly. Here's the honest answer, no hedging.

- **Location:** Manchac is 30 to 40 minutes outside the city near LaPlace. Bayou St. John is inside New Orleans, a straight shot from the French Quarter.
- **The history:** Manchac's ghost-swamp legend centers on Julia Brown and the 1915 hurricane that wiped out three villages. Bayou St. John's history centers on Marie Laveau and documented St. John's Eve ceremonies in the 1870s.
- **The water:** Manchac is cypress swamp, tannin-dark, wild. Bayou St. John is an urban bayou, calmer, framed by houses instead of forest.
- **The wildlife:** Manchac's headline animal is the alligator, common spring through fall, not guaranteed. Bayou St. John's headline animal is closer to a heron or a turtle. Different ecosystem, different expectations.
- **The vibe:** Manchac is fog and quiet cypress. Bayou St. John is history walking distance from your hotel.
- **What stays the same:** no motors, no baiting anything for a photo, a guide who actually knows the real story instead of the recycled internet version, and the same 12-person cap we run everywhere.

If you came here chasing the Julia Brown story, that's a different tour on a different bayou. This one's Marie Laveau's, and it's forty minutes closer to your hotel room.

We've got 384+ five-star reviews across Google and TripAdvisor and a Traveler's Choice award, mostly earned on the Manchac side of the business, but the same guides, the same no-motor rule, and the same "common, not guaranteed" honesty apply here. One guest last June said this was the first time she'd learned who Marie Laveau actually was, not the movie version, on a random Tuesday paddle with her sister. That's the reaction we're going for.
```
