# Weather Hold Protocol

## The Decision Framework

Weather decisions on the water aren't binary. There's a clear "go" zone, a clear "no-go" zone, and a gray area in between. This protocol covers all three.

The goal: never put guests or crew at risk, never cancel a trip that could've safely run, and communicate clearly in either direction.

## Who Decides

**Captain on the vessel** makes the real-time call. If you're on the water and conditions change, you have full authority to return to dock. You don't need permission. You don't need to call Jeffrey first. Get safe, then communicate.

**Manager on duty (Jeffrey/JT)** makes the pre-trip call for cancellations. If conditions are clearly going to be bad, the manager cancels trips in advance so guests aren't showing up to a dock for nothing.

**Joint decision** for the gray area. If it's borderline, the captain and manager discuss it. But here's the rule: if the captain says no-go, it's no-go. The captain has final authority on safety. Always. A manager can cancel a trip a captain wanted to run, but a manager cannot force a captain to run a trip they've called off.

## Hard Thresholds

These aren't guidelines. These are the line.

### Wind > 20 mph

Captain's discretion, but the realistic answer is: this is likely a cancel. At 20+ mph sustained winds on the waterways we operate, vessel stability is compromised, guest comfort drops to zero, and the risk of someone going overboard increases significantly. Gusts matter too, if gusts are hitting 25+ even with sustained winds below 20, treat it as a 20+ situation.

Why 20: our vessels are flat-bottomed party barges with high profiles. They catch wind. A 20 mph crosswind on open water creates conditions that are uncomfortable at best and dangerous at worst.

### Lightning Within 5 Miles

Cancel immediately. If already on the water, return to dock by the most direct safe route.

Water and metal and electricity don't mix. This isn't a judgment call. If you can see lightning or hear thunder (thunder = lightning within ~10 miles), check a lightning tracker. If strikes are within 5 miles and moving toward you, the trip is over.

Wait 30 minutes after the last observed lightning before resuming operations.

### Visibility < 0.5 Mile

Cancel. If on the water, slow to minimum steerage speed and return to dock.

Fog, heavy rain, or any condition that drops visibility below half a mile means you can't see other vessels, obstructions, or navigational markers in time to react. The waterways we operate on have commercial traffic, barges, tugboats, ferries. They can't see you either.

### USCG or DNR Advisory

If the Coast Guard or Louisiana Department of Natural Resources issues a marine advisory, safety zone, or closure for our operating area, operations stop immediately. Full refund to all affected guests. No discretion here, it's a legal compliance issue.

## The Gray Area

Conditions between "clearly fine" and "clearly cancel" are the hardest calls. Some guidance:

**Wind 15-20 mph:** Runnable for experienced captains with a smaller group. Consider the specific route, some stretches are more exposed than others. If you run it, brief the guests that it might be choppy and watch their comfort level.

**Scattered storms:** If storms are in the area but not directly over your route, you can potentially run. But have a bailout plan. Know where you can dock or shelter quickly. Monitor radar continuously.

**Post-rain conditions:** Heavy rain often means debris in the water. Logs, branches, trash. Even if the sky clears, do a visual scan of your route before taking guests out.

**Heat advisories:** Not a safety issue for the vessel, but it's a guest experience issue. Make sure you have shade, water, and a cooler full of ice. Brief guests on hydration. Watch for signs of heat exhaustion.

## Communication

### Pre-Trip Cancellation (Manager Decision)

1. Decision made at least 2 hours before the trip when possible
2. Guests notified via the Guest Comms cancellation template
3. Full refund processed in FareHarbor immediately
4. Offer to reschedule if the guest's dates allow it
5. Post in Slack so all crew know the status

### Mid-Trip Return to Dock (Captain Decision)

1. Make the call, announce it to guests clearly and calmly
2. "We're going to head back to the dock. Conditions are changing and I want to make sure everyone stays safe and comfortable."
3. Don't apologize excessively. Confidence matters. You're protecting them.
4. Return to dock by the safest route
5. Once docked, contact Jeffrey
6. Partial refund determination made by management based on how much of the trip was completed

### Weather Hold (Waiting It Out)

Sometimes you're at the dock and conditions might clear. A weather hold means:

1. Communicate to guests: "We're keeping an eye on conditions. We'll update you in 15 minutes."
2. Give them a specific time for the next update. Don't leave them hanging.
3. Maximum hold time: 30 minutes past scheduled departure. After that, cancel.
4. If it clears and you run a shortened trip, discuss partial refund or rebooking with management.

## Seasonal Weather Patterns

Know what you're dealing with by season:

**Spring (March-May):** Afternoon thunderstorms become common. Mornings are usually clear. Watch the afternoon forecast closely.

**Summer (June-August):** Hot, humid, daily afternoon thunderstorms. Hurricane season starts June 1. Heat is a real factor for guest comfort. Storms roll in fast and can be intense but usually pass quickly.

**Fall (September-November):** Hurricane season peaks in September. Fronts start moving through. Can get some of the best weather of the year between fronts, but the bad days can be genuinely dangerous.

**Winter (December-February):** Cold fronts bring wind. Fog is common in the mornings, especially December and January. Fewer trips, but the ones that run can be affected by wind chill and reduced visibility.

## The Principle

When in doubt, don't go out. No trip is worth someone getting hurt. Guests might be disappointed for an hour. An injury or worse lasts forever. Cancel, refund, reschedule. That's always the right call when you're unsure.
