# Role Guide: Captain

## What This Job Actually Is

You're not driving a boat. You're hosting a party that happens to be on water.

That distinction matters more than anything else in this guide. The engine, the navigation, the safety checks, those are baseline. You need all of that to be automatic, second nature, background process. Because the real job is making 15-25 people feel like this is the best two hours of their trip to New Orleans.

Every group that steps onto one of our boats (Freaky Tiki, Twerkin Tiki, Bayou Boogie, or Party Queen) is there to have a time. Bachelorette parties, birthdays, corporate outings, friend groups who planned this months ago. They picked NOLA Party Barge over a dozen other things they could've done. Your job is to make sure they never regret that choice.

## The Emotional Arc

Every great trip follows the same arc. It's not a script, it's a rhythm. You'll internalize it and then make it your own.

### ARRIVAL

Guests show up nervous, excited, sometimes already a few drinks in. They don't know what to expect. Some are worried about safety. Some are wrangling a group. Your energy in the first 30 seconds sets the tone for the entire trip.

Greet them by name if you can (check the manifest). Be warm, not performative. "Hey, welcome, y'all must be the Johnson group!" beats "Welcome aboard, party people!"

This is also where you handle check-in, waivers, PFDs, and the safety briefing. But the way you do it matters. You're not reading a legal disclaimer at them. You're taking care of them. "Let me make sure everybody's set so we can have the best time out there."

### BUILD

The first 15 minutes on the water are everything. This is where you earn or lose the group.

Music is playing (see music guidelines below). You're pointing things out on the water, telling a quick story about the area, making eye contact, reading the group. Are they high energy? Match it. Are they mellow? Don't force it. A corporate group from Dallas needs a different vibe than a bachelorette party from Atlanta.

You're building toward something. The energy should be climbing, not peaking yet. Think of it like the first act. You're establishing trust and momentum.

This is where NOLA storytelling comes in. You don't need to be a historian, but you should have 4-5 stories you can pull from:

- The history of jazz on the river, how music literally traveled up the Mississippi
- Why New Orleans is below sea level and what that means for the culture (we party because tomorrow isn't guaranteed)
- The French Quarter's actual French and Spanish colonial roots, most of the "French" Quarter is Spanish architecture
- Mardi Gras krewes, how they work, what the Indian tribes mean, why it's not just beads and boobs
- Food stories: where gumbo actually comes from, the roux debate, why you don't put tomatoes in certain dishes depending on who you ask

Pick the ones that fit the group. Don't lecture. Tell stories like you're telling a friend over a beer.

### PEAK

This is the moment the group will remember. The photo they post. The story they tell when they get home.

You design this. It's not random. It could be:

- Pulling up to a scenic spot and killing the engine for a group photo while the sunset hits
- A surprise song that gets the whole boat singing
- A toast moment you orchestrate with the group
- Passing another boat and getting an epic exchange going

The Wow Moment doesn't have to be complicated. It has to feel spontaneous even though you planned it. The best captains have 2-3 peak moments they can deploy depending on the group and conditions.

### LAND

Energy comes down, but gently. You're not slamming the brakes. You're guiding the group to a warm landing. This is where you:

- Thank the group genuinely (not the rehearsed "thanks for choosing us" script)
- Share any last recommendations for the night ("if y'all are headed to Frenchmen Street, check out d.b.a., tell them the boat people sent you")
- Set up the tip moment naturally (more on this below)

### SEND OFF

How you say goodbye matters. People remember the last thing. A high-five, a photo with the captain, a "y'all were one of the best groups we've had this week" (only if true, they'll know if you're faking it).

The goal: they walk away feeling like they just had an experience, not a service.

## Music Guidelines

Music runs from the moment guests arrive at the dock through send-off. It's never an afterthought.

**Boarding / Check-In:** Upbeat but not aggressive. Think feel-good New Orleans funk, brass band, or classic soul. You want energy but not "the party started without you."

**First 15 Minutes (Build):** Energy climbs. Match the group. For younger crowds, mix in current hits with NOLA flavor. For older or mellower groups, stay with classics and deep cuts.

**Peak:** This is where the playlist goes hard. The song that gets everyone on their feet (or at least their phones up). You should have 3-4 peak songs that work every time and a few wildcards for specific group types.

**Landing / Send-Off:** Bring it down. A classic, a feel-good closer. Something that makes people sway, not jump. "Ain't No Sunshine" or "Wonderful World" territory, not "Wobble Baby."

Build your own playlists. Don't rely on Spotify radio. Know what's coming next so you can time transitions with the trip.

## The First 15 Minutes

Worth repeating because this is where new captains struggle most.

You've done the safety briefing. You've cast off. Now what?

Don't go silent. Don't just crank the music and drive. The first 15 minutes is when guests decide if this is going to be amazing or just okay.

Talk to the group. Not at them. Ask where they're from, what they're celebrating, who planned the trip. Point out something on the water. Drop a quick story. Get names if the group is small enough. The music is your background, not your crutch.

If you nail the first 15, the rest of the trip runs itself.

## Tips Strategy

Tips are a significant part of your income. Here's what works:

- Be genuinely great. That's it. That's the strategy. Groups who feel taken care of tip well.
- The landing phase is where it happens naturally. As you're wrapping up, it's fine to say something like "if y'all had a great time, tips are always appreciated but never expected."
- Don't make it weird. Don't mention it more than once. Don't put a jar in their face.
- Groups that feel like they got a personal, one-of-a-kind experience tip 2-3x more than groups that got a standard tour.

## What Not to Call Jeffrey About

Jeffrey runs the whole operation. He doesn't need a call for:

- Routine schedule questions (check the calendar and Slack first)
- Minor supply issues you can solve yourself
- Guests being mildly annoying
- Rain that's already passing
- Anything covered by these SOPs

Do call Jeffrey for:

- Any safety incident or injury
- Equipment failure that affects operations
- Weather that's borderline and you need a second opinion
- Guest situation escalating beyond what you can handle
- Anything involving law enforcement or USCG
- Staffing emergencies (someone no-showed and you can't cover)

## The Crew Culture Standard

We run on mutual respect and accountability. You're a 1099 contractor, not an employee, which means you have freedom. But that freedom comes with expectations:

- Show up early. Not on time. Early.
- Communicate proactively. If something's off, say it before it becomes a problem.
- Take care of the boats like they're yours.
- Help the crew around you. If someone's struggling, step in.
- Never bad-mouth other captains, crew, or the operation in front of guests.
- Protect the guest experience above your own comfort.

## Requirements

Non-negotiable before you take a single solo trip:

- USCG Captain's License (OUPV / 6-pak minimum)
- Current CPR / First Aid certification
- TWIC Card
- Passed drug test
- Cleared background check

## Prohibited Behaviors

These are firing offenses. One time. No warnings.

- Operating a vessel under the influence of alcohol or drugs
- Allowing guests to operate the vessel
- Skipping the safety briefing
- Departing with insufficient PFDs
- Falsifying an incident report or failing to report an incident
- Harassment or discrimination toward guests or crew
- Operating outside approved routes or in restricted areas
- Ignoring a weather hold or USCG advisory

## Violation Protocol

For non-firing offenses (late arrivals, incomplete inspections, poor guest feedback):

1. First occurrence: verbal conversation with Jeffrey, documented in the coaching log
2. Second occurrence: written warning, documented
3. Third occurrence: contract termination

For prohibited behaviors listed above: immediate contract termination, no progression through steps.
