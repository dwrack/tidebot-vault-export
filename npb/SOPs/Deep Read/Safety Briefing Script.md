# Safety Briefing Script

## How to Deliver This

The safety briefing is required before every trip. Not most trips. Every trip.

But here's the thing: if you deliver it like you're reading a legal disclaimer, guests check out immediately and retain nothing. You need to internalize this so it comes out natural, warm, and confident. Like you're a friend who happens to know a lot about boat safety and wants to make sure everyone has a great time.

Don't rush it. Don't mumble through it. Make eye contact. Smile. You're not scaring them, you're taking care of them.

The briefing should take about 90 seconds. If it's taking three minutes, you're over-explaining. If it's taking 30 seconds, you're skipping things.

## The Briefing

Here's the content you need to cover. The exact words are yours. Make it sound like you, not like a manual.

### Welcome

"Hey everybody, welcome aboard [vessel name]! I'm [your name], I'll be your captain today. Before we head out, I want to take a quick minute to make sure everyone knows where everything is and how to stay safe so we can just focus on having a great time."

### PFDs

"These are your life jackets." (Point to where they're stored.) "They're available for everyone on board, and they're required to be worn by anyone under 13 for the whole trip. Adults, you don't have to wear them the whole time, but you should know where yours is and how to put it on. If I ask you to put it on at any point, please do it right away, no questions asked."

(If PFDs were fitted during check-in, acknowledge it: "You already got fitted, so you know where yours is.")

### Staying Seated

"While we're moving, I need everyone to stay seated. I know it's tempting to stand up and dance" (this usually gets a laugh) "but the water can be unpredictable, and I don't want anyone taking a swim they didn't plan on."

### Throwable PFD

"See this ring right here?" (Point to the Type IV throwable.) "If anyone goes in the water, this is going to them immediately. Don't jump in after someone. Point at them, shout 'man overboard,' and let me handle it."

### Emergency Exits

"Getting off the boat is simple. We board and exit from right here." (Point to the boarding area.) "If we need to exit in a hurry, same spot. Don't overthink it."

### Fire Extinguisher

"Fire extinguisher is right here." (Point to it.) "Hopefully we never need it, but now you know."

### Emergency Signal

"Last thing. If you hear me lay on the horn like this" (give a brief demo or describe it, a continuous blast) "that means I need everyone to stop what they're doing, move toward the exit area, and listen for my instructions. That's the 'something's happening, pay attention' signal."

### Close It Out

"That's it! Any questions before we head out?" (Pause. Actually wait.) "All right, let's go have the best time on the water."

## What Makes a Good Briefing

**Confidence.** You've done this a hundred times. It shows. Guests feel safe when the captain clearly knows what they're doing.

**Warmth.** You're not a flight attendant on autopilot. Make eye contact with specific people. Especially the person who looks most nervous, they need to hear this the most.

**Brevity.** Cover everything, but don't belabor it. They're here to party, not attend a safety seminar.

**Interaction.** Asking "any questions?" at the end is mandatory. Sometimes someone will ask something real ("what if I can't swim?") and you need to handle that before you leave the dock.

## Common Guest Questions

**"What if I can't swim?"**
"That's totally fine. That's what the life jackets are for. I'd recommend wearing yours for the whole trip, and I'll make sure you're in a good spot. We're on calm water and I've got you."

**"Can we bring our own drinks?"**
This depends on your specific trip policy. Know the answer before the trip starts. Don't wing it.

**"Is it going to be rough?"**
"It's pretty calm out there today. You might feel a little movement, but nothing wild. If at any point you're not feeling great, let me know and I'll take care of you."

**"Can my kid stand up?"**
"I need everyone seated while we're moving, including kids. When we stop, they can move around a bit, but the life jacket stays on the whole time."

## Non-Negotiables

- Every trip gets a full briefing. No "they were here yesterday, they know the drill."
- Point to actual equipment during the briefing. Don't just describe locations.
- Wait for questions. A genuine pause, not a rhetorical one.
- If a guest is late and missed the briefing, give them a condensed version before the vessel leaves the dock. Don't skip it.
- If you're doing back-to-back trips with the same guests (rare but it happens), still do the briefing. "I know you heard this already, but it's required and it's quick."
