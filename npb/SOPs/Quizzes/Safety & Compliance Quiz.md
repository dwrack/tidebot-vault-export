# Safety & Compliance Quiz

**Passing score: 80% (8 out of 10 correct minimum)**

These scenarios test whether you can make the right call when safety and compliance are on the line.

---

## Question 1

During your morning inspection, you count PFDs and come up one short of the vessel's max capacity listed on the COI. Your first trip is in 45 minutes. What do you do?

A) Run the trip but limit the group to one fewer passenger
B) Do not run the trip until the PFD count matches or exceeds the COI max capacity
C) Run the trip since it's unlikely you'll hit max capacity
D) Borrow a PFD from another vessel if they have extras

**Best answer: B**

The vessel does not leave the dock with insufficient PFDs. Period. Option D might seem reasonable, but only if the other vessel still meets its own requirements after lending one out. The safest answer is B: don't go until the count is right. "Unlikely to hit max" (C) is how violations happen.

## Question 2

You're 30 minutes into a trip when you notice dark clouds building to the west and check your phone. Lightning has been detected 7 miles away, moving in your direction at roughly 10 mph. What's your call?

A) Continue the trip but keep monitoring
B) Begin heading back to dock immediately
C) Wait until lightning is within 5 miles, then head back
D) Anchor in a sheltered area and wait it out

**Best answer: B**

Lightning at 7 miles moving toward you at 10 mph means it could be within 5 miles in about 12 minutes, and you need time to get back to dock. Don't wait until it hits the 5-mile threshold to start moving. Head back now. The protocol says cancel at 5 miles, but smart seamanship means you account for transit time. Waiting until the exact threshold (C) could put you in a dangerous position while still on open water.

## Question 3

A USCG inspector boards your vessel and asks to see the Certificate of Inspection. Where should it be?

A) In the captain's bag with other documents
B) Posted visibly in the passenger area of the vessel
C) Filed in the office on shore
D) On your phone as a digital copy

**Best answer: B**

The COI must be posted visibly where passengers can see it. Not stored, not filed, not digital. Posted. On the vessel. This is a specific Subchapter T requirement. If an inspector asks for it and you have to dig through a bag, you've already failed that check.

## Question 4

A guest falls overboard during a trip. They're conscious, wearing a PFD, and the water is calm. What's the correct sequence?

A) Jump in after them, pull them back to the vessel
B) Stop the vessel, throw the Type IV PFD, maneuver to recover, assist them back on board
C) Call 911 immediately, then attempt recovery
D) Have another guest help pull them in while you maintain course

**Best answer: B**

Stop the vessel. Throw the Type IV throwable PFD (even if they're already wearing one, it gives them something to grab and helps you track their position). Maneuver the vessel to recover them. Assist them back on board. Nobody jumps in after them, that's how you get two people in the water. Call 911 only if there's an injury or you can't recover them.

## Question 5

During morning inspection, you find that your fire extinguisher gauge is in the yellow zone (borderline). The next trip departs in 20 minutes. What do you do?

A) Run the trip, yellow means it still has some charge
B) The vessel does not go out. Replace the extinguisher before departing.
C) Run the trip but make a note to replace it after
D) Shake the extinguisher to redistribute the agent and re-check

**Best answer: B**

Yellow means the extinguisher may not perform when needed. In a fire emergency on a vessel full of passengers, "may not perform" is unacceptable. Replace it before the vessel goes out. Twenty minutes is enough time to grab a replacement if you have spares on site (which you should).

## Question 6

You complete a trip where a guest tripped on the dock and scraped their knee. You cleaned it with the first aid kit and they said they were fine. No big deal. What do you do?

A) Nothing further, it was minor
B) File an internal incident report the same day, even though it was minor
C) Ask the guest to sign something saying they're okay
D) Mention it to Jeffrey next time you see him

**Best answer: B**

Every incident gets a same-day internal report, no matter how minor. That scraped knee could turn into a phone call from a lawyer three months later, and your documentation is your protection. You don't need the guest to sign anything (C), and a verbal mention to Jeffrey (D) isn't a documented record.

## Question 7

Your VHF radio seems to be working but you're getting a lot of static on Channel 16. You can barely make out transmissions. The first trip is in 10 minutes. What do you do?

A) Run the trip, you have your cell phone as backup
B) Do not depart until the radio is functioning clearly on Ch 16
C) Switch to a different channel that's clearer
D) Turn the radio off and on to reset it, if it's still bad, go anyway

**Best answer: B**

VHF radio on Channel 16 is required while underway. Your cell phone is not an acceptable substitute because the Coast Guard communicates on VHF, other vessels hail on VHF, and cell service on the water is unreliable. If Ch 16 isn't clear, troubleshoot it, check connections, try a reset, but don't depart until it works. Switching to another channel (C) defeats the purpose.

## Question 8

It's peak season and you're short-staffed. Your manager asks if you can run a trip with one fewer crew member than the COI specifies. The trip is fully booked. What do you do?

A) Run it since the manager approved it
B) Refuse to run the trip below minimum manning requirements, regardless of who approved it
C) Run it but stay extra vigilant
D) Run it if the guests seem like an easy group

**Best answer: B**

The COI's minimum manning requirement is a federal regulation. It doesn't matter who approves running below it. Not the manager, not the owner. If the COI says two crew, you run with two crew. Running below minimum manning makes you personally liable and puts passengers at risk.

## Question 9

A guest injures their wrist during a trip and you can tell it might be broken. They want to tough it out and finish the trip. What do you do?

A) Respect their wishes and continue the trip
B) Return to dock, ensure they get medical attention, and begin the incident reporting process
C) Continue the trip but keep the injured hand elevated and iced
D) Call 911 while continuing the trip

**Best answer: B**

A potential broken bone requires medical treatment beyond first aid. Return to dock, make sure they get proper medical care, and start your incident report. This is also a USCG-reportable event since it requires medical treatment beyond first aid. The guest's desire to "tough it out" doesn't override your obligation to ensure their safety and comply with reporting requirements.

## Question 10

Your flares are on board but you notice one of the three expired last month. The other two are current. What do you do?

A) Two out of three is fine for today, replace the expired one tomorrow
B) Replace the expired flare before departing. All required flares must be within date.
C) Keep the expired one as a backup and order new ones
D) It's just one month past, they still work

**Best answer: B**

Expired flares are treated as missing during a USCG inspection. It doesn't matter that the other two are fine. If your vessel requires three, you need three in-date flares. Replace it before you go out. And expired flares should be disposed of properly, not kept as "backups" since that creates confusion during an emergency.

## Question 11

You're underway and your engine starts making an unusual grinding noise. It's still running and you're 10 minutes from dock. What do you do?

A) Push through and get back to dock, then deal with it
B) Reduce speed immediately, monitor the engine, and head directly back to dock. Do not run the next trip until the engine is inspected.
C) Kill the engine and call for a tow
D) Increase speed to get back faster before it fails

**Best answer: B**

Reduce speed to minimize stress on the engine and head back. Pushing through at full speed (A) or increasing speed (D) risks catastrophic engine failure on the water with passengers aboard. Killing the engine (C) might be premature if it's still running, but if the noise worsens, that becomes the right call. The critical point: the vessel does not go back out until the engine is inspected by a mechanic.

## Question 12

During a USCG inspection, the inspector asks a crew member to demonstrate how to deploy a fire extinguisher. The crew member doesn't know the PASS method. What happens?

A) The inspector shows them and moves on
B) This is a deficiency that gets noted in the inspection report and may result in further action
C) Nothing, crew training isn't part of the inspection
D) The captain gets a verbal warning

**Best answer: B**

USCG inspectors can and do quiz crew on safety procedures. If a crew member can't demonstrate basic safety equipment operation, that's a deficiency. It reflects on the vessel's compliance and training standards. This is why every crew member needs to know the basics: PASS (Pull, Aim, Squeeze, Sweep), PFD deployment, man-overboard procedures, and emergency signals. For the record, the PASS method: Pull the pin, Aim at the base of the fire, Squeeze the handle, Sweep side to side.
