# Culture & Values Quiz

**Passing score: 80% (8 out of 10 correct minimum)**

Test your understanding of what makes NOLA Party Barge tick. These aren't trick questions, they're judgment calls you'll actually face.

---

## Question 1

A bachelorette group boards and they're already pretty loud and high-energy. What's your move during the first 15 minutes?

A) Turn the music up to max and let them run the show
B) Match their energy, engage with the group, learn names, and channel that energy into the trip arc
C) Keep things calm and controlled so the trip doesn't get out of hand
D) Do your standard opening regardless of the group's vibe

**Best answer: B**

The first 15 minutes is about reading the room and matching energy. A high-energy group doesn't need you to calm them down or to compete with them. Channel it. Learn who the bride is, find out what they're celebrating, and use that energy to build toward your peak moment. The emotional arc still applies, you're just starting at a higher baseline.

## Question 2

You're mid-trip and a guest asks you to tell them something interesting about New Orleans. You:

A) Recite a list of historical facts about the French Quarter
B) Tell a story you've internalized about NOLA history or food culture, keeping it conversational
C) Say "I'm more of a boat guy, not a tour guide" and change the subject
D) Pull out your phone to look something up

**Best answer: B**

Storytelling is part of the job. You should have 4-5 stories ready to go about NOLA history, music, food, or culture. The key word is "story," not "lecture." Tell it like you're talking to a friend, not presenting a report. If you don't have stories ready, you're not prepared.

## Question 3

What is the emotional arc of a trip?

A) Safety Briefing, Tour, Music, Dock
B) Check-In, Cruise, Drop-Off
C) ARRIVAL, BUILD, PEAK, LAND, SEND OFF
D) Welcome, Party, Wind Down, Goodbye

**Best answer: C**

ARRIVAL, BUILD, PEAK, LAND, SEND OFF. Every trip follows this arc. You're building energy intentionally, hitting a designed peak moment, and bringing people down gently before they leave. This isn't a suggestion, it's the framework for every trip.

## Question 4

A guest leaves a mediocre review saying "the boat was fine but it felt like the captain was just going through the motions." What part of the framework most likely failed?

A) The safety briefing wasn't thorough enough
B) The captain didn't design a Wow Moment or engage during the BUILD phase
C) The music wasn't loud enough
D) The route was boring

**Best answer: B**

"Going through the motions" means the captain ran the trip mechanically instead of hosting it. The BUILD phase and the Wow Moment are where you turn a boat ride into an experience. If guests feel like they just rode around on the water, you didn't build to anything or make them feel seen.

## Question 5

It's 4:45 PM and your last trip ends at 5:00. A crew member on another vessel is struggling with their between-trips reset because they had a messy group. What do you do?

A) Focus on your own closeout, that's their boat
B) Help them out once your guests are off the vessel
C) Tell them to just skip some of the reset steps since they're behind
D) Text Jeffrey about it

**Best answer: B**

Crew culture means you help the people around you. If your boat is clear and another captain needs a hand, step in. This isn't about being a hero, it's about being part of a team. Skipping reset steps (C) creates problems for the next group. Ignoring it (A) isn't how we operate.

## Question 6

You notice that a newer captain has been skipping the part of the safety briefing where they point to the throwable PFD. What's the right move?

A) Report them to Jeffrey immediately
B) Mention it to them directly and casually after the trip: "Hey, I noticed you skipped the throwable in your briefing. That one matters."
C) It's not a big deal, guests probably won't need it
D) Wait until it becomes a pattern

**Best answer: B**

Direct, respectful feedback between crew is part of the culture. You don't need to escalate everything to Jeffrey. A casual, private conversation after the trip handles 90% of these situations. The throwable PFD absolutely needs to be pointed out in every briefing, so it does need to be addressed, but peer-to-peer is the first step.

## Question 7

A corporate group from out of town is quiet and reserved during the BUILD phase. They're not rude, just not vibing. What do you do?

A) Crank the music and try harder to get them hyped
B) Adjust your approach: ask questions, find a connection point, lower the energy and go more conversational
C) Just drive the route and let them enjoy the scenery quietly
D) Assume they're not having fun and cut the trip short

**Best answer: B**

Not every group is a party group. Corporate outings, older groups, and some friend groups are more low-key. Your job is to read them and adjust. Going harder (A) will push them further away. Going silent (C) means they get a boat ride, not an experience. Find what interests them, maybe it's the architecture, the food scene, the history, and lean into that.

## Question 8

When is the right time to mention tips?

A) At the beginning of the trip so they know it's expected
B) Multiple times throughout the trip so they don't forget
C) Once during the LAND phase, naturally and briefly
D) Never, it's uncomfortable

**Best answer: C**

One mention, during the landing phase, delivered naturally. Something like "if y'all had a great time, tips are always appreciated but never expected." That's it. Mentioning it early (A) or repeatedly (B) makes it transactional. Never mentioning it (D) leaves money on the table. The real strategy is being so good they want to tip you without being asked.

## Question 9

A guest is getting aggressively drunk and starting to make other guests uncomfortable. They haven't done anything dangerous yet. What do you do?

A) Ignore it, they paid for the trip
B) Cut them off from any bar service, redirect their attention, and monitor closely. If it escalates, head back to dock.
C) Immediately turn the boat around
D) Ask the other guests if they want you to do something about it

**Best answer: B**

You manage the situation before it becomes a crisis. Cut them off (work with crew on this), redirect their energy, and keep a close eye. You're protecting the experience for the whole group. If it escalates to unsafe behavior, then you head back. But jumping straight to canceling the trip (C) punishes the entire group for one person. Asking other guests to weigh in (D) puts them in an awkward position.

## Question 10

What does "hosting vs operating" mean at NPB?

A) The captain handles the fun stuff while the crew handles the boat
B) Your primary job is creating an experience for guests, not just navigating a vessel from point A to point B
C) You should prioritize entertainment over safety
D) The captain should act like a cruise ship host and delegate all technical work

**Best answer: B**

"You're not driving a boat. You're hosting a party that happens to be on water." The engine, navigation, and safety are baseline, they should be automatic. The real job is making guests feel like this was the highlight of their New Orleans trip. Safety is never compromised (C is wrong), and you're still the captain (D is wrong), but the mindset shift from operator to host is what separates a great captain from a mediocre one.

---

## Question 11

You overhear a captain complaining to guests about the schedule being too packed today. How should this be handled?

A) It's venting, everyone does it, no big deal
B) Talk to them after the trip: crew frustrations stay internal, never in front of guests
C) Report it to Jeffrey immediately
D) Jump into the conversation and smooth it over with the guests

**Best answer: B**

Never bad-mouth the operation in front of guests. It makes the whole experience feel unprofessional and makes guests wonder what else is going wrong behind the scenes. The right move is a private conversation after the trip. If it keeps happening after that conversation, then it becomes a Jeffrey issue. But crew-to-crew accountability comes first.

## Question 12

What's the single most important thing that happens during the ARRIVAL phase?

A) Getting the safety briefing done quickly
B) Setting the emotional tone for the entire trip in the first 30 seconds of guest contact
C) Making sure all the paperwork is complete
D) Getting underway as fast as possible

**Best answer: B**

The first impression sets everything. Paperwork and briefings have to happen, but the way you greet guests, your energy, using their name, your body language, that's what determines whether they trust you and relax into the experience. You can handle waivers and PFDs efficiently while still being warm and engaging. The two aren't mutually exclusive.
