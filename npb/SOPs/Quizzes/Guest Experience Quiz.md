# Guest Experience Quiz

**Passing score: 80% (8 out of 10 correct minimum)**

Every question here is a real scenario you'll face. Pick the response that best protects the experience.

---

## Question 1

A guest shows up visibly intoxicated before boarding. They can barely walk straight. Their group is trying to help them onto the boat. What do you do?

A) Let them board, they paid for the trip
B) Quietly pull the group leader aside and explain that the guest can't board in their current condition for safety reasons. Offer alternatives (they can sit at the dock, rejoin if they sober up, etc.)
C) Refuse the entire group boarding
D) Let them on but make them wear a PFD the whole time

**Best answer: B**

You have an obligation to keep everyone safe, including the intoxicated guest. A person who can't walk straight on land is a serious risk on a moving vessel. But you handle it with discretion, not by making a scene. Talk to the group leader privately, explain the safety concern, and offer a reasonable solution. Don't punish the whole group (C) for one person's condition. A PFD (D) doesn't prevent them from falling, injuring themselves, or causing problems.

## Question 2

A large bachelorette group (18 people) wants to change the route mid-trip because someone in the group says they saw a better spot on Instagram. What do you do?

A) Change the route to keep them happy
B) Acknowledge the request warmly, explain that your route is designed for the best experience and safety, and offer to show them something great coming up
C) Flatly refuse and say "the route is the route"
D) Call Jeffrey to ask permission

**Best answer: B**

You're the captain and the host. Guests don't set the route, but you don't shut them down coldly either. Acknowledge what they want, redirect with confidence and warmth, and give them something to look forward to. "I hear you, that spot is cool, but trust me, what's coming up next is even better" works every time if you say it with conviction. You don't need to call Jeffrey for route decisions within your normal operating area.

## Question 3

You're in the BUILD phase and the music is playing, but the group seems disconnected. People are on their phones, conversations are fragmented. What's your move?

A) Turn the music up louder
B) Change tactics: ask the group a question, start a story, do something to create a shared moment that pulls them together
C) Let them be, some groups are just quiet
D) Skip ahead to the peak moment to get their attention

**Best answer: B**

Phones out and disengagement during BUILD means you haven't hooked them yet. More volume (A) won't fix that. Accepting it (C) means you've given up on the experience. Jumping to peak (D) wastes it because you haven't earned it. Create a shared moment: "So who planned this trip? Give them a round of applause." Or drop into a good NOLA story. The group needs a reason to look up from their phones.

## Question 4

A guest pulls you aside and says the music is too loud and they have a headache. Meanwhile, the rest of the group is clearly loving the vibe. How do you handle it?

A) Turn it down for the one guest
B) Acknowledge the guest's discomfort, offer them a quieter spot on the vessel if one exists, and adjust the volume slightly so it's still fun but a touch more manageable
C) Tell them it's a party boat and this is what they signed up for
D) Give them earplugs from the first aid kit

**Best answer: B**

One guest's comfort matters, but so does the entire group's experience. A small volume adjustment and finding them a better spot shows you care without killing the vibe. Telling them "this is what you signed up for" (C) is how you get a 1-star review. Turning it way down (A) punishes the majority. Earplugs (D) is creative but signals you don't actually care about their experience.

## Question 5

It's the first 15 minutes of the trip. You've done the safety briefing, you're underway, and now... what?

A) Put on a playlist and focus on driving
B) Engage the group: ask where they're from, what they're celebrating, point out something on the water, drop a quick NOLA story
C) Give them space to settle in and enjoy the scenery
D) Start with your best story to hook them immediately

**Best answer: B**

The first 15 minutes is where you earn or lose the group. Silence (A, C) tells them this is just a boat ride. Going too hard too fast (D) can feel forced. The sweet spot is natural engagement: learn something about them, share something about where you are, build a connection. The music is your background, not your substitute for human interaction.

## Question 6

You're approaching what you've planned as the peak moment of the trip. A guest is feeling seasick and needs attention. What do you do?

A) Handle the seasick guest quickly and then push through to the peak moment
B) Take care of the seasick guest first: fresh air, water, eyes on the horizon. Adjust the peak moment to happen a few minutes later or in a different way.
C) Ask another guest to help the seasick person so you can focus on the peak
D) Skip the peak moment entirely and start heading back

**Best answer: B**

Guest safety and comfort always come first. But a seasick guest doesn't mean the trip is over for everyone. Take care of them (get them to the rail, fresh air, water, stable position), then adjust your plan. The Wow Moment can shift by a few minutes or change form. Delegating to another guest (C) is inappropriate, they're not crew. Skipping it entirely (D) is only necessary if the situation is serious.

## Question 7

A birthday group has a clearly designated "birthday person" wearing a sash and tiara. How do you work this into the trip?

A) Wish them happy birthday during the safety briefing and move on
B) Make them part of the peak moment: a birthday toast, get the group to sing, give them the spotlight at the best moment of the trip
C) Treat them the same as everyone else to avoid making others feel left out
D) Let the group handle their own birthday celebration

**Best answer: B**

When a group has a clear guest of honor, your Wow Moment practically designs itself. Build toward a birthday toast at the scenic high point, get the whole group singing, give that person a story to tell. This is hosting. Ignoring it (C, D) or giving it a one-second nod (A) misses the whole point. The birthday person's experience is going to be what the entire group talks about later.

## Question 8

A guest with a wheelchair asks about accessibility for your vessel. What's the right approach?

A) Tell them the boats aren't wheelchair accessible and suggest they find another activity
B) Have an honest conversation about the specific access points and limitations of your vessel, work with them and their group to figure out if and how they can safely board and enjoy the trip
C) Let them board and figure it out as you go
D) Offer a full refund and redirect them to a land-based tour

**Best answer: B**

Every situation is different. Some guests with wheelchairs can transfer to a seat with assistance from their group. Some can't. The right answer is an honest, respectful conversation about what the vessel setup looks like, where the challenges are, and working together to find a solution. Don't assume they can't do it (A, D), and don't wing it (C). If it genuinely won't work safely, be straightforward about that, but start from a position of "let's figure this out."

## Question 9

You notice the perfect photo opportunity coming up: great light, the skyline behind you, the group is in a good mood. What do you do?

A) Nothing, that's what their phones are for
B) Call it out: "Hey everybody, this is the shot right here, get your phones out!" Slow the boat or stop briefly so they can get the photo.
C) Take a group photo yourself and send it to them later
D) Wait until the end of the trip for a group photo at the dock

**Best answer: B**

You see the shot they don't. Part of hosting is directing their attention to the moments they'll want to capture. Slowing down or stopping briefly shows you care about their experience beyond just driving the route. Taking it yourself (C) is a nice bonus but doesn't replace the moment of everyone experiencing it together. Waiting for the dock (D) misses the on-water magic entirely.

## Question 10

A guest quietly tells you they can't swim and seems nervous about being on the water. How do you handle this?

A) Tell them statistics about how safe the boats are
B) Reassure them warmly, make sure they have a well-fitted PFD, suggest they wear it the whole trip, and check in on them periodically throughout
C) Suggest they might be more comfortable staying on shore
D) Point out the throwable PFD and say "you'll be fine"

**Best answer: B**

A nervous guest needs genuine reassurance, not data (A) and not dismissal (C, D). Fit their PFD properly, tell them it's totally fine to wear it the whole trip, and then check in with them a few times. "How you doing? Pretty cool out here, right?" goes a long way. Often these guests end up having the best time once they relax, and they become your biggest fans in the review.

## Question 11

A group is upset because they expected the boat to have a specific feature (like a slide or a DJ booth) that was never advertised. They're blaming you. What do you do?

A) Apologize and say there's nothing you can do
B) Acknowledge their disappointment without being defensive, redirect to what you CAN offer, and make the trip so good they forget what they thought they wanted
C) Blame the booking platform for misleading them
D) Offer them a discount on the spot

**Best answer: B**

You can't fix misinformation, but you can control the experience from this point forward. "I hear you, that's frustrating. Here's what we DO have, and I'm going to make sure y'all have an incredible time." Then deliver. Don't throw FareHarbor or the company under the bus (C). Don't offer discounts (D), that's a management call. And a flat "nothing I can do" (A) makes you the bad guy.

## Question 12

It's the SEND OFF. Guests are leaving the boat. What's the difference between a good send-off and a great one?

A) A good send-off says "thanks for coming." A great one makes them feel like they just had an experience they'll talk about for years.
B) A great send-off includes a professional thank-you and a business card
C) A great send-off asks them to leave a review right then and there
D) There's no real difference, the trip is over

**Best answer: A**

The last impression sticks. A great send-off means genuine warmth: "Y'all were seriously one of the best groups we've had, the bride is amazing, have the best night out there." High-fives, a photo with the captain, a restaurant recommendation for their night. They should walk away feeling like they had a personal, one-of-a-kind experience. Not like they just exited a transaction.
